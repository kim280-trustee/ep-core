# M5 Chemistry — Acids, Bases, Redox and Electrochemistry Teacher Starter Pack

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017)

## Lesson 1 — Acid–base theories
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ACID-14 — Identify acids and bases using Arrhenius, Brønsted–Lowry and Lewis definitions.
**Starter / learning task:** Sort familiar substances using the three acid–base theories and explain the limits of each definition. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What does the Arrhenius definition say an acid produces in water?
   **Answer:** It increases the concentration of hydrogen ions, represented in water as hydronium ions.
2. What does a Brønsted–Lowry base do?
   **Answer:** It accepts a proton.
3. What does a Lewis acid accept?
   **Answer:** An electron pair.

## Lesson 2 — Conjugate acid–base pairs
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ACID-15 — Identify conjugate acid–base pairs by tracking proton transfer.
**Starter / learning task:** Track proton transfer in paired reactions and label conjugate species. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What is a conjugate base?
   **Answer:** The species remaining after an acid donates a proton.
2. What is the conjugate acid of NH₃?
   **Answer:** NH₄⁺.
3. What changes between a conjugate acid–base pair?
   **Answer:** They differ by one proton.

## Lesson 3 — Acid and base strength
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ACID-16 — Calculate and compare ionisation or dissociation and interpret acid and base strength.
**Starter / learning task:** Compare supplied Ka, Kb and pKa data; explain what the values imply. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What does Ka measure?
   **Answer:** The equilibrium tendency of an acid to ionise in water.
2. Which is stronger: an acid with Ka = 10⁻² or 10⁻⁵?
   **Answer:** The acid with Ka = 10⁻².
3. What is the relationship between pKa and Ka?
   **Answer:** pKa = −log₁₀ Ka; lower pKa indicates a stronger acid.

## Lesson 4 — pH and ion concentrations
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ACID-17 — Calculate pH, hydronium concentration and hydroxide concentration for acidic and basic solutions.
**Starter / learning task:** Practise pH and concentration calculations using scientific notation and logarithms. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What is pH defined as?
   **Answer:** pH = −log₁₀[H₃O⁺].
2. What is the pH when [H₃O⁺] = 1.0 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹?
   **Answer:** pH = 3.
3. At 25°C, what is pH + pOH for a dilute aqueous solution?
   **Answer:** 14, using Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴.

## Lesson 5 — Neutralisation
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ACID-18 — Write balanced neutralisation equations and determine the acid–base character of the resulting solution.
**Starter / learning task:** Use balanced equations and ion models to distinguish complete neutralisation from the pH of the final salt solution. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What are the usual products of an acid–base neutralisation?
   **Answer:** A salt and water, for typical aqueous acid–base neutralisation.
2. Write the net ionic equation for strong acid–strong base neutralisation.
   **Answer:** H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ → 2H₂O.
3. Does every salt solution have pH 7?
   **Answer:** No; ions can hydrolyse and make the solution acidic or basic.

## Lesson 6 — Salt hydrolysis
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ACID-19 — Write salt-hydrolysis equations and predict whether a salt solution is acidic, basic or neutral.
**Starter / learning task:** Predict salt-solution pH by identifying the acid–base behaviour of each ion. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What is salt hydrolysis?
   **Answer:** Reaction of salt ions with water that can produce hydronium or hydroxide ions.
2. What is the usual character of aqueous NaCl?
   **Answer:** Approximately neutral at 25°C because its ions come from a strong acid and strong base.
3. Why can ammonium chloride solution be acidic?
   **Answer:** NH₄⁺ acts as a weak acid and produces hydronium ions in water.

## Lesson 7 — Acid–base titration
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ACID-20 — Explain titration principles and select a suitable indicator for an acid–base titration.
**Starter / learning task:** Use titration curves and indicator transition ranges to justify indicator selection. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What is the equivalence point in a titration?
   **Answer:** The point where reactants have combined in the stoichiometric amounts specified by the balanced equation.
2. What is the role of an indicator?
   **Answer:** It changes colour over a pH range to signal the titration endpoint.
3. Why should indicator transition range match the steep pH change?
   **Answer:** So the observed endpoint is close to the equivalence point.

## Lesson 8 — Titration calculations
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ACID-21 — Calculate amount or concentration of an acid or base from titration data and reaction stoichiometry.
**Starter / learning task:** Work through a sample titration dataset, showing units, mole ratios and significant figures. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. A 25.0 mL monoprotic acid sample requires 20.0 mL of 0.10 M NaOH. What is the acid concentration?
   **Answer:** Moles NaOH = 0.00200 mol; 1:1 ratio gives 0.00200 mol acid; concentration = 0.00200/0.0250 = 0.0800 M.
2. What information is needed to calculate concentration from titration?
   **Answer:** Balanced reaction stoichiometry, titrant concentration and volume, and analyte volume.
3. Why must the burette reading be recorded carefully?
   **Answer:** A small volume error affects the calculated amount and concentration.

## Lesson 9 — Buffer solutions
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ACID-22 — Describe buffer components, resistance to pH change and common applications.
**Starter / learning task:** Model how a weak acid/conjugate base pair responds to small acid or base additions. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What are the components of an acidic buffer?
   **Answer:** A weak acid and its conjugate base, usually supplied as a salt.
2. How does a buffer respond to a small addition of acid?
   **Answer:** Its conjugate base consumes much of the added hydronium.
3. Give one biological buffer example.
   **Answer:** The carbonic acid–bicarbonate system in blood.

## Lesson 10 — Applications of acid–base chemistry
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ACID-23 — Research and present examples of using acid–base knowledge to solve problems.
**Starter / learning task:** Investigate an application such as antacids, water treatment or soil pH using reliable sources. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. Give an everyday use of acid–base chemistry.
   **Answer:** Antacids neutralise some excess stomach acid.
2. Why is soil pH monitored in agriculture?
   **Answer:** Soil pH affects nutrient availability and plant growth.
3. What should a research presentation include?
   **Answer:** A clear example, the relevant acid–base principle, evidence and a practical benefit or limitation.

## Lesson 11 — Oxidation numbers and redox identification
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-REDOX-24 — Assign oxidation numbers and identify oxidation–reduction reactions from changes in oxidation state.
**Starter / learning task:** Assign oxidation numbers in simple compounds and identify changes in a reaction. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What is the oxidation number of oxygen in most compounds?
   **Answer:** Usually −2, with known exceptions such as peroxides.
2. What happens to oxidation number during oxidation?
   **Answer:** It increases.
3. What defines a redox reaction?
   **Answer:** Oxidation and reduction occur together through electron transfer.

## Lesson 12 — Oxidising and reducing agents
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-REDOX-25 — Analyse oxidation-number changes, identify oxidising and reducing agents, and write oxidation and reduction half-equations.
**Starter / learning task:** Annotate electron transfer and write oxidation and reduction half-equations. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What is an oxidising agent?
   **Answer:** A species that accepts electrons and is itself reduced.
2. What is a reducing agent?
   **Answer:** A species that donates electrons and is itself oxidised.
3. Write the oxidation half-equation for Zn becoming Zn²⁺.
   **Answer:** Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻.

## Lesson 13 — Comparing redox strength
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-REDOX-26 — Interpret controlled evidence comparing oxidising or reducing ability and represent the redox reactions.
**Starter / learning task:** Compare supplied displacement or standard-potential data to rank relative redox behaviour. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What does a stronger reducing agent do more readily?
   **Answer:** It donates electrons and is oxidised more readily.
2. How can displacement observations compare reducing strength?
   **Answer:** A metal that displaces another metal ion is generally the stronger reducing agent in that pair.
3. What safety approach should redox demonstrations use?
   **Answer:** Teacher-approved microscale materials or supplied data with a documented risk assessment.

## Lesson 14 — Balancing redox equations
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-REDOX-27 — Balance redox equations using oxidation-number and half-reaction methods.
**Starter / learning task:** Balance a redox equation by both methods and check atoms and charge. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What must be conserved when balancing a redox equation?
   **Answer:** Atoms and total charge.
2. What is the half-reaction method?
   **Answer:** Balance oxidation and reduction separately, then equalise and combine electron counts.
3. Why are electrons cancelled when half-equations are combined?
   **Answer:** Electrons transferred in oxidation equal electrons accepted in reduction.

## Lesson 15 — Electrochemical cell components
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ELECTRO-28 — Identify cell components; write anode and cathode half-equations, overall reaction and cell notation.
**Starter / learning task:** Draw a labelled galvanic cell and identify electron flow, ion movement and electrode reactions. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. Where does oxidation occur in an electrochemical cell?
   **Answer:** At the anode.
2. Where does reduction occur?
   **Answer:** At the cathode.
3. What does cell notation represent?
   **Answer:** The arrangement of phases and interfaces in an electrochemical cell.

## Lesson 16 — Standard cell potentials
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ELECTRO-29 — Calculate standard cell potential and identify cell type, electrodes and reactions.
**Starter / learning task:** Calculate cell potentials from a provided table of standard reduction potentials. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. How is standard cell potential calculated?
   **Answer:** E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode, using standard reduction potentials.
2. What does a positive E°cell indicate under standard conditions?
   **Answer:** The cell reaction as written is thermodynamically favourable.
3. In a galvanic cell, what energy conversion occurs?
   **Answer:** Chemical energy is converted into electrical energy.

## Lesson 17 — Primary and secondary cells
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ELECTRO-30 — Explain how primary and secondary cells operate and write their reaction equations.
**Starter / learning task:** Compare primary and secondary cell diagrams and explain rechargeability. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What is a primary cell?
   **Answer:** A cell designed for a reaction that is not readily recharged under normal use.
2. What is a secondary cell?
   **Answer:** A rechargeable cell whose reaction can be driven in reverse by an external power source.
3. What is one example of a secondary cell?
   **Answer:** A rechargeable lithium-ion battery or lead–acid battery.

## Lesson 18 — Electrolysis, electroplating and corrosion
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ELECTRO-31 — Explain electroplating, electrolysis, metal purification and corrosion prevention using electrochemical principles.
**Starter / learning task:** Use diagrams and teacher-approved data to explain electrolysis, plating and corrosion prevention. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**

   **Answer:** Reduction.
2. What is electroplating used for?
   **Answer:** Depositing a metal coating on an object using an electrolytic process.
3. Name one way to reduce corrosion.
   **Answer:** Protective coatings, sacrificial anodes or suitable alloy selection.

## Lesson 19 — Electrochemical technology
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ELECTRO-32 — Research and present examples of electrochemical-cell technology used in everyday life.
**Starter / learning task:** Prepare a short evidence-based comparison of battery or fuel-cell technology, including benefits and limitations. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**

   **Answer:** To distinguish measured performance from marketing claims and identify limitations.

# M5 Chemistry — Gas Laws and Properties Teacher Starter Pack

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017)  
**Subject / grade:** Chemistry, M5  
**Official strand:** Additional Chemistry strand 1  
**Coverage:** 6 official M5 learning outcomes; 6 lesson starters; 18 practice questions with answer keys  
**Use:** Teacher planning resource. Use simulations, worked datasets and teacher-controlled demonstrations. Do not use sealed gas-generating reactions or pressurised equipment without approved risk controls.

## Lesson 1 — Boyle, Charles and Gay-Lussac laws
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-GAS-01 — Explain and calculate relationships among gas volume, pressure and absolute temperature when the other relevant variable is held constant.
**Starter / learning task:** What happens to a gas when its container is compressed or heated? Compare particle diagrams and simple datasets. Emphasise kelvin for gas-law calculations.

**Practice**
1. State Boyle's law.
   **Answer:** For a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature, pressure is inversely proportional to volume.
2. What temperature scale is used in gas-law calculations?
   **Answer:** Kelvin.
3. At constant pressure, what happens to gas volume when its absolute temperature doubles?
   **Answer:** The volume doubles, provided the amount of gas remains constant.

## Lesson 2 — The combined gas law
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-GAS-02 — Calculate a gas variable when pressure, volume and temperature change between two states for a fixed amount of gas.
**Starter / learning task:** How can the initial and final states of a gas be compared? Use a two-state table and the combined gas law; require students to show unit conversions.

**Practice**
1. Write the combined gas law.
   **Answer:** P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ for a fixed amount of gas.
2. A gas has a volume of 2.0 L at 100 kPa. At constant temperature, what is its volume at 200 kPa?
   **Answer:** 1.0 L, because pressure doubles while volume halves.
3. Why must temperature be converted to kelvin?
   **Answer:** Gas-law proportionalities use absolute temperature, whose zero corresponds to zero thermal energy in the idealised model.

## Lesson 3 — Avogadro and ideal gas laws
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-GAS-03 — Calculate gas volume, pressure, temperature, amount or mass using Avogadro relationships and the ideal gas equation.
**Starter / learning task:** How are gas amount and volume related at fixed pressure and temperature? Move from particle-count models to PV = nRT calculations.

**Practice**
1. State Avogadro's law.
   **Answer:** At constant temperature and pressure, equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of particles; volume is proportional to amount in moles.
2. Write the ideal gas equation.
   **Answer:** PV = nRT.
3. What must be checked before substituting values into PV = nRT?
   **Answer:** Use a consistent gas constant and units for pressure, volume and temperature.

## Lesson 4 — Dalton's law of partial pressures
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-GAS-04 — Use Dalton's partial-pressure relationships to calculate component partial pressures, mole fractions or amounts in a gas mixture.
**Starter / learning task:** How does each gas contribute to the total pressure of a mixture? Use a labelled particle model and sample partial-pressure data.

**Practice**
1. State Dalton's law.
   **Answer:** The total pressure of a mixture of non-reacting gases equals the sum of their partial pressures.
2. A mixture contains gases with partial pressures of 30 kPa and 70 kPa. What is the total pressure?
   **Answer:** 100 kPa.
3. What is a mole fraction?
   **Answer:** The moles of one component divided by the total moles in the mixture.

## Lesson 5 — Kinetic molecular theory and Graham's law
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-GAS-05 — Use kinetic molecular theory to explain gas diffusion and calculate or compare diffusion rates using molar masses.
**Starter / learning task:** Why do lighter gas particles generally diffuse faster than heavier ones at the same temperature? Use particle-motion diagrams and a comparison dataset.

**Practice**
1. What causes gas pressure in the kinetic molecular model?
   **Answer:** Collisions of gas particles with the walls of their container.
2. State Graham's law for two gases.
   **Answer:** At the same conditions, the ratio of diffusion rates is the square root of the inverse ratio of their molar masses.
3. Which diffuses faster at the same temperature, hydrogen or oxygen?
   **Answer:** Hydrogen, because its molar mass is lower.

## Lesson 6 — Gas laws in daily life and industry
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-GAS-06 — Research and explain applications of gas properties and gas laws in everyday phenomena and industry.
**Starter / learning task:** Where do gas laws help explain weather, breathing, tyres, balloons or industrial gas storage? Ask learners to connect an example to a law and identify safety limits.

**Practice**
1. Why does a flexible balloon shrink when cooled?
   **Answer:** At approximately constant external pressure, the gas volume decreases as its absolute temperature decreases.
2. Why must a gas cylinder be protected from heat?
   **Answer:** Heating a fixed-volume cylinder raises gas pressure and can create a serious hazard.
3. Why are real gases not perfectly described by PV = nRT under all conditions?
   **Answer:** Real particles have finite volume and intermolecular attractions, especially at high pressure and low temperature.

   **Answer:** A collision with sufficient energy and suitable orientation that leads to reaction.

   **Answer:** Provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy and is regenerated.

**Objective:** M5-CHEM-KINETICS-05 — Compare reaction rates when concentration, surface area, temperature or catalyst conditions change, identifying controlled variables.

**Practice**
1. What generally happens to rate when temperature increases?

2. Why can powdered solids react faster than large lumps?

## Lesson 6 — Reaction rates in everyday life and industry
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-KINETICS-06 — Explain examples of factors affecting reaction rate in everyday life and industry using particle-level reasoning.

2. How does a catalyst help industrial production?
   **Answer:** It can increase rate without being consumed overall.
3. Why does increasing concentration often increase rate?
   **Answer:** There are more particles per unit volume and typically more frequent collisions.

## Lesson 7 — Reversible reactions and dynamic equilibrium
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-EQUIL-07 — Use evidence or models to distinguish reversible reactions from reactions that proceed effectively in one direction and explain dynamic equilibrium.
**Starter / learning task:** How can a reversible reaction be distinguished from an irreversible one? Use a worked example, teacher-provided dataset or particle model. Ask learners to state assumptions, show calculations and explain evidence.

**Practice**
1. What is a reversible reaction?
   **Answer:** A reaction that can proceed in both forward and reverse directions.
2. What is dynamic equilibrium?
   **Answer:** A closed-system condition where forward and reverse reaction rates are equal.
3. Do concentrations have to be equal at equilibrium?
   **Answer:** No; they remain constant, but need not be equal.

## Lesson 8 — Concentration and rate changes toward equilibrium
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-EQUIL-08 — Explain how reactant and product concentrations and forward and reverse rates change from reaction start until equilibrium is established.

**Practice**
1. What happens to forward rate after reactants are first mixed?
   **Answer:** It is usually initially high and changes as reactant concentrations change.
2. What happens to reverse rate as products accumulate?
   **Answer:** It generally increases.
3. What defines equilibrium at the particle level?
   **Answer:** Forward and reverse rates are equal.

## Lesson 9 — Equilibrium constants
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-EQUIL-09 — Write an equilibrium-constant expression for a reaction and calculate its value from equilibrium concentrations.
**Starter / learning task:** How does the equilibrium constant describe a system at equilibrium? Use a worked example, teacher-provided dataset or particle model. Ask learners to state assumptions, show calculations and explain evidence.

**Practice**
1. What does Kc represent?
   **Answer:** The equilibrium constant expressed using equilibrium concentrations.
2. Are pure solids included in a Kc expression?
   **Answer:** No; their activity is treated as constant.
3. What does a large Kc generally indicate?
   **Answer:** Products are favoured at equilibrium for the reaction as written.

## Lesson 10 — Equilibrium concentrations
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-EQUIL-10 — Use initial conditions, stoichiometry and an equilibrium constant to determine unknown equilibrium concentrations.

**Practice**

2. What is an ICE table used for?

   **Answer:** Physical validity, units and consistency with the reaction stoichiometry.

## Lesson 11 — Equilibrium in multistep reactions
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-EQUIL-11 — Combine equilibrium relationships for linked reactions and calculate an overall equilibrium constant or unknown equilibrium concentration.

## Lesson 12 — Le Chatelier principle

2. Does a catalyst change the equilibrium constant?

3. What does changing temperature do to K?
   **Answer:** It changes K; the direction depends on whether the forward reaction is endothermic or exothermic.

## Lesson 13 — Equilibrium in biological and industrial systems

   **Answer:** It helps predict conditions that favour desired products and optimise yield.
