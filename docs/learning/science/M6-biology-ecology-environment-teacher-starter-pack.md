# M6 Biology — Ecology and Environmental Sustainability Teacher Starter Pack

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017)  
**Subject / grade:** Biology, M6  
**Official strand:** Additional Biology strand 5 — Ecology  
**Coverage:** 13 official M6 learning outcomes; 13 lesson starters; 39 practice questions with answer keys  
**Use:** Teacher planning resource. Review local examples, data and suitability before classroom use.

## Teaching sequence

Each lesson is designed for a short introductory segment followed by guided analysis, discussion or a data task. Use local Thai and global examples where reliable evidence is available.

### Lesson 1 — Energy transfer in ecosystems
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-01 — Analyse energy transfer through trophic levels and distinguish energy flow from matter cycling.  
**Starter:** Display a simple rice-field food web (rice plant, grasshopper, frog, snake, decomposers). Ask learners to trace two possible energy pathways.  
**Learning task:** Learners label producers, consumers and decomposers, then explain why energy flows one way while nutrients are recycled. Use an ecological pyramid and supplied energy values; describe the approximate 10% transfer idea as a rough model, not a fixed law.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners explain why food chains usually contain a limited number of trophic levels.

**Practice**
1. What is the role of a producer in a food chain?  
   **Answer:** It converts light or chemical energy into stored chemical energy in organic matter, forming the base of many food chains.
2. In a grass → grasshopper → frog chain, which organism is the secondary consumer?  
   **Answer:** The frog.
3. Why is energy described as flowing through an ecosystem rather than cycling?  
   **Answer:** Energy enters (often as sunlight), passes between organisms and is eventually dissipated as heat; matter is recycled.

### Lesson 2 — Biomagnification
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-02 — Explain increasing concentrations of persistent pollutants at higher trophic levels and ways to reduce the problem.  
**Starter:** Present a fictional dataset showing concentrations of a persistent pollutant in plankton, small fish, large fish and fish-eating birds. Ask learners to identify the trend.  
**Learning task:** Distinguish bioaccumulation within an organism from biomagnification across trophic levels. Discuss persistence, fat solubility and food-web transfer. Evaluate prevention at source, regulation, monitoring and safe disposal.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners explain why top predators may receive higher pollutant concentrations.

**Practice**
1. What is biomagnification?  
   **Answer:** An increase in the concentration of a persistent substance in organisms at successively higher trophic levels.
2. How is bioaccumulation different?  
   **Answer:** Bioaccumulation is the build-up of a substance within an individual organism over time.
3. Give one effective way to reduce biomagnification risk.  
   **Answer:** Prevent persistent pollutants from entering the environment through safer substitutes, strict controls and proper waste treatment/disposal.

### Lesson 3 — Nitrogen, sulfur and phosphorus cycles
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-03 — Explain transfers and transformations in three biogeochemical cycles.  
**Starter:** Give groups mixed process cards: nitrogen fixation, nitrification, assimilation, decomposition, denitrification, weathering and atmospheric deposition. Ask them to sort cards into cycles.  
**Learning task:** Learners draw labelled cycle diagrams. Include nitrogen fixation and denitrification; sulfur movement through rocks, organisms and atmosphere; phosphorus weathering, uptake and decomposition. Emphasise that phosphorus has no major gaseous phase in its usual short-term cycle.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners describe how decomposers return nutrients to the environment.

**Practice**
1. What is nitrogen fixation?  
   **Answer:** Conversion of atmospheric nitrogen gas into biologically usable nitrogen compounds, commonly by certain microorganisms.
2. Which process returns nitrogen gas to the atmosphere from nitrates?  
   **Answer:** Denitrification by microorganisms under suitable conditions.
3. What is a major natural source of phosphate entering ecosystems?  
   **Answer:** Weathering of phosphate-containing rocks, followed by movement into soil and water.

### Lesson 4 — Biomes and geographic patterns
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-04 — Compare biomes using climate and characteristic organisms.  
**Starter:** Show climate graphs for a tropical rainforest, desert, grassland and tundra. Learners predict vegetation before seeing photographs.  
**Learning task:** Compare temperature, precipitation, seasonality, vegetation structure and adaptations. Connect latitude, altitude and ocean influence to broad biome patterns.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners justify why two places at similar latitudes may still have different ecosystems.

**Practice**
1. Which two climate variables are especially useful when classifying terrestrial biomes?  
   **Answer:** Temperature and precipitation.
2. Why are desert plants often adapted to reduce water loss?  
   **Answer:** Water availability is limited and evaporation can be high.
3. Name one factor besides latitude that can influence biome conditions.  
   **Answer:** Altitude, distance from the sea, ocean currents or prevailing winds.

### Lesson 5 — Primary and secondary succession
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-05 — Compare the starting conditions and community changes in primary and secondary succession.  
**Starter:** Compare images of bare rock after a new lava flow and a regenerating forest after a storm. Ask what biological material or soil remains.  
**Learning task:** Sequence pioneer species, soil development and later communities. Explain that secondary succession begins where soil or biological legacies remain, so it is often faster than primary succession. Avoid presenting succession as a guaranteed fixed path to one final community.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners classify scenarios and justify their choices.

**Practice**
1. What is primary succession?  
   **Answer:** Community development in a place initially lacking developed soil, such as newly exposed bare rock.
2. Why is secondary succession often faster?  
   **Answer:** Soil, nutrients, seeds, roots or surviving organisms may remain after the disturbance.
3. Give one event that may initiate secondary succession.  
   **Answer:** A storm, fire or abandoned farmland where soil remains.

### Lesson 6 — Characteristics of populations
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-06 — Describe population size, density, distribution and demographic characteristics.  
**Starter:** Give learners a map with counts of the same species in several sample plots. Ask whether the species is evenly or unevenly distributed.  
**Learning task:** Introduce population size, density, dispersion, age structure, birth rate, death rate and migration. Use supplied sample data to calculate density and interpret a simple age-structure diagram.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners explain why population size alone does not describe population condition.

**Practice**
1. What is population density?  
   **Answer:** The number of individuals of a population per unit area or volume.
2. Name two processes that can increase population size.  
   **Answer:** Births and immigration.
3. What does an age-structure diagram help ecologists understand?  
   **Answer:** The proportions of individuals in different age groups and possible future population trends.

### Lesson 7 — Exponential and logistic growth
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-07 — Interpret exponential and logistic population-growth models.  
**Starter:** Show J-shaped and S-shaped curves without labels. Ask learners to describe how growth rate changes.  
**Learning task:** Explain exponential growth under abundant resources and logistic growth as limits slow growth near carrying capacity. Use simple data tables and discuss why real populations fluctuate rather than following perfect curves.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners identify carrying capacity on a logistic curve.

**Practice**
1. What shape is commonly associated with exponential population growth?  
   **Answer:** A J-shaped curve.
2. What is carrying capacity?  
   **Answer:** The population size an environment can sustain over time under prevailing conditions.
3. Why does logistic growth slow as a population approaches carrying capacity?  
   **Answer:** Resources and other environmental limits reduce the population's per-capita growth rate.

### Lesson 8 — Factors controlling population growth
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-08 — Explain how environmental factors and demographic processes affect population size.  
**Starter:** Sort examples such as disease, competition, drought, flood, food supply and predation into possible limiting factors.  
**Learning task:** Distinguish density-dependent factors (often stronger as density rises, such as competition and transmission of some diseases) from density-independent events (such as severe storms). Link births, deaths and migration to population change.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners explain why the same factor may affect populations differently in different settings.

**Practice**
1. Give one density-dependent limiting factor.  
   **Answer:** Competition for food or space, or transmission of an infectious disease.
2. Give one density-independent factor.  
   **Answer:** A severe storm, flood, drought or sudden temperature event.
3. How does immigration affect population size?  
   **Answer:** It adds individuals to the population.

### Lesson 9 — Water scarcity and water pollution
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-09 — Analyse causes, impacts and management of water scarcity and pollution.  
**Starter:** Present a fictional watershed map showing homes, farms, industry and a river. Ask learners to identify possible water pressures without collecting or touching water.  
**Learning task:** Analyse physical and economic scarcity, overuse, drought, contamination and eutrophication. Evaluate demand management, watershed protection, wastewater treatment, efficient irrigation and monitoring using credible datasets.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners connect a proposed solution to the cause it addresses.

**Practice**
1. What is eutrophication?  
   **Answer:** Nutrient enrichment of water that can stimulate excessive algal growth and reduce dissolved oxygen as biomass decomposes.
2. Name one cause of water scarcity.  
   **Answer:** Drought, over-abstraction, pollution that makes water unusable, or inadequate infrastructure.
3. Give one way to reduce agricultural water demand.  
   **Answer:** Improve irrigation efficiency, schedule irrigation appropriately or use suitable water-saving practices.

### Lesson 10 — Air pollution
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-10 — Analyse air-pollution sources, impacts and mitigation.  
**Starter:** Display a reliable air-quality chart (for example, PM2.5 levels) and ask learners to describe trends without making medical diagnoses.  
**Learning task:** Compare particulate matter and gaseous pollutants, identify sources such as traffic, industry, open burning and some natural events, and examine impacts on health, visibility, ecosystems and climate. Evaluate emission controls, cleaner transport, energy efficiency and prevention of open burning.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners distinguish reducing emissions at source from responding after pollution occurs.

**Practice**
1. What does PM2.5 describe?  
   **Answer:** Fine airborne particles with aerodynamic diameters of 2.5 micrometres or less.
2. Give one human source of air pollution.  
   **Answer:** Vehicle exhaust, industrial emissions, power generation or open burning.
3. Why is source control usually important?  
   **Answer:** It prevents pollutants from entering the air rather than only treating exposure after emissions occur.

### Lesson 11 — Soil resource problems
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-11 — Analyse soil erosion, degradation and contamination and suitable responses.  
**Starter:** Compare two diagrams of sloping land, one with vegetation cover and one bare. Ask where runoff and soil loss may be greater.  
**Learning task:** Examine erosion, compaction, salinisation, nutrient depletion and contamination. Evaluate ground cover, contour farming, terracing where suitable, organic matter management and pollution prevention.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners match each soil problem to a context-appropriate conservation response.

**Practice**
1. What is soil erosion?  
   **Answer:** The removal and transport of topsoil by water, wind or other processes.
2. How can vegetation reduce erosion?  
   **Answer:** Roots help hold soil and plant cover reduces the impact of rain and wind.
3. Name one cause of soil degradation.  
   **Answer:** Overgrazing, deforestation, poor land management, salinisation or contamination.

### Lesson 12 — Deforestation and forest conservation
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-12 — Analyse deforestation impacts and evaluate forest protection and restoration.  
**Starter:** Use before-and-after satellite images of a forest landscape. Ask learners to identify changes in habitat and land cover.  
**Learning task:** Analyse habitat fragmentation, carbon storage, water cycles, soil erosion, livelihoods and biodiversity. Compare protected areas, sustainable forestry, restoration, community stewardship and demand-side measures. Discuss that tree planting alone does not replace the ecological value of intact natural forests.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners evaluate a conservation proposal against ecological and community outcomes.

**Practice**
1. What is habitat fragmentation?  
   **Answer:** The division of a habitat into smaller, separated patches.
2. Give one ecological consequence of deforestation.  
   **Answer:** Habitat loss, reduced biodiversity, increased erosion or changes to local water and carbon cycles.
3. Why should forest restoration use suitable native species and local ecological knowledge?  
   **Answer:** To improve ecological fit, support native biodiversity and reduce risks from unsuitable planting.

### Lesson 13 — Wildlife decline and conservation
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-13 — Analyse causes and consequences of wildlife decline and evaluate conservation approaches.  
**Starter:** Present a case study with a species population trend and habitat map. Ask learners to identify what evidence is still needed before choosing an intervention.  
**Learning task:** Analyse habitat loss, overexploitation, invasive species, pollution, disease and climate change. Evaluate habitat protection, wildlife corridors, enforcement, community-based conservation, sustainable use and evidence-led recovery programmes.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners propose a measurable conservation outcome and identify how it could be monitored.

**Practice**
1. Name two major causes of wildlife population decline.  
   **Answer:** Habitat loss, overexploitation, invasive species, pollution, disease or climate change (any two).
2. How can a wildlife corridor help a population?  
   **Answer:** It can connect habitat patches, allowing movement, dispersal and gene flow.
3. Why should conservation programmes monitor population data over time?  
   **Answer:** To evaluate whether interventions are effective and adapt management when results are poor.

## Assessment and teacher review

- Use the 39 questions as formative checks; adapt them to the class language level and local examples.
- Ask learners to support environmental claims with data and distinguish correlation from causation.
- Check that diagrams correctly represent processes and that examples are geographically and scientifically appropriate.
- Use published datasets, maps, simulations, photographs and teacher-selected case studies. Do not ask learners to handle hazardous pollutants, collect or taste polluted water, disturb habitats or capture wildlife.
- For air-quality lessons, follow current school guidance and avoid outdoor activities during unhealthy conditions.
