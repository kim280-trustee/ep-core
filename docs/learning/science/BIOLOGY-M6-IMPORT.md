# M6 Biology Teacher Starter Pack — Nervous Coordination, Movement, Reproduction and Behaviour

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017), Additional Biology, strand 4  
**Grade:** M6  
**Coverage:** 19 official learning outcomes; 19 lesson starters; 57 practice questions with answer keys  
**Official alignment:** OBEC/IPST revised curriculum, pp. 113–114  
**Use:** Teacher planning resource. Review scientific accuracy, language level and safeguarding requirements before adapting or publishing.

## Teaching and safeguarding guidance

Use diagrams, models, simulations, prepared datasets and age-appropriate scientific resources. Sensory activities must be optional, accessible and safe; do not use sharp objects, extreme temperatures, loud sounds, food allergens or anything that could cause pain. Reproductive biology should be taught with respectful, medically accurate language and without asking learners to disclose personal experiences. Avoid animal handling or experiments that stress animals. Refer personal medical questions to qualified health professionals.

## Lesson sequence and practice

### 1. Nervous systems across animal groups
**Objective:** Compare nervous-system organisation in Hydra, Planaria, earthworms, prawns, snails, insects and vertebrates.  
**Starter:** Display nervous-system diagrams from the listed groups; learners arrange them from diffuse nerve nets to more centralised systems.

1. What type of nervous organisation is found in Hydra?  
   **Answer:** A diffuse nerve net.
2. What is a major feature of the Planaria nervous system?  
   **Answer:** Longitudinal nerve cords connected by transverse nerves, often described as a ladder-like arrangement.
3. What is the main advantage of centralisation in a nervous system?  
   **Answer:** It supports coordination and integration of information from different body regions.

### 2. Neuron structure and function
**Objective:** Identify neuron structures and relate them to signal transmission.  
**Starter:** Label a neuron diagram and trace the direction of a signal from input region to output region.

1. What is the usual role of dendrites?  
   **Answer:** They receive signals from other cells.
2. What is the function of the axon?  
   **Answer:** It carries electrical impulses away from the cell body.
3. What is the role of myelin on many axons?  
   **Answer:** It insulates the axon and increases the speed of impulse conduction.

### 3. Membrane potential and nerve impulses
**Objective:** Explain resting potential, action potentials and synaptic transmission.  
**Starter:** Use an animation or graph to identify resting state, depolarisation, repolarisation and recovery.

1. What is the resting membrane potential?  
   **Answer:** The electrical potential difference across a neuron's membrane when it is not producing an action potential.
2. What causes depolarisation during a typical neuronal action potential?  
   **Answer:** Voltage-gated sodium channels open and sodium ions enter the neuron.
3. How is a signal commonly passed across a chemical synapse?  
   **Answer:** Neurotransmitters are released from the presynaptic terminal and bind to receptors on the next cell.

### 4. Central and peripheral nervous systems
**Objective:** Distinguish the central nervous system (CNS) from the peripheral nervous system (PNS).  
**Starter:** Sort labelled structures into CNS and PNS and draw pathways linking receptors, CNS and effectors.

1. Which structures make up the CNS?  
   **Answer:** The brain and spinal cord.
2. What does the PNS include?  
   **Answer:** Nerves and ganglia outside the brain and spinal cord.
3. What is one role of sensory neurons?  
   **Answer:** They carry information from receptors towards the CNS.

### 5. Brain and spinal cord
**Objective:** Relate major brain regions and spinal cord structures to their functions.  
**Starter:** Annotate a brain diagram with broad functions, then trace a simple reflex pathway through the spinal cord.

1. Which brain region is important for planning, memory and conscious thought?  
   **Answer:** The forebrain, particularly the cerebral hemispheres.
2. Which structure helps coordinate balance and movement?  
   **Answer:** The cerebellum, part of the hindbrain.
3. Give one important function of the spinal cord.  
   **Answer:** It carries signals between the brain and body and coordinates some reflexes.

### 6. Somatic and autonomic nervous systems
**Objective:** Compare somatic and autonomic control.  
**Starter:** Classify example responses as mainly somatic or autonomic.

1. Which system controls skeletal muscles for deliberate movement?  
   **Answer:** The somatic nervous system.
2. Which system regulates many involuntary activities such as heart rate and digestion?  
   **Answer:** The autonomic nervous system.
3. Name the two major divisions of the autonomic nervous system.  
   **Answer:** Sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions.

### 7. Human sensory organs and care
**Objective:** Explain the eye, ear, nose, tongue and skin, selected disorders and evidence-based care.  
**Starter:** Match each sensory organ to its receptor type and the stimulus it detects.

1. Which part of the eye controls how much light enters?  
   **Answer:** The iris adjusts pupil size.
2. Which inner-ear structure contains receptors for hearing?  
   **Answer:** The cochlea contains hair cells that detect sound-related vibrations.
3. Which receptors in the nose detect odours?  
   **Answer:** Olfactory receptor neurons in the olfactory epithelium.

### 8. Blind spot, fovea and skin sensitivity
**Objective:** Locate the visual blind spot, explain the fovea and compare skin touch sensitivity.  
**Starter:** Use a printed blind-spot demonstration and a supplied skin-receptor map; learners record observations without touching one another.

1. Why is there a blind spot in each eye?  
   **Answer:** The optic nerve leaves the retina at a region with no photoreceptors.
2. Why does the fovea provide sharp central vision?  
   **Answer:** It has a high density of cones and supports detailed colour vision.
3. Why can fingertips be more sensitive to touch than some other skin regions?  
   **Answer:** They have a high density of touch receptors and small receptive fields.

### 9. Movement structures across animal groups
**Objective:** Compare movement structures in jellyfish, squid, starfish, earthworms, insects, fish and birds.  
**Starter:** Match each animal to its main movement mechanism using labelled diagrams or short videos.

1. How does a jellyfish commonly move through water?  
   **Answer:** By contracting its bell and expelling water for jet propulsion.
2. What structure helps a starfish move?  
   **Answer:** Tube feet operated by the water vascular system.
3. What provides thrust for swimming in many fish?  
   **Answer:** Body and tail-fin movements push water backwards.

### 10. Human bones and muscles
**Objective:** Explain how bones and skeletal muscles work together in movement.  
**Starter:** Use a model of the arm to identify the humerus, radius, ulna, biceps and triceps.

1. What is one function of the skeleton?  
   **Answer:** It supports the body, protects organs and provides attachment points for muscles.
2. Why do skeletal muscles usually work in antagonistic pairs at joints?  
   **Answer:** A muscle can pull but cannot actively push, so an opposing muscle produces movement in the reverse direction.
3. Which muscle contracts to bend the elbow in a typical biceps curl?  
   **Answer:** The biceps brachii contracts while the triceps relaxes.

### 11. Joints and skeletal muscle action
**Objective:** Compare joint types and explain muscle action across joints.  
**Starter:** Examine joint models and classify examples as hinge, ball-and-socket, pivot or gliding joints.

1. What type of joint is the shoulder?  
   **Answer:** A ball-and-socket joint.
2. What type of movement is mainly allowed by the elbow joint?  
   **Answer:** Flexion and extension, like a hinge.
3. What connects skeletal muscle to bone?  
   **Answer:** A tendon.

### 12. Asexual and sexual reproduction in animals
**Objective:** Compare asexual and sexual reproduction in animals.  
**Starter:** Sort examples such as budding, fragmentation and fertilisation into reproductive categories.

1. Does asexual reproduction require the fusion of gametes?  
   **Answer:** No.
2. What is fertilisation?  
   **Answer:** The fusion of male and female gametes to form a zygote.
3. Give one possible advantage of sexual reproduction in changing environments.  
   **Answer:** It can produce genetic variation among offspring.

### 13. Human reproductive system structures
**Objective:** Identify male and female reproductive organs and explain their functions.  
**Starter:** Use medically accurate, age-appropriate diagrams and ask learners to label organs and trace the pathway of gametes.

1. Where are sperm produced?  
   **Answer:** In the seminiferous tubules of the testes.
2. What is the main function of the ovaries?  
   **Answer:** They produce ova and secrete hormones including oestrogen and progesterone.
3. What is the function of the uterus?  
   **Answer:** It is the organ in which an embryo can implant and develop during pregnancy.

### 14. Gamete formation and fertilisation
**Objective:** Describe spermatogenesis, oogenesis and human fertilisation.  
**Starter:** Compare simplified timelines of sperm and egg formation and locate the usual site of fertilisation.

1. What process produces haploid sperm cells?  
   **Answer:** Spermatogenesis, which includes meiosis.
2. What is the usual site of human fertilisation?  
   **Answer:** The ampulla region of the uterine (fallopian) tube.
3. Why is meiosis important in gamete formation?  
   **Answer:** It reduces the chromosome number by half so fertilisation can restore the diploid number.

### 15. Embryonic and post-embryonic development
**Objective:** Compare development in frogs, chickens and humans.  
**Starter:** Place teacher-provided developmental-stage cards in sequence and compare direct development with metamorphosis.

1. What is an embryo?  
   **Answer:** An organism in an early stage of development after fertilisation.
2. What is metamorphosis in frogs?  
   **Answer:** The developmental transformation from a tadpole into an adult frog.
3. Name one extra-embryonic structure that supports a developing chick.  
   **Answer:** The amnion, yolk sac, chorion or allantois; each has a distinct supporting role.

### 16. Hormones and endocrine tissues
**Objective:** Summarise hormone functions and create an endocrine-system concept map.  
**Starter:** Match selected glands or tissues to hormones, target organs and physiological effects.

1. Which gland produces insulin?  
   **Answer:** The pancreas, specifically its beta cells in the islets.
2. What is one major function of thyroid hormones?  
   **Answer:** They help regulate metabolic rate and support growth and development.
3. What is a hormone?  
   **Answer:** A chemical messenger released by cells or glands that affects target cells with appropriate receptors.

### 17. Innate and learned animal behaviour
**Objective:** Compare innate and learned behaviour.  
**Starter:** Sort short animal-behaviour clips into innate, learned or behaviour influenced by both.

1. What is an innate behaviour?  
   **Answer:** A behaviour that is present without the individual having to learn it through experience.
2. What is habituation?  
   **Answer:** A simple form of learning in which response to a repeated, harmless stimulus decreases.
3. Give one example of learned behaviour.  
   **Answer:** A dog learning to respond to a trained signal or a bird learning a song through experience.

### 18. Behaviour and nervous-system evolution
**Objective:** Relate behaviour to the evolution of nervous-system organisation.  
**Starter:** Compare evidence from animal case studies, avoiding the assumption that evolution is a simple ladder of progress.

1. What is one broad trend seen in many animal lineages?  
   **Answer:** Nervous systems can show increasing specialisation and centralisation, although evolution is branching and diverse.
2. How can sensory specialisation influence behaviour?  
   **Answer:** It allows animals to detect particular environmental cues and respond in ways relevant to survival or reproduction.
3. Why should animal nervous systems not be ranked as a simple ladder from inferior to superior?  
   **Answer:** Different nervous systems are adaptations to different environments and ways of life.

### 19. Animal communication
**Objective:** Explain visual, acoustic, chemical and tactile communication between animals.  
**Starter:** Show examples of courtship displays, alarm calls, scent marking and tactile signals; learners identify signal, receiver and likely response.

1. What is a pheromone?  
   **Answer:** A chemical signal released by an organism that affects the behaviour or physiology of another member of the same species.
2. Give one example of acoustic communication.  
   **Answer:** A bird song or a frog call used in territorial or reproductive signalling.
3. Why does the receiver's sensory system matter in animal communication?  
   **Answer:** The receiver must be able to detect and interpret the signal for it to influence behaviour.

## Assessment and teacher review

- Require labelled diagrams and accurate signal pathways for nervous-system topics.
- Assess comparisons across animal groups using evidence rather than simplistic evolutionary rankings.
- Use safe, optional sensory demonstrations and accessible alternatives.
- Keep reproductive-health teaching factual, respectful and age appropriate.
- Link lessons and questions to the corresponding M6-BIO-ANIMAL-XX objective after the knowledge-graph migration is reviewed and applied.

# M6 Biology — Biodiversity, Origin of Life and Classification Teacher Starter Pack

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017)  
**Subject / grade:** Biology, M6  
**Official strand:** Additional Biology strand 2  
**Coverage:** 5 official M6 learning outcomes; 5 lesson starters; 15 practice questions with answer keys  
**Use:** Teacher planning resource. Use diagrams, fossil and molecular datasets, safe local observations and fictional classification sets. Present origin-of-life explanations as scientific hypotheses evaluated against evidence, not as settled details.

## Lesson 1 — Levels and importance of biodiversity
**Objective:** M6-BIO-BIODIV-01 — Explain genetic, species and ecosystem diversity and how they are connected.
**Starter / learning task:** Explain genetic, species and ecosystem diversity and how they are connected. Have learners justify their explanations with examples, diagrams or evidence from supplied resources.

**Practice**
1. What is genetic diversity?
   **Answer:** Variation in genetic information among individuals or populations of the same species.
2. How is species diversity different from ecosystem diversity?
   **Answer:** Species diversity concerns the variety and relative abundance of species; ecosystem diversity concerns the variety of habitats and ecological systems.
3. Why does biodiversity matter to people and ecosystems?
   **Answer:** It supports ecosystem functions and services, resilience, food resources, medicines and cultural values.

## Lesson 2 — Origin of early cells and unicellular life
**Objective:** M6-BIO-BIODIV-02 — Evaluate scientific hypotheses and evidence about early cells and the evolution of unicellular organisms.
**Starter / learning task:** Evaluate scientific hypotheses and evidence about early cells and the evolution of unicellular organisms. Have learners justify their explanations with examples, diagrams or evidence from supplied resources.

**Practice**
1. What is a protocell in origin-of-life research?
   **Answer:** A proposed early cell-like system with a boundary and some life-like chemical processes; it is not itself a confirmed living ancestor.
2. What evidence supports the ancient existence of microbial life?
   **Answer:** Evidence includes ancient microbial structures, chemical signatures and geological records, interpreted with uncertainty.
3. Why should explanations of life's origin distinguish evidence from hypotheses?
   **Answer:** The early Earth record is incomplete, so scientific models are evaluated and revised as new evidence appears.

## Lesson 3 — Major groups of organisms
**Objective:** M6-BIO-BIODIV-03 — Compare bacteria, protists, plants, fungi and animals using cellular and nutritional characteristics.
**Starter / learning task:** Compare bacteria, protists, plants, fungi and animals using cellular and nutritional characteristics. Have learners justify their explanations with examples, diagrams or evidence from supplied resources.

**Practice**
1. Are bacteria prokaryotic or eukaryotic?
   **Answer:** Prokaryotic; they lack a membrane-bound nucleus.
2. What feature distinguishes fungi from plants in nutrition?
   **Answer:** Fungi absorb nutrients from organic material; plants generally photosynthesise, although there are exceptions.
3. Why is appearance alone unreliable for classification?
   **Answer:** Similar forms can evolve independently; cellular, reproductive and molecular evidence provides stronger relationships.

## Lesson 4 — Taxonomic hierarchy and scientific names
**Objective:** M6-BIO-BIODIV-04 — Classify organisms through taxonomic ranks and write species-level scientific names correctly.
**Starter / learning task:** Classify organisms through taxonomic ranks and write species-level scientific names correctly. Have learners justify their explanations with examples, diagrams or evidence from supplied resources.

**Practice**
1. Name the common taxonomic ranks from broad to specific.
   **Answer:** Domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species.
2. How is a binomial scientific name written?
   **Answer:** The genus begins with a capital letter, the specific epithet is lowercase, and the whole name is italicised when typed.
3. Why are scientific names useful?
   **Answer:** They provide a standard international name that reduces ambiguity among common names.

## Lesson 5 — Dichotomous keys
**Objective:** M6-BIO-BIODIV-05 — Construct and apply a dichotomous key using observable paired characteristics.
**Starter / learning task:** Construct and apply a dichotomous key using observable paired characteristics. Have learners justify their explanations with examples, diagrams or evidence from supplied resources.

**Practice**
1. What is a dichotomous key?
   **Answer:** An identification tool that uses a sequence of paired choices to identify an organism or specimen.
2. What makes a useful pair of statements in a key?
   **Answer:** The two alternatives are clear, mutually exclusive and based on observable characteristics.
3. Why should a key avoid vague terms such as 'large' without a reference?
   **Answer:** Different users may interpret them differently, reducing reliable identification.

# M6 Biology — Ecology and Environmental Sustainability Teacher Starter Pack

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017)  
**Subject / grade:** Biology, M6  
**Official strand:** Additional Biology strand 5 — Ecology  
**Coverage:** 13 official M6 learning outcomes; 13 lesson starters; 39 practice questions with answer keys  
**Use:** Teacher planning resource. Review local examples, data and suitability before classroom use.

## Teaching sequence

Each lesson is designed for a short introductory segment followed by guided analysis, discussion or a data task. Use local Thai and global examples where reliable evidence is available.

### Lesson 1 — Energy transfer in ecosystems
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-01 — Analyse energy transfer through trophic levels and distinguish energy flow from matter cycling.  
**Starter:** Display a simple rice-field food web (rice plant, grasshopper, frog, snake, decomposers). Ask learners to trace two possible energy pathways.  
**Learning task:** Learners label producers, consumers and decomposers, then explain why energy flows one way while nutrients are recycled. Use an ecological pyramid and supplied energy values; describe the approximate 10% transfer idea as a rough model, not a fixed law.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners explain why food chains usually contain a limited number of trophic levels.

**Practice**
1. What is the role of a producer in a food chain?  
   **Answer:** It converts light or chemical energy into stored chemical energy in organic matter, forming the base of many food chains.
2. In a grass → grasshopper → frog chain, which organism is the secondary consumer?  
   **Answer:** The frog.
3. Why is energy described as flowing through an ecosystem rather than cycling?  
   **Answer:** Energy enters (often as sunlight), passes between organisms and is eventually dissipated as heat; matter is recycled.

### Lesson 2 — Biomagnification
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-02 — Explain increasing concentrations of persistent pollutants at higher trophic levels and ways to reduce the problem.  
**Starter:** Present a fictional dataset showing concentrations of a persistent pollutant in plankton, small fish, large fish and fish-eating birds. Ask learners to identify the trend.  
**Learning task:** Distinguish bioaccumulation within an organism from biomagnification across trophic levels. Discuss persistence, fat solubility and food-web transfer. Evaluate prevention at source, regulation, monitoring and safe disposal.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners explain why top predators may receive higher pollutant concentrations.

**Practice**
1. What is biomagnification?  
   **Answer:** An increase in the concentration of a persistent substance in organisms at successively higher trophic levels.
2. How is bioaccumulation different?  
   **Answer:** Bioaccumulation is the build-up of a substance within an individual organism over time.
3. Give one effective way to reduce biomagnification risk.  
   **Answer:** Prevent persistent pollutants from entering the environment through safer substitutes, strict controls and proper waste treatment/disposal.

### Lesson 3 — Nitrogen, sulfur and phosphorus cycles
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-03 — Explain transfers and transformations in three biogeochemical cycles.  
**Starter:** Give groups mixed process cards: nitrogen fixation, nitrification, assimilation, decomposition, denitrification, weathering and atmospheric deposition. Ask them to sort cards into cycles.  
**Learning task:** Learners draw labelled cycle diagrams. Include nitrogen fixation and denitrification; sulfur movement through rocks, organisms and atmosphere; phosphorus weathering, uptake and decomposition. Emphasise that phosphorus has no major gaseous phase in its usual short-term cycle.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners describe how decomposers return nutrients to the environment.

**Practice**
1. What is nitrogen fixation?  
   **Answer:** Conversion of atmospheric nitrogen gas into biologically usable nitrogen compounds, commonly by certain microorganisms.
2. Which process returns nitrogen gas to the atmosphere from nitrates?  
   **Answer:** Denitrification by microorganisms under suitable conditions.
3. What is a major natural source of phosphate entering ecosystems?  
   **Answer:** Weathering of phosphate-containing rocks, followed by movement into soil and water.

### Lesson 4 — Biomes and geographic patterns
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-04 — Compare biomes using climate and characteristic organisms.  
**Starter:** Show climate graphs for a tropical rainforest, desert, grassland and tundra. Learners predict vegetation before seeing photographs.  
**Learning task:** Compare temperature, precipitation, seasonality, vegetation structure and adaptations. Connect latitude, altitude and ocean influence to broad biome patterns.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners justify why two places at similar latitudes may still have different ecosystems.

**Practice**
1. Which two climate variables are especially useful when classifying terrestrial biomes?  
   **Answer:** Temperature and precipitation.
2. Why are desert plants often adapted to reduce water loss?  
   **Answer:** Water availability is limited and evaporation can be high.
3. Name one factor besides latitude that can influence biome conditions.  
   **Answer:** Altitude, distance from the sea, ocean currents or prevailing winds.

### Lesson 5 — Primary and secondary succession
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-05 — Compare the starting conditions and community changes in primary and secondary succession.  
**Starter:** Compare images of bare rock after a new lava flow and a regenerating forest after a storm. Ask what biological material or soil remains.  
**Learning task:** Sequence pioneer species, soil development and later communities. Explain that secondary succession begins where soil or biological legacies remain, so it is often faster than primary succession. Avoid presenting succession as a guaranteed fixed path to one final community.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners classify scenarios and justify their choices.

**Practice**
1. What is primary succession?  
   **Answer:** Community development in a place initially lacking developed soil, such as newly exposed bare rock.
2. Why is secondary succession often faster?  
   **Answer:** Soil, nutrients, seeds, roots or surviving organisms may remain after the disturbance.
3. Give one event that may initiate secondary succession.  
   **Answer:** A storm, fire or abandoned farmland where soil remains.

### Lesson 6 — Characteristics of populations
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-06 — Describe population size, density, distribution and demographic characteristics.  
**Starter:** Give learners a map with counts of the same species in several sample plots. Ask whether the species is evenly or unevenly distributed.  
**Learning task:** Introduce population size, density, dispersion, age structure, birth rate, death rate and migration. Use supplied sample data to calculate density and interpret a simple age-structure diagram.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners explain why population size alone does not describe population condition.

**Practice**
1. What is population density?  
   **Answer:** The number of individuals of a population per unit area or volume.
2. Name two processes that can increase population size.  
   **Answer:** Births and immigration.
3. What does an age-structure diagram help ecologists understand?  
   **Answer:** The proportions of individuals in different age groups and possible future population trends.

### Lesson 7 — Exponential and logistic growth
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-07 — Interpret exponential and logistic population-growth models.  
**Starter:** Show J-shaped and S-shaped curves without labels. Ask learners to describe how growth rate changes.  
**Learning task:** Explain exponential growth under abundant resources and logistic growth as limits slow growth near carrying capacity. Use simple data tables and discuss why real populations fluctuate rather than following perfect curves.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners identify carrying capacity on a logistic curve.

**Practice**
1. What shape is commonly associated with exponential population growth?  
   **Answer:** A J-shaped curve.
2. What is carrying capacity?  
   **Answer:** The population size an environment can sustain over time under prevailing conditions.
3. Why does logistic growth slow as a population approaches carrying capacity?  
   **Answer:** Resources and other environmental limits reduce the population's per-capita growth rate.

### Lesson 8 — Factors controlling population growth
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-08 — Explain how environmental factors and demographic processes affect population size.  
**Starter:** Sort examples such as disease, competition, drought, flood, food supply and predation into possible limiting factors.  
**Learning task:** Distinguish density-dependent factors (often stronger as density rises, such as competition and transmission of some diseases) from density-independent events (such as severe storms). Link births, deaths and migration to population change.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners explain why the same factor may affect populations differently in different settings.

**Practice**
1. Give one density-dependent limiting factor.  
   **Answer:** Competition for food or space, or transmission of an infectious disease.
2. Give one density-independent factor.  
   **Answer:** A severe storm, flood, drought or sudden temperature event.
3. How does immigration affect population size?  
   **Answer:** It adds individuals to the population.

### Lesson 9 — Water scarcity and water pollution
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-09 — Analyse causes, impacts and management of water scarcity and pollution.  
**Starter:** Present a fictional watershed map showing homes, farms, industry and a river. Ask learners to identify possible water pressures without collecting or touching water.  
**Learning task:** Analyse physical and economic scarcity, overuse, drought, contamination and eutrophication. Evaluate demand management, watershed protection, wastewater treatment, efficient irrigation and monitoring using credible datasets.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners connect a proposed solution to the cause it addresses.

**Practice**
1. What is eutrophication?  
   **Answer:** Nutrient enrichment of water that can stimulate excessive algal growth and reduce dissolved oxygen as biomass decomposes.
2. Name one cause of water scarcity.  
   **Answer:** Drought, over-abstraction, pollution that makes water unusable, or inadequate infrastructure.
3. Give one way to reduce agricultural water demand.  
   **Answer:** Improve irrigation efficiency, schedule irrigation appropriately or use suitable water-saving practices.

### Lesson 10 — Air pollution
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-10 — Analyse air-pollution sources, impacts and mitigation.  
**Starter:** Display a reliable air-quality chart (for example, PM2.5 levels) and ask learners to describe trends without making medical diagnoses.  
**Learning task:** Compare particulate matter and gaseous pollutants, identify sources such as traffic, industry, open burning and some natural events, and examine impacts on health, visibility, ecosystems and climate. Evaluate emission controls, cleaner transport, energy efficiency and prevention of open burning.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners distinguish reducing emissions at source from responding after pollution occurs.

**Practice**
1. What does PM2.5 describe?  
   **Answer:** Fine airborne particles with aerodynamic diameters of 2.5 micrometres or less.
2. Give one human source of air pollution.  
   **Answer:** Vehicle exhaust, industrial emissions, power generation or open burning.
3. Why is source control usually important?  
   **Answer:** It prevents pollutants from entering the air rather than only treating exposure after emissions occur.

### Lesson 11 — Soil resource problems
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-11 — Analyse soil erosion, degradation and contamination and suitable responses.  
**Starter:** Compare two diagrams of sloping land, one with vegetation cover and one bare. Ask where runoff and soil loss may be greater.  
**Learning task:** Examine erosion, compaction, salinisation, nutrient depletion and contamination. Evaluate ground cover, contour farming, terracing where suitable, organic matter management and pollution prevention.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners match each soil problem to a context-appropriate conservation response.

**Practice**
1. What is soil erosion?  
   **Answer:** The removal and transport of topsoil by water, wind or other processes.
2. How can vegetation reduce erosion?  
   **Answer:** Roots help hold soil and plant cover reduces the impact of rain and wind.
3. Name one cause of soil degradation.  
   **Answer:** Overgrazing, deforestation, poor land management, salinisation or contamination.

### Lesson 12 — Deforestation and forest conservation
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-12 — Analyse deforestation impacts and evaluate forest protection and restoration.  
**Starter:** Use before-and-after satellite images of a forest landscape. Ask learners to identify changes in habitat and land cover.  
**Learning task:** Analyse habitat fragmentation, carbon storage, water cycles, soil erosion, livelihoods and biodiversity. Compare protected areas, sustainable forestry, restoration, community stewardship and demand-side measures. Discuss that tree planting alone does not replace the ecological value of intact natural forests.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners evaluate a conservation proposal against ecological and community outcomes.

**Practice**
1. What is habitat fragmentation?  
   **Answer:** The division of a habitat into smaller, separated patches.
2. Give one ecological consequence of deforestation.  
   **Answer:** Habitat loss, reduced biodiversity, increased erosion or changes to local water and carbon cycles.
3. Why should forest restoration use suitable native species and local ecological knowledge?  
   **Answer:** To improve ecological fit, support native biodiversity and reduce risks from unsuitable planting.

### Lesson 13 — Wildlife decline and conservation
**Objective:** M6-BIO-ECO-13 — Analyse causes and consequences of wildlife decline and evaluate conservation approaches.  
**Starter:** Present a case study with a species population trend and habitat map. Ask learners to identify what evidence is still needed before choosing an intervention.  
**Learning task:** Analyse habitat loss, overexploitation, invasive species, pollution, disease and climate change. Evaluate habitat protection, wildlife corridors, enforcement, community-based conservation, sustainable use and evidence-led recovery programmes.  
**Check for understanding:** Learners propose a measurable conservation outcome and identify how it could be monitored.

**Practice**
1. Name two major causes of wildlife population decline.  
   **Answer:** Habitat loss, overexploitation, invasive species, pollution, disease or climate change (any two).
2. How can a wildlife corridor help a population?  
   **Answer:** It can connect habitat patches, allowing movement, dispersal and gene flow.
3. Why should conservation programmes monitor population data over time?  
   **Answer:** To evaluate whether interventions are effective and adapt management when results are poor.

## Assessment and teacher review

- Use the 39 questions as formative checks; adapt them to the class language level and local examples.
- Ask learners to support environmental claims with data and distinguish correlation from causation.
