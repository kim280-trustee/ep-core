# M5 Earth, Astronomy and Space Science — Weather, Atmosphere, Oceans and Climate

**Official alignment:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017), Additional Earth, Astronomy and Space Science, M5 standard 2, outcomes 1–13. The official IPST curriculum-use manual lists these outcomes across printed pages 21–24 and provides teacher analysis and activities in the M5 section.

Each outcome below includes a lesson starter and three answer-keyed practice questions.

## Outcome 1 — Uneven Solar Energy and Regional Air Temperature

**Objective:** Explain factors causing differences in solar energy received and emitted across Earth and relate them to regional air-temperature differences.

**Starter:** Compare the angle of sunlight at the equator and at higher latitudes. Ask why the same beam of sunlight heats different surface areas.

**Teach:** Latitude, Earth's spherical shape and axial tilt; surface albedo; clouds, aerosols and greenhouse gases; absorption, reflection and emission.

**Questions**
1. Why does sunlight generally heat the equator more directly than high latitudes? **Answer:** Sunlight strikes at a higher angle and its energy is concentrated over a smaller surface area.
2. Name two factors besides latitude that affect local heating. **Answer:** Surface type/albedo and cloud cover; greenhouse gases and aerosols also affect energy transfer.
3. What happens when the same incoming solar energy is spread over a larger surface area? **Answer:** Energy received per unit area decreases.

## Outcome 2 — Earth’s Energy Balance

**Objective:** Explain the processes that establish Earth’s energy balance, including incoming solar radiation, reflection, absorption, transfer and outgoing radiation.

**Starter:** Ask students what might happen to Earth's average temperature if Earth absorbed more energy than it emitted for many years.

**Teach:** Incoming shortwave solar radiation, reflection (albedo), absorption by atmosphere and surface, transfer of energy, outgoing longwave radiation and the greenhouse effect.

**Questions**
1. What is Earth's energy balance? **Answer:** Over a long-term average, incoming energy is balanced by energy returned to space.
2. What is albedo? **Answer:** The fraction of incoming radiation reflected by a surface or system.
3. If Earth absorbs more energy than it emits, what happens to its energy content? **Answer:** It increases, producing a warming tendency.

## Outcome 3 — Forces Driving Atmospheric Circulation

**Objective:** Explain how pressure-gradient force, Coriolis force, centripetal force and friction affect atmospheric circulation and wind.

**Starter:** Use a simple pressure map with high- and low-pressure centres. Ask students which way air initially moves.

**Teach:** Pressure-gradient force drives air from high toward low pressure; Coriolis deflects moving air due to Earth's rotation; friction slows near-surface wind; curved flow involves centripetal acceleration.

**Questions**
1. In which general direction does the pressure-gradient force act? **Answer:** From higher pressure toward lower pressure.
2. How does the Coriolis effect deflect moving air in the Northern Hemisphere? **Answer:** To the right of its direction of motion.
3. Why is surface wind usually slower than wind higher above the ground? **Answer:** Friction with the surface and obstacles slows it.

## Outcome 4 — Latitudinal Atmospheric Circulation

**Objective:** Explain atmospheric circulation by latitude and relate global circulation cells to prevailing winds and climate patterns.

**Starter:** Display a world map with the equator and 30°, 60° and 90° latitude lines. Ask why wet and dry climate belts occur in broad bands.

**Teach:** Hadley, Ferrel and Polar cells; rising air near the equator and around subpolar zones; sinking air in subtropical and polar high-pressure belts; trade winds and westerlies.

**Questions**
1. What is the name of the circulation cell between the equator and about 30° latitude? **Answer:** Hadley cell.
2. Why are many subtropical regions relatively dry? **Answer:** Air commonly sinks there, warms and becomes less favourable for cloud formation and rainfall.
3. What are trade winds? **Answer:** Persistent tropical easterly winds associated with the Hadley circulation.

## Outcome 5 — Ocean-Water Stratification

**Objective:** Explain factors that produce ocean-water layers, including differences in temperature, salinity and density.

**Starter:** Show a diagram of a warm surface layer above colder deep ocean water. Ask why the layers do not mix quickly.

**Teach:** Temperature and salinity influence density; surface mixed layer, thermocline and deep layer; stratification can inhibit vertical mixing.

**Questions**
1. Which is generally denser: colder seawater or warmer seawater of the same salinity? **Answer:** Colder seawater.
2. What is a thermocline? **Answer:** A layer where water temperature changes rapidly with depth.
3. How can increased salinity affect seawater density? **Answer:** It generally increases density.

## Outcome 6 — Ocean Circulation and Its Patterns

**Objective:** Explain factors driving surface and deep-ocean circulation and describe major patterns of ocean-water movement.

**Starter:** Display a global ocean-current map. Ask why currents do not all flow in the same direction.

**Teach:** Surface currents are driven mainly by winds and shaped by Coriolis effect and continents; deep circulation is influenced by density differences from temperature and salinity; connect surface and deep circulation.

**Questions**
1. What is the main driver of many surface ocean currents? **Answer:** Prevailing winds.
2. What drives thermohaline circulation? **Answer:** Density differences caused mainly by temperature and salinity.
3. What is one way continents affect ocean currents? **Answer:** They redirect currents and help shape ocean gyres.

## Outcome 7 — Effects of Ocean Circulation

**Objective:** Explain how ocean circulation affects weather and climate, marine organisms and environmental conditions.

**Starter:** Compare coastal climate near a warm current with a coast influenced by a cold current.

**Teach:** Heat transport, coastal temperature and rainfall patterns, nutrient upwelling, marine productivity, and climate variability including El Niño and La Niña.

**Questions**
1. How can a warm ocean current affect nearby coastal air? **Answer:** It can warm and moisten the air, influencing local weather and climate.
2. Why can upwelling support productive fisheries? **Answer:** It brings nutrient-rich deeper water toward the surface, supporting plankton and food webs.
3. El Niño is associated with changes in which ocean-atmosphere system? **Answer:** The tropical Pacific Ocean and its coupled atmospheric circulation.

## Outcome 8 — Atmospheric Stability and Cloud Formation

**Objective:** Explain the relationship between atmospheric stability, vertical air movement and cloud formation.

**Starter:** Show a cumulonimbus cloud and a clear-sky image. Ask what conditions help air rise to form tall clouds.

**Teach:** Compare the temperature of a rising air parcel with surrounding air; stable air resists vertical movement, while unstable air supports rising motion; cooling to dew point leads to condensation and cloud formation.

**Questions**
1. What happens to an air parcel that is colder and denser than its surroundings? **Answer:** It tends to sink and the atmosphere is stable with respect to that parcel.
2. What happens to a warm, buoyant air parcel in an unstable atmosphere? **Answer:** It continues to rise.
3. What process forms cloud droplets when rising air cools to saturation? **Answer:** Water vapour condenses onto cloud condensation nuclei.

## Outcome 9 — Weather Fronts and Associated Conditions

**Objective:** Explain how warm, cold, occluded and stationary fronts form and relate each front type to associated cloud and weather conditions.

**Starter:** Show a weather map with a cold-front symbol and ask what happens when two air masses meet.

**Teach:** A front is a boundary between air masses with different properties. Compare warm, cold, stationary and occluded fronts, their slopes, cloud patterns and typical precipitation.

**Questions**
1. What is a cold front? **Answer:** The boundary where advancing colder, denser air pushes beneath warmer air.
2. Which front commonly produces widespread layered cloud and steady precipitation ahead of it? **Answer:** A warm front.
3. What happens at an occluded front? **Answer:** A cold front overtakes a warm front, lifting the warm air from the surface.

## Outcome 10 — Drivers of Global Climate Change

**Objective:** Explain natural and human-related factors that affect global climate, using evidence such as ice-core records and observed climate data.

**Starter:** Compare a graph of long-term global temperature with atmospheric carbon-dioxide measurements. Ask what evidence can establish a climate trend.

**Teach:** Natural drivers include orbital variations, volcanic aerosols and solar variability; human drivers include greenhouse-gas emissions, land-use change and aerosols. Evaluate evidence from ice cores, instrumental records and other proxies.

**Questions**
1. Name one major human influence on recent global warming. **Answer:** Increased greenhouse-gas concentrations from fossil-fuel use and land-use change.
2. What can ice cores tell scientists about past climate? **Answer:** They preserve indicators such as trapped ancient air and isotopic signals that help reconstruct past atmospheric composition and temperature.
3. Is one unusually cold day evidence against long-term global warming? **Answer:** No. Weather varies day to day; climate trends are assessed over longer periods.

## Outcome 11 — Climate-Change Impacts and Mitigation

**Objective:** Analyse and discuss events and impacts associated with global climate change and present human practices that can help slow climate change.

**Starter:** Ask students to identify possible local impacts of rising temperatures, changing rainfall and sea-level rise.

**Teach:** Analyse observed and projected impacts on ecosystems, water, food systems, health and coastal communities. Distinguish mitigation (reducing causes) from adaptation (reducing harm).

**Questions**
1. Give one possible impact of rising global temperature on coastal communities. **Answer:** Sea-level rise can increase coastal flooding and erosion.
2. Is planting trees mitigation or adaptation? **Answer:** It can contribute to mitigation by storing carbon, depending on species, location and long-term management.
3. Give one example of climate adaptation. **Answer:** Improving flood warning systems or designing infrastructure for heat and heavy rainfall.

## Outcome 12 — Interpreting Weather-Map Symbols

**Objective:** Interpret standard weather-station symbols and map features, including pressure, temperature, humidity, wind, cloud and precipitation information.

**Starter:** Display a station model and ask students to identify wind direction, cloud cover and temperature.

**Teach:** Read station plots and isobars; identify high- and low-pressure systems, fronts, wind barbs, cloud cover and precipitation symbols; check map legend and time.

**Questions**
1. What do closely spaced isobars usually indicate? **Answer:** A stronger pressure gradient and generally stronger winds.
2. What does a wind barb show? **Answer:** Wind direction and speed.
3. Why must the time of observation be checked when interpreting a weather map? **Answer:** Weather systems move and conditions change over time.

## Outcome 13 — Analysing and Forecasting Weather from Data

**Objective:** Use weather maps and supporting information such as forecasts, radar and satellite observations to analyse and make basic weather predictions for practical planning.

**Starter:** Give students a simple forecast, radar image and satellite image. Ask what each source contributes.

**Teach:** Combine station observations, pressure patterns, fronts, radar precipitation and satellite cloud information; recognise uncertainty and avoid treating a forecast as a guarantee.

**Questions**
1. What does weather radar help identify? **Answer:** The location and movement of precipitation.
2. What can satellite imagery show that a single weather station cannot? **Answer:** Large-scale cloud patterns and the development and movement of weather systems.
3. Why should forecasts be updated before planning an outdoor event? **Answer:** Forecasts change as new observations and model information become available.

## Teacher safety and assessment notes

Use official weather data, maps, simulations and safe classroom demonstrations. Do not ask students to approach storms, climb exposed locations or observe lightning outdoors. Climate-change tasks should use traceable datasets and distinguish evidence, uncertainty, weather variability, long-term climate trends, mitigation and adaptation. Assess interpretation of data and reasoning, not memorisation of unsupported local forecasts.
