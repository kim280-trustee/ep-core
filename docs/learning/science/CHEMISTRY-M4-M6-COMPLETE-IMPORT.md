## Lesson 1 — Atomic models and scientific evidence
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-ATOM-01 — Compare Dalton, Thomson, Rutherford, Bohr and quantum-mechanical models. Use evidence and explain why scientific models change.
**Starter / learning task:** What is an atomic model? Use worked examples, particle diagrams, periodic-table data or teacher-provided datasets to develop the concept. Guide learners to explain their reasoning and check units, symbols and assumptions.

**Practice**
1. What is an atomic model?
   **Answer:** A scientific representation used to explain and predict atomic structure and behaviour.
2. What evidence challenged the plum-pudding model?
   **Answer:** Rutherford's alpha-scattering results showed a small, dense, positively charged nucleus.
3. Why are models revised?
   **Answer:** New evidence may reveal limitations in an existing explanation.

## Lesson 2 — Nuclear symbols and isotopes
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-ATOM-02 — Interpret nuclide notation and calculate proton, neutron and electron counts for neutral atoms and simple ions.
**Starter / learning task:** What does atomic number represent? Use worked examples, particle diagrams, periodic-table data or teacher-provided datasets to develop the concept. Guide learners to explain their reasoning and check units, symbols and assumptions.

**Practice**
1. What does atomic number represent?
   **Answer:** The number of protons in the nucleus.
2. How many neutrons are in carbon-14?
   **Answer:** 14 − 6 = 8 neutrons.
3. What are isotopes?
   **Answer:** Atoms of the same element with equal proton numbers but different neutron numbers.

## Lesson 3 — Electron configurations

**Starter / learning task:** How many electrons does a neutral atom with atomic number 11 have? Use worked examples, particle diagrams, periodic-table data or teacher-provided datasets to develop the concept. Guide learners to explain their reasoning and check units, symbols and assumptions.

**Practice**
1. How many electrons does a neutral atom with atomic number 11 have?
   **Answer:** 11 electrons.
2. Write the shell arrangement for sodium.
   **Answer:** 2, 8, 1.
3. Why do valence electrons matter?
   **Answer:** They strongly influence bonding and chemical reactivity.

## Lesson 4 — Groups, periods and element classification
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-ATOM-04 — Locate representative and transition elements and classify metals, non-metals and metalloids.
**Starter / learning task:** What does a period indicate? Use worked examples, particle diagrams, periodic-table data or teacher-provided datasets to develop the concept. Guide learners to explain their reasoning and check units, symbols and assumptions.

**Practice**

   **Answer:** A horizontal row; for main-group elements it relates to occupied principal energy levels.
2. Where are most metals found?

3. Name one metalloid.

## Lesson 5 — Periodic trends
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-ATOM-05 — Use atomic radius, ionisation energy and electronegativity trends to explain patterns across periods and down groups.
**Starter / learning task:** What generally happens to atomic radius across a period? Use worked examples, particle diagrams, periodic-table data or teacher-provided datasets to develop the concept. Guide learners to explain their reasoning and check units, symbols and assumptions.

**Practice**
1. What generally happens to atomic radius across a period?
   **Answer:** It generally decreases as effective nuclear attraction increases.
2. What generally happens to atomic radius down a group?

3. Why do periodic trends repeat?
   **Answer:** Elements have repeating valence-electron patterns.

## Lesson 6 — Transition metals

## Lesson 7 — Radioactivity and half-life

2. A sample has a half-life of 5 days. What fraction remains after 10 days?

3. Why is half-life useful?
   **Answer:** It helps estimate decay over time and compare radioactive materials.

## Lesson 8 — Element applications and environmental impacts

## Lesson 9 — Ion formation and ionic bonding

**Starter / learning task:** How does sodium form Na⁺? Use worked examples, particle diagrams, periodic-table data or teacher-provided datasets to develop the concept. Guide learners to explain their reasoning and check units, symbols and assumptions.

3. What holds an ionic solid together?

## Lesson 10 — Ionic formulas and names
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-ATOM-10 — Construct electrically neutral formula units from ion charges and name common ionic compounds.
**Starter / learning task:** What is the formula of magnesium chloride? Use worked examples, particle diagrams, periodic-table data or teacher-provided datasets to develop the concept. Guide learners to explain their reasoning and check units, symbols and assumptions.

## Lesson 11 — Born-Haber cycles

**Starter / learning task:** What principle underpins a Born-Haber cycle? Use worked examples, particle diagrams, periodic-table data or teacher-provided datasets to develop the concept. Guide learners to explain their reasoning and check units, symbols and assumptions.

2. What does atomisation represent?
   **Answer:** Formation of gaseous atoms from an element in its standard state.
3. Why state the sign convention for lattice enthalpy?
   **Answer:** Definitions differ; the sign depends on whether lattice formation or separation is described.

## Lesson 12 — Properties of ionic compounds
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-ATOM-12 — Connect ionic lattice structure with melting, brittleness, solubility and conductivity.
**Starter / learning task:** Why do ionic solids often have high melting points? Use worked examples, particle diagrams, periodic-table data or teacher-provided datasets to develop the concept. Guide learners to explain their reasoning and check units, symbols and assumptions.

**Practice**
1. Why do ionic solids often have high melting points?
   **Answer:** Strong electrostatic attractions in the lattice require substantial energy to overcome.
2. Why can a solid ionic crystal conduct poorly?
   **Answer:** Its ions are fixed in place and cannot move freely.
3. When can an ionic substance conduct electricity?
   **Answer:** When molten or dissolved and its ions are mobile.

## Lesson 13 — Ionic and net ionic equations
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-ATOM-13 — Write balanced ionic equations and cancel spectator ions in precipitation or neutralisation examples.

## Lesson 16 — Bond length and bond energy
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-ATOM-16 — Compare single, double and triple bonds and estimate reaction enthalpy from average bond enthalpies.
**Starter / learning task:** Which is generally shortest: C–C, C=C or C≡C? Use worked examples, particle diagrams, periodic-table data or teacher-provided datasets to develop the concept. Guide learners to explain their reasoning and check units, symbols and assumptions.

2. What does bond enthalpy estimate?

## Assessment and safety

- Use the questions as formative checks and include structured calculation problems with units and significant figures where appropriate.
- Use simulations, diagrams and teacher-selected datasets for radioactive decay; do not handle radioactive sources in ordinary classroom activities.
- Do not allow unsupervised handling of corrosive chemicals, reactive metals, toxic compounds or open flames. Use microscale demonstrations and approved safety procedures where practical work is required.
- For Born-Haber and bond-enthalpy calculations, state the data source, definitions and sign convention explicitly.
- Ask learners to distinguish a model from direct observation and support claims with evidence.

# M4 Chemistry — Chemical Reactions and Stoichiometry Teacher Starter Pack

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017)  
**Subject / grade:** Chemistry, M4  
**Official strand:** Additional Chemistry strand 2  
**Coverage:** 7 official M4 learning outcomes; 7 lesson starters; 21 practice questions with answer keys  
**Use:** Teacher planning resource. Use teacher-approved datasets and demonstrations. Do not mix chemicals or conduct gas-producing reactions without an approved risk assessment, suitable equipment and direct teacher supervision.

## Lesson 1 — Interpreting and balancing chemical equations
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-STOICH-01 — Interpret formulae, coefficients, state symbols and reaction conditions; balance equations by conserving atoms.
**Starter / learning task:** Display an unbalanced equation and ask what information the formulae and arrow communicate. Model balancing with atom-count tables before students practise.

**Practice**
1. What does a coefficient in a chemical equation show?  
   **Answer:** The relative number of particles or moles of that substance.
2. Balance: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O.  
   **Answer:** 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.
3. Why must an equation be balanced?  
   **Answer:** Atoms are conserved in a chemical reaction; each element must have equal atom counts on both sides.

## Lesson 2 — Stoichiometry using mass
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-STOICH-02 — Convert mass to amount of substance and use mole ratios to calculate reactant or product masses.
**Starter / learning task:** Use a balanced equation and molar-mass table. Model the sequence: mass → moles → mole ratio → moles → mass.

**Practice**
1. What is the amount of substance in 18 g of water (M = 18 g mol⁻¹)?  
   **Answer:** 1 mol.
2. In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, how many moles of water form from 3 mol H₂ with sufficient oxygen?  
   **Answer:** 3 mol H₂O.
3. What conversion is needed before using a mole ratio with a mass?  
   **Answer:** Convert mass to moles using molar mass.

## Lesson 3 — Stoichiometry using solution concentration
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-STOICH-03 — Use molar concentration and solution volume to determine amount of dissolved reactant or product.
**Starter / learning task:** Connect molarity to moles per litre. Work through n = cV, checking that volume is expressed in litres.

**Practice**
1. What does a concentration of 0.5 mol L⁻¹ mean?  
   **Answer:** Each litre of solution contains 0.5 mol of solute.
2. How many moles are in 0.20 L of a 0.50 mol L⁻¹ solution?  
   **Answer:** n = cV = 0.50 × 0.20 = 0.10 mol.
3. Why convert millilitres to litres in n = cV when c is in mol L⁻¹?  
   **Answer:** The volume unit must match the concentration unit.

## Lesson 4 — Stoichiometry involving gas volume
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-STOICH-04 — Use appropriate gas-volume relationships and mole ratios under stated conditions.
**Starter / learning task:** Compare gas volumes only after identifying the stated temperature and pressure conditions. Use a supplied data table or ideal-gas relationship as appropriate.

**Practice**
1. Why must gas conditions be stated in a volume calculation?  
   **Answer:** Gas volume depends on temperature and pressure.
2. At the same temperature and pressure, what is the volume ratio for gases in a reaction?  
   **Answer:** It follows the mole ratio in the balanced equation.
3. Which equation relates gas pressure, volume, amount and temperature for an ideal gas?  
   **Answer:** PV = nRT.

## Lesson 5 — Multistep stoichiometry
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-STOICH-05 — Combine balanced equations and mole relationships for reactions occurring in multiple steps.
**Starter / learning task:** Present a two-stage reaction pathway. Have learners identify the intermediate, balance each equation and connect the mole ratios across stages.

**Practice**
1. What is an intermediate in a multistep reaction?  
   **Answer:** A substance produced in one step and consumed in a later step.
2. Why should each step be balanced before combining equations?  
   **Answer:** Balanced equations provide valid mole ratios and conserve atoms.
3. What should happen to an intermediate when adding compatible reaction steps?  
   **Answer:** It cancels if produced and consumed in equal amounts.

## Lesson 6 — Limiting reactants
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-STOICH-06 — Identify the limiting reactant and calculate product formed and excess reactant remaining.
**Starter / learning task:** Use a recipe analogy before moving to a balanced equation. Calculate the product possible from each reactant and identify which runs out first.

**Practice**
1. What is the limiting reactant?  
   **Answer:** The reactant consumed first, which determines the maximum amount of product.
2. If a reaction needs 2 mol A for every 1 mol B, how much A is needed for 3 mol B?  
   **Answer:** 6 mol A.
3. What remains after the limiting reactant is used up?  
   **Answer:** Any unconsumed excess reactant.

## Lesson 7 — Percentage yield
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-STOICH-07 — Calculate theoretical yield and percentage yield from actual experimental yield.
**Starter / learning task:** Distinguish the maximum predicted product from the product actually collected. Model percentage yield with units and sensible rounding.

**Practice**
1. What is theoretical yield?  
   **Answer:** The maximum product amount predicted by stoichiometry.
2. A reaction has a theoretical yield of 10 g and an actual yield of 8 g. What is the percentage yield?  
   **Answer:** (8 ÷ 10) × 100 = 80%.
3. Give one reason actual yield may be lower than theoretical yield.  
   **Answer:** The reaction may be incomplete, side reactions may occur, or product may be lost during separation.

# M5 Chemistry — Acids, Bases, Redox and Electrochemistry Teacher Starter Pack

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017)

**Official strand:** Additional Chemistry strand 2  
**Coverage:** 19 official M5 learning outcomes (14–32); 19 lesson starters; 57 practice questions with answer keys  
**Use:** Teacher planning resource. Use teacher-approved microscale demonstrations, simulations or supplied datasets. Do not improvise chemical mixtures, perform unsupervised titrations or use mains electricity. Follow school laboratory risk assessments, PPE and waste-disposal procedures.

## Lesson 1 — Acid–base theories
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ACID-14 — Identify acids and bases using Arrhenius, Brønsted–Lowry and Lewis definitions.
**Starter / learning task:** Sort familiar substances using the three acid–base theories and explain the limits of each definition. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What does the Arrhenius definition say an acid produces in water?
   **Answer:** It increases the concentration of hydrogen ions, represented in water as hydronium ions.
2. What does a Brønsted–Lowry base do?
   **Answer:** It accepts a proton.
3. What does a Lewis acid accept?
   **Answer:** An electron pair.

## Lesson 2 — Conjugate acid–base pairs
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ACID-15 — Identify conjugate acid–base pairs by tracking proton transfer.
**Starter / learning task:** Track proton transfer in paired reactions and label conjugate species. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What is a conjugate base?
   **Answer:** The species remaining after an acid donates a proton.
2. What is the conjugate acid of NH₃?
   **Answer:** NH₄⁺.
3. What changes between a conjugate acid–base pair?
   **Answer:** They differ by one proton.

## Lesson 3 — Acid and base strength
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ACID-16 — Calculate and compare ionisation or dissociation and interpret acid and base strength.
**Starter / learning task:** Compare supplied Ka, Kb and pKa data; explain what the values imply. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What does Ka measure?
   **Answer:** The equilibrium tendency of an acid to ionise in water.
2. Which is stronger: an acid with Ka = 10⁻² or 10⁻⁵?
   **Answer:** The acid with Ka = 10⁻².
3. What is the relationship between pKa and Ka?
   **Answer:** pKa = −log₁₀ Ka; lower pKa indicates a stronger acid.

## Lesson 4 — pH and ion concentrations
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ACID-17 — Calculate pH, hydronium concentration and hydroxide concentration for acidic and basic solutions.
**Starter / learning task:** Practise pH and concentration calculations using scientific notation and logarithms. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What is pH defined as?
   **Answer:** pH = −log₁₀[H₃O⁺].
2. What is the pH when [H₃O⁺] = 1.0 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹?
   **Answer:** pH = 3.
3. At 25°C, what is pH + pOH for a dilute aqueous solution?
   **Answer:** 14, using Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴.

## Lesson 5 — Neutralisation
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ACID-18 — Write balanced neutralisation equations and determine the acid–base character of the resulting solution.
**Starter / learning task:** Use balanced equations and ion models to distinguish complete neutralisation from the pH of the final salt solution. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What are the usual products of an acid–base neutralisation?
   **Answer:** A salt and water, for typical aqueous acid–base neutralisation.
2. Write the net ionic equation for strong acid–strong base neutralisation.
   **Answer:** H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ → 2H₂O.
3. Does every salt solution have pH 7?
   **Answer:** No; ions can hydrolyse and make the solution acidic or basic.

## Lesson 6 — Salt hydrolysis
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ACID-19 — Write salt-hydrolysis equations and predict whether a salt solution is acidic, basic or neutral.
**Starter / learning task:** Predict salt-solution pH by identifying the acid–base behaviour of each ion. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What is salt hydrolysis?
   **Answer:** Reaction of salt ions with water that can produce hydronium or hydroxide ions.
2. What is the usual character of aqueous NaCl?
   **Answer:** Approximately neutral at 25°C because its ions come from a strong acid and strong base.
3. Why can ammonium chloride solution be acidic?
   **Answer:** NH₄⁺ acts as a weak acid and produces hydronium ions in water.

## Lesson 7 — Acid–base titration
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ACID-20 — Explain titration principles and select a suitable indicator for an acid–base titration.
**Starter / learning task:** Use titration curves and indicator transition ranges to justify indicator selection. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What is the equivalence point in a titration?
   **Answer:** The point where reactants have combined in the stoichiometric amounts specified by the balanced equation.
2. What is the role of an indicator?
   **Answer:** It changes colour over a pH range to signal the titration endpoint.
3. Why should indicator transition range match the steep pH change?
   **Answer:** So the observed endpoint is close to the equivalence point.

## Lesson 8 — Titration calculations
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ACID-21 — Calculate amount or concentration of an acid or base from titration data and reaction stoichiometry.
**Starter / learning task:** Work through a sample titration dataset, showing units, mole ratios and significant figures. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. A 25.0 mL monoprotic acid sample requires 20.0 mL of 0.10 M NaOH. What is the acid concentration?
   **Answer:** Moles NaOH = 0.00200 mol; 1:1 ratio gives 0.00200 mol acid; concentration = 0.00200/0.0250 = 0.0800 M.
2. What information is needed to calculate concentration from titration?
   **Answer:** Balanced reaction stoichiometry, titrant concentration and volume, and analyte volume.
3. Why must the burette reading be recorded carefully?
   **Answer:** A small volume error affects the calculated amount and concentration.

## Lesson 9 — Buffer solutions
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ACID-22 — Describe buffer components, resistance to pH change and common applications.
**Starter / learning task:** Model how a weak acid/conjugate base pair responds to small acid or base additions. Ask learners to show working, use correct symbols and units, and explain their reasoning.

**Practice**
1. What are the components of an acidic buffer?
   **Answer:** A weak acid and its conjugate base, usually supplied as a salt.
2. How does a buffer respond to a small addition of acid?
   **Answer:** Its conjugate base consumes much of the added hydronium.
3. Give one biological buffer example.
   **Answer:** The carbonic acid–bicarbonate system in blood.

## Lesson 10 — Applications of acid–base chemistry
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-ACID-23 — Research and present examples of using acid–base knowledge to solve problems.

   **Answer:** The volume doubles, provided the amount of gas remains constant.

   **Answer:** P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ for a fixed amount of gas.

   **Answer:** 1.0 L, because pressure doubles while volume halves.

**Practice**

   **Answer:** At constant temperature and pressure, equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of particles; volume is proportional to amount in moles.

   **Answer:** PV = nRT.

   **Answer:** Use a consistent gas constant and units for pressure, volume and temperature.

   **Answer:** 100 kPa.
3. What is a mole fraction?
   **Answer:** The moles of one component divided by the total moles in the mixture.

**Starter / learning task:** Compare linear, branched and cross-linked polymer models and relate structure to material behaviour. Ask learners to justify classifications and predictions using molecular structures, balanced equations or supplied evidence.

**Practice**
1. How can cross-linking affect a polymer?
   **Answer:** It can increase stiffness, strength or elasticity and reduce chain mobility, depending on degree and chemistry.
2. Why can branching change polymer density?
   **Answer:** Branching can reduce how closely polymer chains pack together.
3. How does polymer structure influence use?
   **Answer:** It affects properties such as flexibility, strength, thermal behaviour and chemical resistance.

## Lesson 13 — Plastics and rubber
**Objective:** M6-CHEM-POLY-13 — Classify common plastic and rubber products from labelled samples, recycling codes or supplied property data.
**Starter / learning task:** Classify common plastic and rubber products from labelled samples, recycling codes or supplied property data. Ask learners to justify classifications and predictions using molecular structures, balanced equations or supplied evidence.

**Practice**
1. What does thermoplastic mean?
   **Answer:** A polymer that softens when heated and can generally be reshaped repeatedly without major chemical change.
2. What is an elastomer?
   **Answer:** A polymer that can undergo substantial reversible deformation.
3. Why are material tests useful?
   **Answer:** They provide evidence about properties that help identify suitable applications.

## Lesson 14 — Polymer modification
**Objective:** M6-CHEM-POLY-14 — Investigate examples of polymer blending, cross-linking, additives or changes in polymerisation conditions using data.
**Starter / learning task:** Investigate examples of polymer blending, cross-linking, additives or changes in polymerisation conditions using data. Ask learners to justify classifications and predictions using molecular structures, balanced equations or supplied evidence.

**Practice**
1. How can a plasticiser change a polymer?
   **Answer:** It can increase chain mobility and flexibility.
2. What can vulcanisation do to rubber?
   **Answer:** It creates cross-links that improve elasticity, strength and resistance to deformation.
3. Why must additives be evaluated?
   **Answer:** They may change performance, safety, recyclability and environmental impact.

## Lesson 15 — Environmental impacts of polymers
**Objective:** M6-CHEM-POLY-15 — Evaluate a local polymer-waste case and propose prevention, reuse, recycling or safer design strategies.
**Starter / learning task:** Evaluate a local polymer-waste case and propose prevention, reuse, recycling or safer design strategies. Ask learners to justify classifications and predictions using molecular structures, balanced equations or supplied evidence.

**Practice**
1. Why can plastic waste persist in the environment?
   **Answer:** Many common polymers degrade slowly under environmental conditions.
2. What is one limitation of recycling?
   **Answer:** Collection, sorting, contamination and material degradation can reduce the amount that can be recycled effectively.
3. Name one way to reduce polymer waste at source.
   **Answer:** Choose reusable products and avoid unnecessary single-use packaging.
