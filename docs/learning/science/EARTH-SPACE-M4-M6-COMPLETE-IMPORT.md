# SOURCE PACK 1: M4-earth-space-geology-teacher-starter-pack.md

# M4 Earth, Astronomy and Space Science — Geology Teacher Starter Pack

**Official alignment:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017), Additional Earth, Astronomy and Space Science, M4 standard 1, outcomes 1–12. The IPST curriculum-use manual places all twelve outcomes in the M4 section; the teaching analysis for outcome 12 explicitly labels it M4 (printed curriculum pp. 17–20; teacher analysis pp. 80–92).

Each official outcome has one lesson starter and three answer-keyed practice questions.

## Outcome 1 — Earth’s Internal Structure

**Objective:** Explain the layers and properties of Earth’s internal structure using evidence such as rock and meteorite composition and seismic-wave behaviour.

**Starter:** Ask how scientists can investigate Earth’s interior when they cannot drill to its centre.

**Teach:** Chemical layers (crust, mantle, core); mechanical layers (lithosphere, asthenosphere, deeper mantle, outer and inner core); seismic P- and S-wave behaviour; density and compositional evidence; discontinuities such as the Moho and core boundaries.

**Questions**
1. What are Earth’s three main compositional layers? **Answer:** Crust, mantle and core.
2. Which seismic waves cannot travel through the liquid outer core? **Answer:** S-waves.
3. What evidence helps scientists infer Earth’s internal structure? **Answer:** Seismic-wave travel and behaviour, together with rock, meteorite and density/composition evidence.

## Outcome 2 — Geological Evidence for Plate Movement

**Objective:** Explain geological evidence supporting the movement of tectonic plates, including matching rock belts, mountain ranges, fossils, glacial traces, ocean-floor ages, palaeomagnetism and mid-ocean ridges.

**Starter:** Display maps showing the east coast of South America and west coast of Africa. Ask what evidence could test whether the continents were once joined.

**Teach:** Continental fit is one clue, not proof by itself; matching fossils and rock/mountain belts; ancient glacial evidence; seafloor age patterns; symmetrical magnetic stripes; mid-ocean ridges and seafloor spreading.

**Questions**
1. What does matching fossil evidence on continents now separated by oceans suggest? **Answer:** Those landmasses were once connected or had a different relative position.
2. What do symmetrical magnetic stripes on either side of a mid-ocean ridge support? **Answer:** Seafloor spreading.
3. Why is several independent lines of evidence stronger than coastline fit alone? **Answer:** Different evidence types independently support the same explanation for plate movement.

## Outcome 3 — Plate Boundaries and Geological Features

**Objective:** Identify causes and explain types of plate-boundary movement and the landforms and geological structures associated with convergent, divergent and transform boundaries.

**Starter:** Use two books or cards to model plates moving apart, colliding and sliding past each other. Ask what landforms might form.

**Teach:** Plate motion and mantle dynamics; divergent boundaries and new crust; convergent boundaries, subduction, trenches, volcanic arcs and mountain building; transform boundaries and strike-slip faults; link boundary type to earthquake and volcanic patterns.

**Questions**
1. What commonly forms where an oceanic plate subducts beneath another plate? **Answer:** A deep-ocean trench and a volcanic arc, with frequent earthquakes.
2. What happens at a divergent plate boundary? **Answer:** Plates move apart and new crust can form as magma rises and solidifies.
3. Why can transform boundaries produce earthquakes? **Answer:** Plates slide past each other; stress builds along faults and is released suddenly.

## Outcome 4 — Reconstructing Past Geological Events

**Objective:** Analyse present-day geological evidence and explain the sequence of past geological events using stratigraphic principles, relative and absolute dating, and the geologic time scale.

**Starter:** Show a simple stack of sedimentary layers with a fault cutting across them. Ask students which event happened first and how they know.

**Teach:** Superposition, original horizontality and cross-cutting relationships; fossils and correlation; relative dating versus radiometric/absolute dating; geologic time scale; reconstruct event sequences from field evidence.

**Questions**
1. In an undisturbed sequence of sedimentary rocks, which layer is generally oldest? **Answer:** The bottom layer.
2. If a fault cuts through several rock layers, did the fault form before or after those layers? **Answer:** After them, because the fault cuts across them.
3. What is the difference between relative and absolute dating? **Answer:** Relative dating orders events; absolute dating estimates a numerical age.

## Outcome 5 — Volcanic Eruptions and Safety

**Objective:** Explain the causes and processes of volcanic eruptions, factors affecting eruption intensity and volcano shape, and use hazard information to propose monitoring and safety practices.

**Starter:** Compare images of a broad shield volcano and a steep stratovolcano. Ask why their shapes and eruption styles can differ.

**Teach:** Magma generation and ascent; gas content, silica content and viscosity; explosive versus effusive eruptions; ash, lava, pyroclastic flows and lahars; hazard maps, monitoring and official evacuation advice.

**Questions**
1. How does high-viscosity magma generally affect escaping volcanic gases? **Answer:** It traps gases more readily and can contribute to explosive eruptions.
2. Name one major volcanic hazard besides lava flows. **Answer:** Ash fall, pyroclastic flows or lahars.
3. What should people do when authorities issue a volcanic-hazard evacuation order? **Answer:** Follow official instructions and move to the designated safe area.

## Outcome 6 — Earthquakes and Safety

**Objective:** Explain earthquake causes, processes, magnitude, intensity and effects, identify hazard-risk areas, and propose monitoring and personal safety practices.

**Starter:** Ask why an earthquake can be felt strongly in one location but weakly in another.

**Teach:** Stress accumulation and sudden fault slip; focus and epicentre; magnitude measures event size while intensity describes observed effects at a location; depth, distance, ground conditions and building design affect impacts; hazard maps and preparedness.

**Questions**
1. What is the earthquake focus? **Answer:** The point inside Earth where rupture begins.
2. How does magnitude differ from intensity? **Answer:** Magnitude describes the size/energy of the earthquake; intensity describes shaking and effects at a particular place.
3. What is a recommended action during strong indoor shaking? **Answer:** Drop, cover and hold on, staying away from windows.

## Outcome 7 — Tsunamis and Safety

**Objective:** Explain tsunami causes, generation processes and impacts, identify areas at risk, and propose warning, preparedness and safe-response practices.

**Starter:** Show a cross-section of a tsunami in deep ocean and near shore. Ask why the wave can become much taller near land.

**Teach:** Sudden displacement of a large water volume, commonly by undersea earthquakes but also landslides or volcanic activity; long wavelength and high speed in deep water; shoaling near shore; warning signs and evacuation routes.

**Questions**
1. What is a common cause of a large tsunami? **Answer:** A major undersea earthquake that suddenly displaces the seafloor and water column.
2. Why can tsunami waves become much higher near a coast? **Answer:** As water becomes shallower, wave speed and wavelength decrease and wave height can increase.
3. What should someone do after strong or long coastal earthquake shaking if no official warning has yet arrived? **Answer:** Move immediately to higher ground or inland, following local evacuation guidance.

## Outcome 8 — Identifying Minerals and Their Uses

**Objective:** Examine and identify minerals using observable physical properties and appropriate tests, analyse their properties, and propose suitable and sustainable uses of mineral resources.

**Starter:** Show quartz, glass and calcite samples or photographs. Ask how to tell a naturally occurring mineral from a manufactured material.

**Teach:** Mineral definition; hardness, lustre, streak, cleavage, fracture, density and reaction to dilute acid; use a Mohs hardness reference; connect properties to applications; use safe teacher-prepared samples and avoid tasting or inhaling powders.

**Questions**
1. Which property is compared using the Mohs scale? **Answer:** Hardness (resistance to scratching).
2. Why can streak be more useful than surface colour for identifying some minerals? **Answer:** Streak is the colour of the mineral’s powder and is often more consistent than surface colour.
3. Why should mineral resources be used responsibly? **Answer:** Deposits are finite and extraction can affect ecosystems, water and communities.

## Outcome 9 — Classifying Rocks and Their Uses

**Objective:** Examine, classify and name rocks using formation processes, texture and mineral composition; distinguish igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic rocks and explain suitable uses.

**Starter:** Display granite, sandstone and marble. Ask students to group them and explain what evidence they used.

**Teach:** Igneous rocks form from cooled magma/lava; sedimentary rocks form through deposition, compaction/cementation or chemical/biological processes; metamorphic rocks change under heat, pressure and fluids without melting; texture and mineral composition support identification.

**Questions**
1. Which rock type forms when magma or lava cools and solidifies? **Answer:** Igneous rock.
2. What process commonly turns loose sediment into sedimentary rock? **Answer:** Compaction and cementation (lithification).
3. Is marble igneous, sedimentary or metamorphic? **Answer:** Metamorphic; it forms when limestone is changed by heat and pressure.

## Outcome 10 — Petroleum and Coal Formation and Exploration

**Objective:** Explain how petroleum and coal form and how geological information is used to investigate and explore their deposits.

**Starter:** Ask students where fuels such as petrol, diesel and natural gas originate and what geological evidence helps locate them.

**Teach:** Organic matter burial and transformation over geological time; coal formation from ancient plant material; petroleum generation from organic-rich sedimentary rocks; source, reservoir and cap rocks; traps, seismic surveys and geological mapping; finite resources and environmental impacts.

**Questions**
1. What type of source material commonly forms coal? **Answer:** Accumulated ancient plant material buried and transformed over geological time.
2. What is the role of a cap rock in a petroleum trap? **Answer:** It is a low-permeability layer that helps prevent hydrocarbons from escaping the reservoir.
3. Why are seismic surveys useful in petroleum exploration? **Answer:** They help image underground rock layers and structures that may contain traps.

## Outcome 11 — Products from Petroleum and Coal

**Objective:** Describe properties of products derived from petroleum and coal and present appropriate, efficient and responsible uses.

**Starter:** Show product labels for plastic packaging, asphalt, lubricants and fuels. Ask which materials may come from petroleum or coal processing.

**Teach:** Petroleum refining separates fractions by boiling range; fuels and petrochemical feedstocks; polymers, lubricants and bitumen; coal products and industrial uses; compare properties with intended uses; discuss pollution, efficiency, recycling and alternatives.

**Questions**
1. What property is used to separate many petroleum fractions during fractional distillation? **Answer:** Differences in boiling points.
2. Name one non-fuel product made from petroleum-derived feedstocks. **Answer:** Plastics/polymers, synthetic fibres, lubricants or bitumen.
3. Give one responsible practice that reduces impacts from petroleum-derived products. **Answer:** Reduce unnecessary use, reuse/recycle suitable materials, improve efficiency or choose lower-impact alternatives.

## Outcome 12 — Topographic and Geological Map Interpretation

**Objective:** Read and interpret topographic and geological maps for a specified area and explain applications such as resource assessment, land-use planning and infrastructure siting.

**Starter:** Display a topographic map with contour lines and a geological map with coloured rock units. Ask what each map can tell a town planner.

**Teach:** Map title, scale, orientation, legend and coordinates; contour intervals and elevation; contour spacing and slope; geological units, contacts, strike/dip and structural symbols; combine both maps to assess terrain, geology, resources and site suitability.

**Questions**
1. What do closely spaced contour lines usually indicate? **Answer:** A steep slope.
2. What information does a geological map show that a topographic map may not? **Answer:** The distribution and boundaries of rock units and geological structures.
3. Give one practical use of combining topographic and geological maps. **Answer:** Choosing safer locations for roads, buildings, reservoirs or resource exploration.

## Teacher safety and accuracy notes

- Use photographs, maps, prepared samples and simulations where practical work presents avoidable risk.
- Do not conduct acid tests on minerals without approved teacher supervision, suitable dilute reagents, PPE and school laboratory controls.
- Earthquake, tsunami and volcanic-hazard lessons must prioritise official monitoring agencies and evacuation guidance; never ask students to approach an active hazard.
- Avoid implying that any one map or geological indicator guarantees a safe construction site; professional site investigation is required.
- Discuss fossil fuels as finite resources and include environmental and sustainability considerations.

# SOURCE PACK 2: M5-earth-space-weather-atmosphere-oceans-climate-teacher-starter-pack.md

# M5 Earth, Astronomy and Space Science — Weather, Atmosphere, Oceans and Climate

**Official alignment:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017), Additional Earth, Astronomy and Space Science, M5 standard 2, outcomes 1–13. The official IPST curriculum-use manual lists these outcomes across printed pages 21–24 and provides teacher analysis and activities in the M5 section.

Each outcome below includes a lesson starter and three answer-keyed practice questions.

## Outcome 1 — Uneven Solar Energy and Regional Air Temperature

**Objective:** Explain factors causing differences in solar energy received and emitted across Earth and relate them to regional air-temperature differences.

**Starter:** Compare the angle of sunlight at the equator and at higher latitudes. Ask why the same beam of sunlight heats different surface areas.

**Teach:** Latitude, Earth's spherical shape and axial tilt; surface albedo; clouds, aerosols and greenhouse gases; absorption, reflection and emission.

**Questions**
1. Why does sunlight generally heat the equator more directly than high latitudes? **Answer:** Sunlight strikes at a higher angle and its energy is concentrated over a smaller surface area.
2. Name two factors besides latitude that affect local heating. **Answer:** Surface type/albedo and cloud cover; greenhouse gases and aerosols also affect energy transfer.
3. What happens when the same incoming solar energy is spread over a larger surface area? **Answer:** Energy received per unit area decreases.

## Outcome 2 — Earth’s Energy Balance

**Objective:** Explain the processes that establish Earth’s energy balance, including incoming solar radiation, reflection, absorption, transfer and outgoing radiation.

**Starter:** Ask students what might happen to Earth's average temperature if Earth absorbed more energy than it emitted for many years.

**Teach:** Incoming shortwave solar radiation, reflection (albedo), absorption by atmosphere and surface, transfer of energy, outgoing longwave radiation and the greenhouse effect.

**Questions**
1. What is Earth's energy balance? **Answer:** Over a long-term average, incoming energy is balanced by energy returned to space.
2. What is albedo? **Answer:** The fraction of incoming radiation reflected by a surface or system.
3. If Earth absorbs more energy than it emits, what happens to its energy content? **Answer:** It increases, producing a warming tendency.

## Outcome 3 — Forces Driving Atmospheric Circulation

**Objective:** Explain how pressure-gradient force, Coriolis force, centripetal force and friction affect atmospheric circulation and wind.

**Starter:** Use a simple pressure map with high- and low-pressure centres. Ask students which way air initially moves.

**Teach:** Pressure-gradient force drives air from high toward low pressure; Coriolis deflects moving air due to Earth's rotation; friction slows near-surface wind; curved flow involves centripetal acceleration.

**Questions**
1. In which general direction does the pressure-gradient force act? **Answer:** From higher pressure toward lower pressure.
2. How does the Coriolis effect deflect moving air in the Northern Hemisphere? **Answer:** To the right of its direction of motion.
3. Why is surface wind usually slower than wind higher above the ground? **Answer:** Friction with the surface and obstacles slows it.

## Outcome 4 — Latitudinal Atmospheric Circulation

**Objective:** Explain atmospheric circulation by latitude and relate global circulation cells to prevailing winds and climate patterns.

**Starter:** Display a world map with the equator and 30°, 60° and 90° latitude lines. Ask why wet and dry climate belts occur in broad bands.

**Teach:** Hadley, Ferrel and Polar cells; rising air near the equator and around subpolar zones; sinking air in subtropical and polar high-pressure belts; trade winds and westerlies.

**Questions**
1. What is the name of the circulation cell between the equator and about 30° latitude? **Answer:** Hadley cell.
2. Why are many subtropical regions relatively dry? **Answer:** Air commonly sinks there, warms and becomes less favourable for cloud formation and rainfall.
3. What are trade winds? **Answer:** Persistent tropical easterly winds associated with the Hadley circulation.

## Outcome 5 — Ocean-Water Stratification

**Objective:** Explain factors that produce ocean-water layers, including differences in temperature, salinity and density.

**Starter:** Show a diagram of a warm surface layer above colder deep ocean water. Ask why the layers do not mix quickly.

**Teach:** Temperature and salinity influence density; surface mixed layer, thermocline and deep layer; stratification can inhibit vertical mixing.

**Questions**
1. Which is generally denser: colder seawater or warmer seawater of the same salinity? **Answer:** Colder seawater.
2. What is a thermocline? **Answer:** A layer where water temperature changes rapidly with depth.
3. How can increased salinity affect seawater density? **Answer:** It generally increases density.

## Outcome 6 — Ocean Circulation and Its Patterns

**Objective:** Explain factors driving surface and deep-ocean circulation and describe major patterns of ocean-water movement.

**Starter:** Display a global ocean-current map. Ask why currents do not all flow in the same direction.

**Teach:** Surface currents are driven mainly by winds and shaped by Coriolis effect and continents; deep circulation is influenced by density differences from temperature and salinity; connect surface and deep circulation.

**Questions**
1. What is the main driver of many surface ocean currents? **Answer:** Prevailing winds.
2. What drives thermohaline circulation? **Answer:** Density differences caused mainly by temperature and salinity.
3. What is one way continents affect ocean currents? **Answer:** They redirect currents and help shape ocean gyres.

## Outcome 7 — Effects of Ocean Circulation

**Objective:** Explain how ocean circulation affects weather and climate, marine organisms and environmental conditions.

**Starter:** Compare coastal climate near a warm current with a coast influenced by a cold current.

**Teach:** Heat transport, coastal temperature and rainfall patterns, nutrient upwelling, marine productivity, and climate variability including El Niño and La Niña.

**Questions**
1. How can a warm ocean current affect nearby coastal air? **Answer:** It can warm and moisten the air, influencing local weather and climate.
2. Why can upwelling support productive fisheries? **Answer:** It brings nutrient-rich deeper water toward the surface, supporting plankton and food webs.
3. El Niño is associated with changes in which ocean-atmosphere system? **Answer:** The tropical Pacific Ocean and its coupled atmospheric circulation.

## Outcome 8 — Atmospheric Stability and Cloud Formation

**Objective:** Explain the relationship between atmospheric stability, vertical air movement and cloud formation.

**Starter:** Show a cumulonimbus cloud and a clear-sky image. Ask what conditions help air rise to form tall clouds.

**Teach:** Compare the temperature of a rising air parcel with surrounding air; stable air resists vertical movement, while unstable air supports rising motion; cooling to dew point leads to condensation and cloud formation.

**Questions**
1. What happens to an air parcel that is colder and denser than its surroundings? **Answer:** It tends to sink and the atmosphere is stable with respect to that parcel.
2. What happens to a warm, buoyant air parcel in an unstable atmosphere? **Answer:** It continues to rise.
3. What process forms cloud droplets when rising air cools to saturation? **Answer:** Water vapour condenses onto cloud condensation nuclei.

## Outcome 9 — Weather Fronts and Associated Conditions

**Objective:** Explain how warm, cold, occluded and stationary fronts form and relate each front type to associated cloud and weather conditions.

**Starter:** Show a weather map with a cold-front symbol and ask what happens when two air masses meet.

**Teach:** A front is a boundary between air masses with different properties. Compare warm, cold, stationary and occluded fronts, their slopes, cloud patterns and typical precipitation.

**Questions**
1. What is a cold front? **Answer:** The boundary where advancing colder, denser air pushes beneath warmer air.
2. Which front commonly produces widespread layered cloud and steady precipitation ahead of it? **Answer:** A warm front.
3. What happens at an occluded front? **Answer:** A cold front overtakes a warm front, lifting the warm air from the surface.

## Outcome 10 — Drivers of Global Climate Change

**Objective:** Explain natural and human-related factors that affect global climate, using evidence such as ice-core records and observed climate data.

**Starter:** Compare a graph of long-term global temperature with atmospheric carbon-dioxide measurements. Ask what evidence can establish a climate trend.

**Teach:** Natural drivers include orbital variations, volcanic aerosols and solar variability; human drivers include greenhouse-gas emissions, land-use change and aerosols. Evaluate evidence from ice cores, instrumental records and other proxies.

**Questions**
1. Name one major human influence on recent global warming. **Answer:** Increased greenhouse-gas concentrations from fossil-fuel use and land-use change.
2. What can ice cores tell scientists about past climate? **Answer:** They preserve indicators such as trapped ancient air and isotopic signals that help reconstruct past atmospheric composition and temperature.
3. Is one unusually cold day evidence against long-term global warming? **Answer:** No. Weather varies day to day; climate trends are assessed over longer periods.

## Outcome 11 — Climate-Change Impacts and Mitigation

**Objective:** Analyse and discuss events and impacts associated with global climate change and present human practices that can help slow climate change.

**Starter:** Ask students to identify possible local impacts of rising temperatures, changing rainfall and sea-level rise.

**Teach:** Analyse observed and projected impacts on ecosystems, water, food systems, health and coastal communities. Distinguish mitigation (reducing causes) from adaptation (reducing harm).

**Questions**
1. Give one possible impact of rising global temperature on coastal communities. **Answer:** Sea-level rise can increase coastal flooding and erosion.
2. Is planting trees mitigation or adaptation? **Answer:** It can contribute to mitigation by storing carbon, depending on species, location and long-term management.
3. Give one example of climate adaptation. **Answer:** Improving flood warning systems or designing infrastructure for heat and heavy rainfall.

## Outcome 12 — Interpreting Weather-Map Symbols

**Objective:** Interpret standard weather-station symbols and map features, including pressure, temperature, humidity, wind, cloud and precipitation information.

**Starter:** Display a station model and ask students to identify wind direction, cloud cover and temperature.

**Teach:** Read station plots and isobars; identify high- and low-pressure systems, fronts, wind barbs, cloud cover and precipitation symbols; check map legend and time.

**Questions**
1. What do closely spaced isobars usually indicate? **Answer:** A stronger pressure gradient and generally stronger winds.
2. What does a wind barb show? **Answer:** Wind direction and speed.
3. Why must the time of observation be checked when interpreting a weather map? **Answer:** Weather systems move and conditions change over time.

## Outcome 13 — Analysing and Forecasting Weather from Data

**Objective:** Use weather maps and supporting information such as forecasts, radar and satellite observations to analyse and make basic weather predictions for practical planning.

**Starter:** Give students a simple forecast, radar image and satellite image. Ask what each source contributes.

**Teach:** Combine station observations, pressure patterns, fronts, radar precipitation and satellite cloud information; recognise uncertainty and avoid treating a forecast as a guarantee.

**Questions**
1. What does weather radar help identify? **Answer:** The location and movement of precipitation.
2. What can satellite imagery show that a single weather station cannot? **Answer:** Large-scale cloud patterns and the development and movement of weather systems.
3. Why should forecasts be updated before planning an outdoor event? **Answer:** Forecasts change as new observations and model information become available.

## Teacher safety and assessment notes

Use official weather data, maps, simulations and safe classroom demonstrations. Do not ask students to approach storms, climb exposed locations or observe lightning outdoors. Climate-change tasks should use traceable datasets and distinguish evidence, uncertainty, weather variability, long-term climate trends, mitigation and adaptation. Assess interpretation of data and reasoning, not memorisation of unsupported local forecasts.

# M6 Earth, Astronomy and Space Science — Astronomy and Space Teacher Starter Pack

**Official alignment:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017), Additional Earth, Astronomy and Space Science, M6 standard 3, outcomes 1–19. The IPST curriculum-use manual presents this M6 sequence on printed pages 25–30 and cross-references it on pages 31–32.

Each official outcome has a lesson starter and three answer-keyed practice questions.

## Outcome 1 — Evolution of the Universe after the Big Bang

**Objective:** Explain changes in the energy, matter, size and temperature of the universe at different stages after the Big Bang.

**Starter:** Show a simple timeline from the early universe to galaxies today. Ask students what evidence suggests the universe has changed over time.

**Teach:** Expansion and cooling; early particles and atoms; formation of structures, stars and galaxies; distinguish the scientific model from unsupported claims.

**Questions**
1. What does the Big Bang model describe? **Answer:** The expansion and evolution of the universe from an extremely hot, dense early state.
2. As the universe expands, what generally happens to its average temperature? **Answer:** It decreases.
3. Name one large-scale structure that formed later in cosmic history. **Answer:** Galaxies.

## Outcome 2 — Evidence Supporting the Big Bang

**Objective:** Explain evidence supporting the Big Bang model, including the relationship between galaxy recession velocity and distance and the cosmic microwave background.

**Starter:** Present a simplified graph of galaxy distance against recession velocity and ask students to describe the pattern.

**Teach:** Hubble–Lemaître relationship; expanding universe; cosmic microwave background (CMB) as relic radiation; evidence supports a model rather than a direct observation of the initial event.

**Questions**
1. What relationship is observed between the distance to many galaxies and their recession velocity? **Answer:** More distant galaxies generally have greater recession velocities.
2. What is the cosmic microwave background? **Answer:** Relic electromagnetic radiation from the early universe, now observed mainly in the microwave region.
3. Why is the CMB important evidence? **Answer:** Its near-uniform distribution and spectrum are consistent with predictions of the hot early-universe model.

## Outcome 3 — The Milky Way and the Solar System’s Location

**Objective:** Explain the structure and components of the Milky Way and locate the Solar System within it, linking this position to the Milky Way appearance observed from Earth.

**Starter:** Show a dark-sky Milky Way photograph. Ask why it appears as a pale band rather than a complete galaxy shape.

**Teach:** Barred spiral galaxy; central bulge, disk, spiral arms and halo; interstellar gas, dust and stars; Solar System in the disk, away from the galactic centre; viewing the dense disk edge-on.

**Questions**
1. What type of galaxy is the Milky Way? **Answer:** A barred spiral galaxy.
2. Where is the Solar System located? **Answer:** In the Milky Way’s disk, in the Orion Arm/Local Spur region, far from the galactic centre.
3. Why does the Milky Way appear as a band across a dark sky? **Answer:** We are viewing the dense disk of stars from within the galaxy.

## Outcome 4 — Formation of Stars

**Objective:** Explain star formation by describing changes in pressure, temperature and size as a protostar develops into a star.

**Starter:** Show a sequence of a nebula, collapsing cloud and protostar. Ask what causes a cloud of gas and dust to contract.

**Teach:** Gravitational collapse of a dense region in a molecular cloud; contraction increases central pressure and temperature; a protostar forms; sustained hydrogen fusion marks the start of the main sequence.

**Questions**
1. What force drives the collapse of a star-forming cloud? **Answer:** Gravity.
2. What happens to the central temperature as a protostar contracts? **Answer:** It increases.
3. What process begins when the core becomes hot and dense enough in a Sun-like star? **Answer:** Sustained hydrogen nuclear fusion.

## Outcome 5 — Energy Production in Stars

**Objective:** Explain stellar energy production and its effects by analysing the proton–proton chain and carbon–nitrogen–oxygen cycle.

**Starter:** Ask students how the Sun can release energy for billions of years without burning like wood or coal.

**Teach:** Nuclear fusion in stellar cores; proton–proton chain in Sun-like stars; CNO cycle more important in hotter, more massive stars; mass converted to energy; hydrostatic balance.

**Questions**
1. What is the main energy source of a main-sequence star? **Answer:** Nuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium.
2. Which fusion pathway is especially important in Sun-like stars? **Answer:** The proton–proton chain.
3. Which pathway becomes especially important in hotter, more massive main-sequence stars? **Answer:** The carbon–nitrogen–oxygen (CNO) cycle.

## Outcome 6 — Stellar Luminosity and Magnitude

**Objective:** Identify factors affecting stellar luminosity and explain the relationship between luminosity and stellar magnitude.

**Starter:** Compare two stars in images: one appears bright and one dim. Ask whether apparent brightness alone tells us which star emits more energy.

**Teach:** Luminosity is total energy emitted per unit time; radius and surface temperature affect luminosity; apparent magnitude depends on distance and luminosity; magnitude scale is inverse.

**Questions**
1. What does luminosity measure? **Answer:** The total energy a star emits per unit time.
2. Name two properties that affect a star’s luminosity. **Answer:** Radius and surface temperature.
3. On the astronomical magnitude scale, which is brighter: magnitude 1 or magnitude 5? **Answer:** Magnitude 1.

## Outcome 7 — Stellar Colour, Surface Temperature and Spectrum

**Objective:** Explain the relationships among a star’s colour, surface temperature and spectrum.

**Starter:** Show blue, white, yellow and red stars. Ask students whether colour can provide information about temperature.

**Teach:** Approximate blackbody radiation; hotter stars appear bluer and cooler stars redder; spectral lines reveal chemical composition, temperature and other physical properties.

**Questions**
1. Which is generally hotter: a blue star or a red star? **Answer:** A blue star.
2. What can a star’s spectrum reveal besides colour? **Answer:** Chemical composition and information about temperature and motion.
3. Why can astronomers use a spectrum rather than colour alone? **Answer:** Spectral features provide more detailed evidence about a star’s physical properties.

## Outcome 8 — Stellar Distance by Parallax

**Objective:** Explain the parallax method for measuring stellar distance and calculate distance from a measured parallax angle.

**Starter:** Ask students to hold a finger at arm’s length and alternately close each eye. Discuss how the finger appears to shift against the background.

**Teach:** Annual stellar parallax due to Earth's orbit; smaller parallax means greater distance; parsec relationship (d=1/p), where distance is in parsecs and parallax is in arcseconds.

**Questions**
1. What causes annual stellar parallax? **Answer:** Earth's changing position as it orbits the Sun.
2. A star has a parallax of 0.5 arcseconds. What is its distance? **Answer:** 2 parsecs.
3. If a star’s parallax angle becomes smaller, what happens to its distance? **Answer:** Its distance is greater.

## Outcome 9 — Stellar Evolution and the Hertzsprung–Russell Diagram

**Objective:** Explain stellar evolutionary stages in relation to initial mass and analyse changes in stellar properties using the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram.

**Starter:** Show a Hertzsprung–Russell diagram and ask students why stars occupy different regions.

**Teach:** Main sequence, red giant/supergiant, white dwarf and massive-star endpoints; initial mass controls evolutionary pathway; interpret axes, luminosity and temperature.

**Questions**
1. What is plotted on a Hertzsprung–Russell diagram? **Answer:** Stellar luminosity (or absolute magnitude) against surface temperature or spectral class.
2. How does initial mass affect stellar evolution? **Answer:** It strongly determines the star’s lifetime, evolutionary path and final remnant.
3. What can a Sun-like star become after the red-giant stage? **Answer:** It sheds outer layers and leaves a white dwarf.

## Outcome 10 — Solar System Formation and Habitable Planetary Zones

**Objective:** Explain the formation of the Solar System, classify the regions and populations orbiting the Sun, and describe planetary characteristics that can support life.

**Starter:** Show a Solar System diagram and ask why rocky planets are near the Sun while gas and ice giants are farther away.

**Teach:** Solar nebula model; accretion; inner rocky planets, asteroid belt, outer giant planets and small-body populations; habitable zone is about conditions allowing liquid water, not proof of life.

**Questions**
1. What is the leading model for Solar System formation? **Answer:** The solar nebula model.
2. Why are the inner planets mainly rocky? **Answer:** High temperatures near the young Sun favoured the formation and retention of rocky and metallic materials.
3. Does being in the habitable zone guarantee a planet has life? **Answer:** No. It only indicates that conditions may allow liquid water under suitable atmospheric and surface conditions.

## Outcome 11 — Kepler’s Laws and Newtonian Gravity

**Objective:** Explain planetary motion using Kepler’s laws and Newton’s law of gravitation, and calculate planetary orbital periods.

**Starter:** Compare the orbital paths of two planets. Ask why a planet closer to the Sun usually completes an orbit more quickly.

**Teach:** Elliptical orbits; equal areas in equal times; relationship between orbital period and semi-major axis; Newtonian gravitational force; use consistent units and state assumptions.

**Questions**
1. What shape are idealised planetary orbits around the Sun? **Answer:** Ellipses, with the Sun at one focus.
2. Which planet has the shorter orbital period: one closer to the Sun or one farther away? **Answer:** The closer planet.
3. For planets orbiting the same star, what relationship does Kepler’s third law describe? **Answer:** The square of orbital period is proportional to the cube of the orbit’s semi-major axis.

## Outcome 12 — The Sun, Solar Wind and Solar Storms

**Objective:** Explain the Sun’s structure, the origin of solar wind and solar storms, and analyse and present their effects on Earth, including Thailand.

**Starter:** Show an aurora photograph and a diagram of the Sun’s layers. Ask how activity on the Sun can affect technology on Earth.

**Teach:** Core, radiative and convective zones; photosphere, chromosphere and corona; solar wind, flares and coronal mass ejections; geomagnetic disturbances, satellites, communications and power grids.

**Questions**
1. From which outer atmospheric region does much of the solar wind originate? **Answer:** The corona.
2. What is one possible effect of a strong geomagnetic storm? **Answer:** It can disrupt satellite operations, radio communication, navigation or power systems.
3. Can auroras occur at latitudes beyond the polar regions during strong geomagnetic activity? **Answer:** Yes, although visibility depends on storm strength and location.

## Outcome 13 — Celestial Sphere and Astronomical Coordinates

**Objective:** Construct a celestial-sphere model, relate its key points and lines to the real sky, and explain locating stars using horizontal and equatorial coordinate systems.

**Starter:** Ask students how they would describe a star’s position so that another observer could find it.

**Teach:** Celestial sphere as a model; horizon, zenith, celestial poles and equator; altitude and azimuth in the horizontal system; right ascension and declination in the equatorial system.

**Questions**
1. What two coordinates are used in the horizontal coordinate system? **Answer:** Altitude and azimuth.
2. What two coordinates are used in the equatorial system? **Answer:** Right ascension and declination.
3. Why is the celestial sphere called a model? **Answer:** It represents the apparent positions of celestial objects on an imaginary sphere surrounding the observer.

## Outcome 14 — Apparent Rising and Setting Paths

**Objective:** Observe the sky and explain the apparent rising and setting paths of the Sun and stars.

**Starter:** Ask students why the Sun appears to rise in the east and set in the west.

**Teach:** Earth's rotation from west to east creates apparent daily motion; latitude affects the apparent paths; the Sun’s annual path changes with season and observer location.

**Questions**
1. What causes the daily apparent motion of the Sun across the sky? **Answer:** Earth's rotation on its axis.
2. In which general direction does the Sun appear to rise? **Answer:** East.
3. Do stars follow exactly the same apparent path for observers at different latitudes? **Answer:** No. Their apparent paths depend on the observer’s latitude.

## Outcome 15 — Apparent Solar Time

**Objective:** Collect and compare observations of the times when the Sun crosses an observer’s meridian on different days to explain apparent solar time.

**Starter:** Ask why the time between successive local solar noons is not exactly the same every day.

**Teach:** Apparent solar time based on the real Sun’s position; solar noon when the Sun crosses the local meridian; variation caused by Earth’s orbit and axial tilt; equation of time.

**Questions**
1. What event defines apparent local solar noon? **Answer:** The Sun crossing the observer’s local meridian.
2. Is apparent solar time perfectly uniform from day to day? **Answer:** No.
3. What is one cause of variation in apparent solar time? **Answer:** Earth's elliptical orbit and axial tilt.

## Outcome 16 — Mean Solar Time and Time Zones

**Objective:** Explain mean solar time and how local times are compared and organised into time zones around Earth.

**Starter:** Compare the local clock time in Thailand and another country. Ask why clocks show different times at the same moment.

**Teach:** Mean Sun as a uniformly moving reference; mean solar day of 24 hours; Earth’s rotation and longitude; standard time zones and UTC; account for official time-zone boundaries.

**Questions**
1. How long is the mean solar day? **Answer:** 24 hours.
2. Why do places at different longitudes have different local solar times? **Answer:** Earth rotates, so the Sun crosses each longitude at a different time.
3. What is UTC used for? **Answer:** A common international time reference for comparing and coordinating time.

## Outcome 17 — Planetary Elongation and Apparent Position

**Objective:** Explain planetary elongation in relation to orbital position and connect it to the apparent positions of planets observed from Earth.

**Starter:** Show a diagram of Earth, the Sun and an inner planet. Ask why Venus is usually seen near sunrise or sunset rather than high overhead at midnight.

**Teach:** Elongation is the angular separation between a planet and the Sun as seen from Earth; distinguish eastern and western elongation; relate orbital geometry to visibility and conjunction/opposition configurations.

**Questions**
1. What does planetary elongation measure? **Answer:** The angular separation between a planet and the Sun in the sky as seen from Earth.
2. When is an inner planet often visible near the horizon? **Answer:** Around dawn or dusk, depending on whether it is west or east of the Sun.
3. Why can an outer planet be visible around midnight? **Answer:** Near opposition it appears opposite the Sun in the sky and is above the horizon for much of the night.

## Outcome 18 — Space Exploration Technologies and Applications

**Objective:** Research and explain space exploration using telescopes across different wavelengths, satellites, spacecraft and space stations, and propose applications of space technology in everyday life or the future.

**Starter:** Ask students to list services they use that may depend on satellites.

**Teach:** Ground and space telescopes across the electromagnetic spectrum; Earth-observation, communication and navigation satellites; probes, crewed spacecraft and space stations; applications in weather, disaster monitoring, agriculture, communication and materials.

**Questions**
1. Why do astronomers use telescopes that observe different wavelengths? **Answer:** Different wavelengths reveal different physical processes and objects, and some wavelengths are blocked by Earth's atmosphere.
2. Give one practical use of Earth-observation satellites. **Answer:** Monitoring weather, floods, fires, crops or land-use change.
3. What is one difference between a satellite and a space probe? **Answer:** A satellite orbits a body, while a probe is a spacecraft sent to investigate space or another celestial body; a probe may also enter orbit.

## Outcome 19 — Planning a Sky-Observation Activity

**Objective:** Research, design and present an activity for observing celestial objects with the unaided eye and/or a telescope.

**Starter:** Ask students what they could observe in the night sky without travelling to a specialist observatory.

**Teach:** Select a safe observation target; plan date, time, location and weather backup; use a sky chart or astronomy app; record direction, altitude, time and conditions; telescope handling and eye safety.

**Questions**
1. What information should be recorded during a sky observation? **Answer:** Date, time, location, target, direction/altitude, equipment and observing conditions.
2. Why should students check weather and moonlight before an observation? **Answer:** Clouds and bright moonlight can reduce visibility.
3. What is the essential safety rule when observing the Sun? **Answer:** Never look directly at the Sun through the unaided eye, binoculars or a telescope; use only purpose-built, certified solar filters correctly fitted to the front of suitable equipment or use indirect projection under expert supervision.

## Teacher safety and assessment notes

Never look directly at the Sun through the unaided eye, binoculars or an unfiltered telescope. Use certified front-mounted solar filters or safe indirect projection under expert supervision. For stellar evolution and cosmology, use simulations, spectra, published datasets and scale models. Do not imply that habitable-zone location proves life exists. Assess evidence interpretation, model use, calculations, and the ability to explain uncertainty.
