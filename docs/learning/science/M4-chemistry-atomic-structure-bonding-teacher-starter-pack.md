## Lesson 1 — Atomic models and scientific evidence
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-ATOM-01 — Compare Dalton, Thomson, Rutherford, Bohr and quantum-mechanical models. Use evidence and explain why scientific models change.
**Starter / learning task:** What is an atomic model? Use worked examples, particle diagrams, periodic-table data or teacher-provided datasets to develop the concept. Guide learners to explain their reasoning and check units, symbols and assumptions.

**Practice**
1. What is an atomic model?
   **Answer:** A scientific representation used to explain and predict atomic structure and behaviour.
2. What evidence challenged the plum-pudding model?
   **Answer:** Rutherford's alpha-scattering results showed a small, dense, positively charged nucleus.
3. Why are models revised?
   **Answer:** New evidence may reveal limitations in an existing explanation.

**Practice**
1. What does atomic number represent?
   **Answer:** The number of protons in the nucleus.
2. How many neutrons are in carbon-14?

3. What are isotopes?
   **Answer:** Atoms of the same element with equal proton numbers but different neutron numbers.

## Lesson 5 — Periodic trends
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-ATOM-05 — Use atomic radius, ionisation energy and electronegativity trends to explain patterns across periods and down groups.

**Practice**
1. What generally happens to atomic radius across a period?
   **Answer:** It generally decreases as effective nuclear attraction increases.
2. What generally happens to atomic radius down a group?
   **Answer:** It generally increases because additional electron shells are occupied.
3. Why do periodic trends repeat?
   **Answer:** Elements have repeating valence-electron patterns.

**Practice**
1. What is radioactive decay?
   **Answer:** Spontaneous change of an unstable nucleus accompanied by emitted radiation or particles.
2. A sample has a half-life of 5 days. What fraction remains after 10 days?
   **Answer:** One quarter remains after two half-lives.
3. Why is half-life useful?
   **Answer:** It helps estimate decay over time and compare radioactive materials.

**Practice**

   **Answer:** Hess's law: total enthalpy change is independent of route.
2. What does atomisation represent?

## Lesson 12 — Properties of ionic compounds
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-ATOM-12 — Connect ionic lattice structure with melting, brittleness, solubility and conductivity.
**Starter / learning task:** Why do ionic solids often have high melting points? Use worked examples, particle diagrams, periodic-table data or teacher-provided datasets to develop the concept. Guide learners to explain their reasoning and check units, symbols and assumptions.

**Practice**
1. Why do ionic solids often have high melting points?
   **Answer:** Strong electrostatic attractions in the lattice require substantial energy to overcome.
2. Why can a solid ionic crystal conduct poorly?
   **Answer:** Its ions are fixed in place and cannot move freely.
3. When can an ionic substance conduct electricity?
   **Answer:** When molten or dissolved and its ions are mobile.

## Lesson 16 — Bond length and bond energy
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-ATOM-16 — Compare single, double and triple bonds and estimate reaction enthalpy from average bond enthalpies.
**Starter / learning task:** Which is generally shortest: C–C, C=C or C≡C? Use worked examples, particle diagrams, periodic-table data or teacher-provided datasets to develop the concept. Guide learners to explain their reasoning and check units, symbols and assumptions.

**Practice**
1. Which is generally shortest: C–C, C=C or C≡C?
   **Answer:** C≡C is generally shortest.
2. What does bond enthalpy estimate?
   **Answer:** The energy needed to break one mole of a specified bond in gaseous species.
3. How is reaction enthalpy estimated from bond enthalpies?
   **Answer:** Sum bonds broken minus sum bonds formed.

## Assessment and safety

- Use the questions as formative checks and include structured calculation problems with units and significant figures where appropriate.
- Use simulations, diagrams and teacher-selected datasets for radioactive decay; do not handle radioactive sources in ordinary classroom activities.
- Do not allow unsupervised handling of corrosive chemicals, reactive metals, toxic compounds or open flames. Use microscale demonstrations and approved safety procedures where practical work is required.
- For Born-Haber and bond-enthalpy calculations, state the data source, definitions and sign convention explicitly.
- Ask learners to distinguish a model from direct observation and support claims with evidence.
