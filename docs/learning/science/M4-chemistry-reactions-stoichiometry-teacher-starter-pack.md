## Lesson 1 — Interpreting and balancing chemical equations
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-STOICH-01 — Interpret formulae, coefficients, state symbols and reaction conditions; balance equations by conserving atoms.
**Starter / learning task:** Display an unbalanced equation and ask what information the formulae and arrow communicate. Model balancing with atom-count tables before students practise.

**Practice**
1. What does a coefficient in a chemical equation show?  
   **Answer:** The relative number of particles or moles of that substance.
2. Balance: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O.  
   **Answer:** 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.
3. Why must an equation be balanced?  
   **Answer:** Atoms are conserved in a chemical reaction; each element must have equal atom counts on both sides.

## Lesson 2 — Stoichiometry using mass
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-STOICH-02 — Convert mass to amount of substance and use mole ratios to calculate reactant or product masses.
**Starter / learning task:** Use a balanced equation and molar-mass table. Model the sequence: mass → moles → mole ratio → moles → mass.

**Practice**
1. What is the amount of substance in 18 g of water (M = 18 g mol⁻¹)?  
   **Answer:** 1 mol.
2. In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, how many moles of water form from 3 mol H₂ with sufficient oxygen?  
   **Answer:** 3 mol H₂O.
3. What conversion is needed before using a mole ratio with a mass?  
   **Answer:** Convert mass to moles using molar mass.

## Lesson 3 — Stoichiometry using solution concentration
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-STOICH-03 — Use molar concentration and solution volume to determine amount of dissolved reactant or product.
**Starter / learning task:** Connect molarity to moles per litre. Work through n = cV, checking that volume is expressed in litres.

**Practice**
1. What does a concentration of 0.5 mol L⁻¹ mean?  
   **Answer:** Each litre of solution contains 0.5 mol of solute.
2. How many moles are in 0.20 L of a 0.50 mol L⁻¹ solution?  
   **Answer:** n = cV = 0.50 × 0.20 = 0.10 mol.
3. Why convert millilitres to litres in n = cV when c is in mol L⁻¹?  
   **Answer:** The volume unit must match the concentration unit.

## Lesson 4 — Stoichiometry involving gas volume
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-STOICH-04 — Use appropriate gas-volume relationships and mole ratios under stated conditions.
**Starter / learning task:** Compare gas volumes only after identifying the stated temperature and pressure conditions. Use a supplied data table or ideal-gas relationship as appropriate.

**Practice**
1. Why must gas conditions be stated in a volume calculation?  
   **Answer:** Gas volume depends on temperature and pressure.
2. At the same temperature and pressure, what is the volume ratio for gases in a reaction?  
   **Answer:** It follows the mole ratio in the balanced equation.
3. Which equation relates gas pressure, volume, amount and temperature for an ideal gas?  
   **Answer:** PV = nRT.

## Lesson 5 — Multistep stoichiometry
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-STOICH-05 — Combine balanced equations and mole relationships for reactions occurring in multiple steps.
**Starter / learning task:** Present a two-stage reaction pathway. Have learners identify the intermediate, balance each equation and connect the mole ratios across stages.

**Practice**
1. What is an intermediate in a multistep reaction?  
   **Answer:** A substance produced in one step and consumed in a later step.
2. Why should each step be balanced before combining equations?  
   **Answer:** Balanced equations provide valid mole ratios and conserve atoms.
3. What should happen to an intermediate when adding compatible reaction steps?  
   **Answer:** It cancels if produced and consumed in equal amounts.

## Lesson 6 — Limiting reactants
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-STOICH-06 — Identify the limiting reactant and calculate product formed and excess reactant remaining.
**Starter / learning task:** Use a recipe analogy before moving to a balanced equation. Calculate the product possible from each reactant and identify which runs out first.

**Practice**
1. What is the limiting reactant?  
   **Answer:** The reactant consumed first, which determines the maximum amount of product.
2. If a reaction needs 2 mol A for every 1 mol B, how much A is needed for 3 mol B?  
   **Answer:** 6 mol A.
3. What remains after the limiting reactant is used up?  
   **Answer:** Any unconsumed excess reactant.

## Lesson 7 — Percentage yield
**Objective:** M4-CHEM-STOICH-07 — Calculate theoretical yield and percentage yield from actual experimental yield.
**Starter / learning task:** Distinguish the maximum predicted product from the product actually collected. Model percentage yield with units and sensible rounding.

**Practice**
1. What is theoretical yield?  
   **Answer:** The maximum product amount predicted by stoichiometry.
2. A reaction has a theoretical yield of 10 g and an actual yield of 8 g. What is the percentage yield?  
   **Answer:** (8 ÷ 10) × 100 = 80%.
3. Give one reason actual yield may be lower than theoretical yield.  
   **Answer:** The reaction may be incomplete, side reactions may occur, or product may be lost during separation.
