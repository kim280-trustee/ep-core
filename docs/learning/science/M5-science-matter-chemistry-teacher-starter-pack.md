## Lesson 1 — Classify elements and compounds from chemical formulae

**Objective:** `M5-S2_1-01`  
**Official indicator:** ว 2.1 ม.5/1 | **Priority:** Must-Know  
**Learning outcome:** Identify whether a substance is an element or compound and whether it is represented as atoms, molecules or ions by interpreting its chemical formula.  
**Starter:** Show several familiar chemical formulae. Students classify each as an element or compound and explain the evidence.

**Formative practice**
1. What does a chemical formula show?
   **Answer:** It shows which elements are present and the number or ratio of their atoms or ions.
2. Is H₂O an element or a compound?
   **Answer:** A compound, because it contains hydrogen and oxygen chemically combined.
3. In what form is NaCl commonly represented in a formula?
   **Answer:** As a formula unit of ions, Na⁺ and Cl⁻, in an ionic compound.

## Lesson 2 — Compare Bohr and electron-cloud atomic models

**Objective:** `M5-S2_1-02`  
**Official indicator:** ว 2.1 ม.5/2 | **Priority:** Should-Know  
**Learning outcome:** Compare the Bohr model, with electrons in energy levels, with the electron-cloud model, which represents probabilities of finding electrons.  
**Starter:** Display Bohr and electron-cloud diagrams. Students list one similarity and one difference.

**Formative practice**
1. What does the Bohr model show?
   **Answer:** Electrons in defined energy levels or shells around the nucleus.
2. What does an electron cloud represent?
   **Answer:** The probability of finding an electron in different regions around the nucleus.
3. Why is an electron cloud not drawn as a fixed circular path?
   **Answer:** Electrons cannot be assigned a precise fixed path; the model represents probability.

## Lesson 3 — Determine protons, neutrons and electrons

**Objective:** `M5-S2_1-03`  
**Official indicator:** ว 2.1 ม.5/3 | **Priority:** Must-Know  
**Learning outcome:** Determine the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in neutral atoms and simple ions.  
**Starter:** Give students simple atomic and ion symbols. They calculate the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons.

**Formative practice**
1. What determines the identity of an element?
   **Answer:** The number of protons in its nucleus (atomic number).
2. How many electrons does a neutral atom with 11 protons have?
   **Answer:** 11 electrons.
3. How does a neutral atom form a 2+ ion?
   **Answer:** It loses two electrons.

## Lesson 4 — Write nuclear symbols and identify isotopes

**Objective:** `M5-S2_1-04`  
**Official indicator:** ว 2.1 ม.5/4 | **Priority:** Must-Know  
**Learning outcome:** Write nuclear symbols using atomic number and mass number, and identify isotopes of the same element.  
**Starter:** Compare two nuclear symbols for the same element and ask students what is the same and what is different.

**Formative practice**
1. What does the atomic number represent?
   **Answer:** The number of protons.
2. What does the mass number represent?
   **Answer:** The total number of protons and neutrons.
3. What makes two atoms isotopes of the same element?
   **Answer:** They have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

## Lesson 5 — Use the periodic table to classify elements

**Objective:** `M5-S2_1-05`  
**Official indicator:** ว 2.1 ม.5/5 | **Priority:** Must-Know  
**Learning outcome:** Use group and period positions to identify element categories, including metals, non-metals, metalloids, representative elements and transition elements.  
**Starter:** Give students a periodic table and ask them to locate a metal, non-metal and transition element.

**Formative practice**
1. What is a group in the periodic table?
   **Answer:** A vertical column.
2. What is a period?
   **Answer:** A horizontal row.
3. Where are most metals found?
   **Answer:** On the left and in the centre of the periodic table.

## Lesson 6 — Compare metals and non-metals

**Objective:** `M5-S2_1-06`  
**Official indicator:** ว 2.1 ม.5/6 | **Priority:** Must-Know  
**Learning outcome:** Compare electrical conductivity and electron-transfer tendencies of metals and non-metals.  
**Starter:** Compare a metal wire and a non-metal sample, focusing on conductivity and electron behaviour.

**Formative practice**
1. Which group generally conducts electricity better: metals or non-metals?
   **Answer:** Metals.
2. What do metals commonly do when forming ions?
   **Answer:** They lose electrons and form positive ions.
3. What do non-metals commonly do when forming ions?
   **Answer:** They gain electrons and form negative ions.

## Lesson 8 — Identify single, double and triple covalent bonds

**Objective:** `M5-S2_1-08`  
**Official indicator:** ว 2.1 ม.5/8 | **Priority:** Must-Know  
**Learning outcome:** Interpret structural formulae to identify single, double and triple covalent bonds and the shared electron pairs between atoms.  
**Starter:** Display structural formulae and ask students to count the shared electron pairs.

**Formative practice**
1. How many shared electron pairs form a single covalent bond?
   **Answer:** One pair.
2. How many shared electron pairs form a double bond?
   **Answer:** Two pairs.
3. What does three lines between two atoms represent in a structural formula?
   **Answer:** A triple covalent bond, with three shared electron pairs.

## Lesson 9 — Identify polarity in diatomic molecules

**Objective:** `M5-S2_1-09`  
**Official indicator:** ว 2.1 ม.5/9 | **Priority:** Must-Know  
**Learning outcome:** Determine whether a covalent molecule containing two atoms is polar or non-polar by considering the atoms and bond polarity.  
**Starter:** Display Bohr and electron-cloud diagrams. Students list one similarity and one difference.

**Formative practice**
1. Is Cl₂ polar or non-polar?
   **Answer:** Non-polar, because both atoms are identical and share electrons equally.
2. Why is HCl polar?
   **Answer:** Chlorine attracts the shared electrons more strongly, producing an uneven charge distribution.
3. What is a dipole?
   **Answer:** A separation of partial positive and partial negative charge.

## Lesson 10 — Identify hydrogen bonding from structural formulae

**Objective:** `M5-S2_1-10`  
**Official indicator:** ว 2.1 ม.5/10 | **Priority:** Must-Know  
**Learning outcome:** Identify molecules that can form hydrogen bonds by examining structural formulae and the presence of suitable hydrogen–nitrogen, hydrogen–oxygen or hydrogen–fluorine bonds.  
**Starter:** Show several familiar chemical formulae. Students classify each as an element or compound and explain the evidence.

**Formative practice**
1. Which atoms commonly participate in hydrogen bonding with hydrogen?
   **Answer:** Nitrogen, oxygen and fluorine.
2. Can water molecules form hydrogen bonds with one another?
   **Answer:** Yes, because water contains O–H bonds and oxygen has lone pairs.
3. Is hydrogen bonding the same as a covalent O–H bond?
   **Answer:** No. It is an intermolecular attraction, not the covalent bond within a water molecule.

## Lesson 11 — Classify saturated and unsaturated hydrocarbons

**Objective:** `M5-S2_1-14`  
**Official indicator:** ว 2.1 ม.5/14 | **Priority:** Must-Know  
**Learning outcome:** Use structural formulae to distinguish saturated hydrocarbons, which contain only carbon–carbon single bonds, from unsaturated hydrocarbons containing double or triple bonds.  
**Starter:** Compare ethane, ethene and ethyne structural formulae and identify the bond types.

**Formative practice**
1. What elements are present in a hydrocarbon?
   **Answer:** Carbon and hydrogen only.
2. Is ethane saturated or unsaturated?
   **Answer:** Saturated; it contains a carbon–carbon single bond.
3. What feature makes a hydrocarbon unsaturated?
   **Answer:** At least one carbon–carbon double or triple bond.

## Lesson 12 — Compare polymers and their monomers

**Objective:** `M5-S2_1-15`  
**Official indicator:** ว 2.1 ม.5/15 | **Priority:** Must-Know  
**Learning outcome:** Research and compare physical properties of a polymer with those of its monomer, including state, melting behaviour and solubility.  
**Starter:** Show a chain of linked paper clips and individual clips as a model of polymer and monomer.

**Formative practice**
1. What is a monomer?
   **Answer:** A small molecule that can join with other molecules to form a polymer.
2. What is a polymer?
   **Answer:** A large molecule made from many repeating monomer units.
3. Why can a polymer have different physical properties from its monomer?
   **Answer:** Its long molecular chains and structure create different physical behaviour.

## Lesson 14 — Explain solubility using polarity

**Objective:** `M5-S2_1-17`  
**Official indicator:** ว 2.1 ม.5/17 | **Priority:** Must-Know  
**Learning outcome:** Explain how solubility depends on the relative polarity of solute and solvent, using the principle that substances with similar polarity tend to dissolve in one another.  
**Starter:** Compare Cl₂ and HCl diagrams. Students predict whether charge is distributed evenly.

**Formative practice**
1. What does 'like dissolves like' mean?
   **Answer:** Polar substances tend to dissolve in polar solvents, and non-polar substances in non-polar solvents.
2. Will oil mix well with water?
   **Answer:** No. Oil is largely non-polar while water is polar.
3. Why does salt dissolve in water?
   **Answer:** Water is polar and can attract and stabilise the ions in the salt.

**Starter:** Give an unbalanced chemical equation and ask students to identify reactants and products.

**Formative practice**
1. Where are reactants written in a chemical equation?
   **Answer:** On the left side of the arrow.
2. What does (g) mean in a chemical equation?
   **Answer:** The substance is in the gas state.
3. Why must a chemical equation be balanced?

## Lesson 18 — Investigate factors affecting reaction rate

**Objective:** `M5-S2_1-21`  
**Official indicator:** ว 2.1 ม.5/21 | **Priority:** Must-Know  
**Learning outcome:** Conduct experiments and explain how concentration, surface area, temperature and catalysts affect the rate of a chemical reaction.  
**Starter:** Show two simple reaction setups with different temperatures or surface areas and ask which reacts faster.

**Formative practice**
1. What happens to reaction rate when concentration increases, under suitable conditions?
   **Answer:** The rate usually increases because particles collide more frequently.
2. Why can powdered calcium carbonate react faster than a large lump?
   **Answer:** It has a greater exposed surface area.
3. What does a catalyst do?
   **Answer:** It increases reaction rate by providing an alternative pathway with lower activation energy, without being used up overall.

## Lesson 21 — Calculate radioactive half-life and remaining quantity

**Objective:** `M5-S2_1-24`  
**Official indicator:** ว 2.1 ม.5/24 | **Priority:** Must-Know  
**Learning outcome:** Explain properties of radioactive substances and calculate half-life and the quantity remaining after a given time.  
**Starter:** Use 100 counters to model repeated halving and ask students to predict the amount remaining.

**Formative practice**
1. What is half-life?
   **Answer:** The time required for half of the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay.
2. A sample starts at 80 g. How much remains after two half-lives?
   **Answer:** 20 g.
3. Does the half-life of a particular isotope change because the sample is smaller?
   **Answer:** No. Its half-life is a characteristic property of that isotope.

## Lesson 23 — Explain boiling points of covalent substances

**Objective:** `M5-S2_1-11`  
**Official indicator:** ว 2.1 ม.5/11 | **Priority:** Must-Know  
**Learning outcome:** Explain how molecular polarity and intermolecular forces, including hydrogen bonding, affect boiling points of covalent substances.  
**Starter:** Compare water and hydrogen sulfide. Ask students why water has a much higher boiling point.

1. What kind of attraction helps explain water's relatively high boiling point?
   **Answer:** Hydrogen bonding between water molecules.
2. How can molecular polarity affect boiling point?
   **Answer:** Greater intermolecular attractions generally require more energy to separate molecules, increasing boiling point.
3. Which bond types within a molecule can enable hydrogen bonding between molecules?
   **Answer:** N–H, O–H or F–H bonds.

## Lesson 25 — Classify dissociation and electrolyte solutions

**Objective:** `M5-S2_1-13`  
**Official indicator:** ว 2.1 ม.5/13 | **Priority:** Must-Know  
**Learning outcome:** Determine whether a substance dissolves by dissociation or without dissociation, explain the reason, and classify the resulting solution as an electrolyte or non-electrolyte.  
**Starter:** Compare salt water and sugar water using a safe conductivity demonstration or simulation.

1. What happens when sodium chloride dissolves in water?
   **Answer:** It dissociates into mobile Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions.
2. Does sugar solution normally contain ions formed by dissociation?
   **Answer:** No. Sugar molecules dissolve without dissociating into ions.
3. Which solution conducts electricity: salt water or sugar water?
   **Answer:** Salt water conducts because it contains mobile ions; sugar water is a non-electrolyte solution.
