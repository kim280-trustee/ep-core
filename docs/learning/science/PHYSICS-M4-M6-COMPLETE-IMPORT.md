# M4 Physics — Foundations, Measurement, Motion and Forces

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017)  
**Subject / grade:** Physics, M4  
**Official coverage:** Additional Physics learning outcomes 1–7  
**Coverage:** 7 lesson starters; 21 practice questions with answer keys  
**Use:** Teacher planning resource. Prefer diagrams, simulations and low-risk demonstrations; do not use hazardous equipment or unsafe suspended loads.

## Lesson 1 — The nature and development of physics

**Objective:** M4-PHY-FOUNDATIONS-01 — Explain how physics knowledge is developed and how physical ideas contribute to new knowledge and technology.

**Starter:** Show a familiar technology such as a mobile phone, bicycle brake or solar panel. Ask learners which physics ideas help it work. Build a simple sequence: observation → question → investigation/model → evidence → explanation → further questions.

**Practice**
1. Name two ways scientists develop knowledge in physics.
   **Answer:** Observation and experimentation; modelling and analysing evidence are also valid.
2. Why do scientific explanations change when new evidence becomes available?
   **Answer:** Scientific explanations are tested against evidence and may be refined when better evidence or models become available.
3. Give one example of physics knowledge being used in technology.
   **Answer:** Any valid example, such as electricity in phones, optics in cameras, or mechanics in vehicle safety.

## Lesson 2 — Measurement, uncertainty and graphs

**Objective:** M4-PHY-MEASUREMENT-02 — Measure and report physical quantities appropriately, consider uncertainty, and analyse straight-line graphs.

**Starter:** Compare measuring a desk with a ruler and estimating its length by eye. Discuss why measurements include uncertainty. Demonstrate SI units, sensible significant figures and plotting a straight-line graph from supplied data.

**Practice**
1. Convert 250 cm to metres.
   **Answer:** 2.50 m.
2. A measured length is 12.4 cm with an estimated uncertainty of ±0.1 cm. State the result.
   **Answer:** (12.4 ± 0.1) cm.
3. A straight-line distance–time graph rises from 0 m at 0 s to 20 m at 5 s. Find its gradient and state what it represents.
   **Answer:** Gradient = 20/5 = 4 m/s; it represents velocity for this straight-line graph.

## Lesson 3 — Uniformly accelerated straight-line motion

**Objective:** M4-PHY-MOTION-03 — Use graphs and equations to explain uniformly accelerated straight-line motion and investigate gravitational acceleration.

**Starter:** Use a trolley video or simulation. Ask learners how velocity changes over equal time intervals. Connect the slope of a velocity–time graph to acceleration and its area to displacement.

**Practice**
1. A cyclist increases velocity from 2 m/s to 10 m/s in 4 s. Calculate acceleration.
   **Answer:** a = (10 − 2)/4 = 2 m/s².
2. An object starts from rest and accelerates at 3 m/s² for 5 s. Find its final velocity.
   **Answer:** v = u + at = 0 + 3 × 5 = 15 m/s.
3. What is the approximate gravitational acceleration near Earth's surface?
   **Answer:** 9.8 m/s² downward (often approximated as 10 m/s²).

## Lesson 4 — Forces and resultant force

**Objective:** M4-PHY-FORCES-04 — Explain how forces affect motion and determine the resultant of two forces acting at an angle.

**Starter:** Show two learners pulling a light object in different directions using a diagram or simulation. Ask whether the effects simply add. Introduce force as a vector with magnitude and direction.

**Practice**
1. Two forces of 5 N and 3 N act in the same direction. Find the resultant.
   **Answer:** 8 N in that direction.
2. Two forces of 8 N act in opposite directions. Find the resultant.
   **Answer:** 0 N; the forces balance.
3. Two perpendicular forces of 3 N and 4 N act on an object. Find the magnitude of the resultant.
   **Answer:** R = √(3² + 4²) = 5 N.

## Lesson 5 — Newton laws and free-body diagrams

**Objective:** M4-PHY-NEWTON-05 — Draw free-body diagrams and apply Newton laws to motion.

**Starter:** Ask why a passenger moves forward when a moving bus stops. Use simple diagrams to identify weight, normal force, friction and applied force. Emphasise that each force acts on the selected object.

**Practice**
1. State Newton's first law.
   **Answer:** An object remains at rest or moves with constant velocity unless acted on by a non-zero resultant external force.
2. A 4 kg object experiences a resultant force of 12 N. Calculate acceleration.
   **Answer:** a = F/m = 12/4 = 3 m/s².
3. A book rests on a horizontal table. Name the two main vertical forces acting on the book.
   **Answer:** Weight downward and normal contact force upward; they balance when the book is at rest.

## Lesson 6 — Universal gravitation and gravitational fields

**Objective:** M4-PHY-GRAVITATION-06 — Explain universal gravitation, gravitational fields and weight.

**Starter:** Compare the mass of an object on Earth and on the Moon. Ask what changes and what remains the same. Distinguish mass from weight and introduce gravitational field strength.

**Practice**
1. What is the difference between mass and weight?
   **Answer:** Mass measures inertia and is measured in kilograms; weight is gravitational force and is measured in newtons.
2. Calculate the weight of a 5 kg object where g = 10 N/kg.
   **Answer:** W = mg = 5 × 10 = 50 N.
3. If the distance between two point masses increases, what happens to the gravitational force between them?
   **Answer:** It decreases according to the inverse-square relationship.

## Lesson 7 — Friction

**Objective:** M4-PHY-FRICTION-07 — Explain static and kinetic friction, determine friction coefficients and apply the ideas to everyday situations.

**Starter:** Compare a shoe on a dry floor and a smooth wet surface. Ask why friction matters for walking and braking. Use diagrams or supplied data to distinguish static friction from kinetic friction.

**Practice**
1. What is the difference between static friction and kinetic friction?
   **Answer:** Static friction acts when surfaces do not slide relative to each other; kinetic friction acts while surfaces slide.
2. A 10 N friction force acts when the normal force is 25 N. Calculate the coefficient of kinetic friction.
   **Answer:** μ = F/N = 10/25 = 0.40.
3. Give one useful and one harmful effect of friction.
   **Answer:** Useful: walking or braking. Harmful: wear or unwanted heating of machine parts.

## Teaching and assessment notes

- Keep vector direction and sign conventions explicit in motion and force calculations.
- Use appropriate SI units and sensible significant figures; discuss uncertainty rather than presenting measurements as exact.
- Distinguish mass (kg), weight (N), and gravitational field strength (N/kg).
- A free-body diagram includes forces acting on one selected object, not forces that object exerts on other objects.
- The coefficient of friction is dimensionless. Use safe surfaces and teacher-approved data; do not ask students to create dangerous slip conditions.
- Use simulations and supplied data for investigations that require specialist equipment.

# M4 Physics — Mechanical Equilibrium, Centre of Mass and Stability

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017)  
**Subject / grade:** Physics, M4  
**Official coverage in this batch:** Additional Physics learning outcomes 8–9  
**Coverage:** 2 lesson starters; 6 practice questions with answer keys  
**Use:** Teacher planning resource. Use diagrams, low-risk classroom models and supplied datasets; assess calculations with units and clear sign conventions.

## Lesson 1 — Mechanical equilibrium, moments and couples

**Objective:** M4-PHY-MECH-08 — Explain translational and rotational equilibrium; calculate moments and resultant moments; analyse couples; draw free-body diagrams for objects in mechanical equilibrium; and investigate equilibrium of three forces.

**Starter / learning task:** Ask why a book resting on a table does not accelerate or rotate. Learners draw a free-body diagram, identify forces and discuss the conditions for translational and rotational equilibrium. Use a ruler, pivot and small masses for a teacher-supervised moment demonstration. Compare a door pushed near and far from its hinge to introduce moment arm.

**Practice**
1. What is translational equilibrium?
   **Answer:** The resultant force on an object is zero, so its linear acceleration is zero.
2. What is the moment of a force about a pivot?
   **Answer:** The turning effect of a force; its magnitude is force multiplied by the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the force's line of action.
3. What conditions describe complete mechanical equilibrium for a rigid object in a plane?
   **Answer:** The resultant force is zero and the resultant moment about any chosen point is zero.

## Lesson 2 — Centre of mass, centre of gravity and stability

**Objective:** M4-PHY-MECH-09 — Observe and explain how a force through an object’s centre of mass affects its motion, distinguish centre of mass from centre of gravity, and analyse how centre-of-gravity position affects stability.

**Starter / learning task:** Compare pushing a book through its middle and near an edge. Learners predict whether it will translate, rotate or do both. Use a suspended card with plumb lines or a balanced ruler to locate a centre of mass. Discuss why a lower centre of gravity generally improves stability, using a wide-base object and a tall narrow object as examples.

**Practice**
1. What is the centre of mass?
   **Answer:** The position that represents the weighted average location of an object's mass.
2. When do centre of mass and centre of gravity coincide?
   **Answer:** In a uniform gravitational field, they are at the same position.
3. Why is an object with a lower centre of gravity often more stable?
   **Answer:** It generally needs a larger tilt before the line of action of its weight falls outside its base of support.

## Teaching and assessment notes

- Require labelled free-body diagrams and explicit moment arms.
- State the chosen sign convention for clockwise and anticlockwise moments.
- Use small, stable classroom objects; do not use heavy suspended loads or unstable elevated structures.
- Distinguish the centre of mass as a mass-distribution concept from centre of gravity as the effective point of action of weight.
- These two outcomes are a staged subset of the national Physics curriculum, not a claim that all M4 Physics outcomes are complete.

# M4 Physics — Momentum, Impulse and Collisions

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017)  
**Subject / grade:** Physics, M4  
**Official coverage:** Additional Physics learning outcomes 14–15  
**Coverage:** 2 lesson starters; 6 practice questions with answer keys

## Lesson 1 — Momentum and impulse

**Objective:** M4-PHY-MOMENTUM-14 — Explain and calculate momentum and impulse, including impulse from the area under a resultant-force–time graph.

**Starter / learning task:** Compare stopping a slowly moving empty trolley with stopping a faster or more heavily loaded trolley. Learners discuss why the change in momentum and stopping time matter. Introduce momentum as p = mv and impulse as the change in momentum. Interpret a simple force–time graph and identify impulse as its signed area. Use only low-mass trolley demonstrations or supplied data.

**Practice**
1. Calculate the momentum of a 3 kg trolley moving at 4 m/s.
   **Answer:** p = mv = 3 × 4 = 12 kg m/s.
2. A 20 N resultant force acts for 0.5 s. Calculate the impulse.
   **Answer:** I = FΔt = 20 × 0.5 = 10 N s.
3. A force–time graph is a rectangle with force 8 N and duration 3 s. Find the impulse.
   **Answer:** Area = 8 × 3 = 24 N s. This equals the change in momentum.

## Lesson 2 — One-dimensional collisions and conservation of momentum

**Objective:** M4-PHY-COLLISION-15 — Investigate, explain and calculate one-dimensional elastic and inelastic collisions and separation events using conservation of momentum when the system has no external resultant force.

**Starter / learning task:** Use a labelled diagram or a low-friction trolley simulation to compare two carts before and after a collision. Learners calculate total momentum before and after. Distinguish elastic collisions, in which total kinetic energy is conserved, from inelastic collisions, in which it is not. Emphasise that momentum conservation applies to an isolated system, even when kinetic energy is not conserved.

**Practice**
1. A 2 kg cart travels at 3 m/s. Calculate its momentum.
   **Answer:** p = 2 × 3 = 6 kg m/s.
2. A 1 kg cart moving at 4 m/s collides and sticks to a stationary 1 kg cart. Find their shared velocity after collision.
   **Answer:** Initial momentum = 4 kg m/s; combined mass = 2 kg; v = 4 ÷ 2 = 2 m/s in the original direction.
3. State the difference between an elastic and an inelastic collision.
   **Answer:** Both conserve total momentum in an isolated system. An elastic collision also conserves total kinetic energy; an inelastic collision does not.

## Teaching and assessment notes

- Treat momentum and velocity as vector quantities; choose and state a positive direction.
- Impulse is the signed area under a resultant-force–time graph and equals change in momentum.
- Use consistent SI units: kg m/s for momentum and N s for impulse.
- State the system boundary and check whether external forces are negligible before applying momentum conservation.
- Do not imply kinetic energy is conserved in every collision.
- Use simulations, video analysis or teacher-controlled low-energy carts; avoid high-speed or heavy-object collision demonstrations.

IPST, *Physics curriculum-use manual for upper-secondary additional Physics*, M4 learning outcomes 14–15, printed pages 118–120.

# M4 Physics — Projectile and Circular Motion

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017)  
**Subject / grade:** Physics, M4  
**Official coverage:** Additional Physics learning outcomes 16–17  
**Coverage:** 2 lesson starters; 6 practice questions with answer keys

## Lesson 1 — Projectile motion

**Objective:** M4-PHY-PROJECTILE-16 — Explain, analyse and calculate projectile motion, recognising the independence of horizontal and vertical components when air resistance is neglected.

**Starter / learning task:** Show a slow-motion video or simulation of a ball launched horizontally. Ask learners to describe its horizontal and vertical motion separately. Establish that horizontal velocity remains constant in the ideal model while vertical motion accelerates under gravity. Use a simulation or supplied measurements to compare launch speeds and heights; do not use high-power launchers.

**Practice**
1. A ball is launched horizontally at 6 m/s and travels for 2 s. How far does it travel horizontally, neglecting air resistance?  
   **Answer:** x = vt = 6 × 2 = 12 m.
2. An object is dropped from rest. Taking g = 10 m/s², find its vertical speed after 3 s.  
   **Answer:** v = u + gt = 0 + 10 × 3 = 30 m/s downward.
3. Explain why horizontal and vertical motions can be analysed independently in ideal projectile motion.  
   **Answer:** Gravity acts vertically and there is no horizontal resultant force when air resistance is neglected. The vertical velocity changes while horizontal velocity remains constant.

## Lesson 2 — Uniform circular motion and satellite orbits

**Objective:** M4-PHY-CIRCULAR-17 — Investigate relationships among centripetal force, radius, linear speed, angular speed and mass, and apply circular-motion principles to satellite orbits.

**Starter / learning task:** Use a labelled animation of a vehicle turning on a level circular track or a simulation of a satellite orbit. Learners identify the direction of velocity and the inward direction of centripetal acceleration/force. Explore how changing mass, speed or radius changes the required centripetal force. Use simulations rather than swinging heavy objects.

**Practice**
1. A 2 kg object moves at 4 m/s in a circle of radius 2 m. Calculate the centripetal force.  
   **Answer:** F = mv²/r = 2 × 4² ÷ 2 = 16 N, directed towards the centre.
2. A point on a rotating wheel has radius 0.5 m and angular speed 6 rad/s. Calculate its linear speed.  
   **Answer:** v = ωr = 6 × 0.5 = 3 m/s.
3. What provides the centripetal force for a satellite in a circular orbit around Earth?  
   **Answer:** Earth's gravitational attraction provides the inward centripetal force.

## Teaching and assessment notes

- State the ideal-model assumption that air resistance is negligible for standard projectile calculations.
- Resolve projectile motion into horizontal and vertical components; use consistent signs and SI units.
- Centripetal force is the net inward force, not an additional force separate from the physical interaction providing it.
- Use F = mv²/r and v = ωr with units and direction clearly stated.
- For satellite orbits, identify gravity as the centripetal interaction.
- Prefer simulations, video analysis and teacher-approved low-energy demonstrations.

## Official alignment

IPST, *Physics curriculum-use manual for upper-secondary additional Physics*, M4 learning outcomes 16–17; cross-checked with the IPST official comparison of Physics learning outcomes and Physical Science indicators, which maps Group 1 outcomes 16–17 to M4.

# M4 Physics — Simple Machines

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017)  
**Subject / grade:** Physics, M4  
**Official coverage:** Additional Physics learning outcome 13  
**Coverage:** 1 lesson starter; 3 practice questions with answer keys

## Lesson — Work, efficiency and mechanical advantage

**Objective:** M4-PHY-SIMPLE-MACHINES-13 — Explain the operation of selected simple machines using work and mechanical equilibrium; calculate efficiency and mechanical advantage.

**Starter / learning task:** Display examples or diagrams of a lever, pulley, inclined plane, wedge, screw and wheel-and-axle. Ask learners how each device changes the size or direction of an applied force. Compare input work with useful output work. Introduce:
- Efficiency (%) = (useful output work ÷ input work) × 100
- Mechanical advantage (MA) = load force ÷ effort force

Discuss why real machines have efficiency below 100%, including friction and deformation. Use safe classroom models or diagrams; do not ask learners to lift heavy loads.

**Practice**
1. A machine receives 200 J of input work and produces 150 J of useful output work. Calculate its efficiency.  
   **Answer:** Efficiency = (150 ÷ 200) × 100 = 75%.
2. A lever lifts a 300 N load using an effort force of 75 N. Calculate its mechanical advantage.  
   **Answer:** MA = load ÷ effort = 300 ÷ 75 = 4.
3. A pulley system has an efficiency of 80% and receives 500 J of input work. Calculate its useful output work.  
   **Answer:** Useful output work = 0.80 × 500 = 400 J.

## Teaching and assessment notes

- Distinguish mechanical advantage from efficiency: MA compares forces, while efficiency compares useful output work with input work.
- In ideal calculations, account for the assumptions stated in the question; real machines lose energy through friction and other effects.
- Connect levers and pulley systems to mechanical equilibrium and force balance.
- Use SI units and show the ratio before converting efficiency to a percentage.
- Use light, teacher-approved models and never use unstable or heavy loads.

## Official alignment

IPST, *Physics curriculum-use manual for upper-secondary additional Physics*, M4 learning outcome 13. The official outcome register places this outcome with the M4 mechanics sequence.

# M4 Physics — Work, Energy and Conservation of Mechanical Energy

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017)  
**Subject / grade:** Physics, M4  
**Official coverage in this batch:** Additional Physics learning outcomes 11–12  
**Coverage:** 2 lesson starters; 6 practice questions with answer keys  
**Use:** Teacher planning resource. Use low-risk classroom investigations, simulations and supplied datasets. State units and sign conventions clearly.

## Lesson 1 — Work, energy and the work-energy relationship

**Objective:** M4-PHY-WORK-11 — Explain and calculate kinetic energy, gravitational potential energy and elastic potential energy; investigate relationships between work and changes in these forms of energy; and apply the work-energy theorem.

**Starter / learning task:** Compare pushing an empty trolley and a loaded trolley through the same distance. Ask what changes when the applied force or displacement changes. Learners identify when a force does positive, negative or zero work. Use a spring scale and a small trolley only if available and teacher approved; otherwise analyse a supplied force–displacement dataset. Connect the area under a force–displacement graph to work for a constant or varying force.

**Practice**
1. A 10 N horizontal force moves a box 3 m in the direction of the force. Calculate the work done.
   **Answer:** W = Fd = 10 × 3 = 30 J.
2. A 2 kg object moves at 4 m/s. Calculate its kinetic energy.
   **Answer:** KE = ½mv² = ½ × 2 × 4² = 16 J.
3. A resultant force does 25 J of work on an object. Its kinetic energy increases by 25 J. Which principle does this illustrate?
   **Answer:** The work-energy theorem: the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy.

## Lesson 2 — Conservation of mechanical energy

**Objective:** M4-PHY-ENERGY-12 — Explain conservation of mechanical energy when only conservative forces do work, and calculate quantities in motion using conservation of mechanical energy.

**Starter / learning task:** Show a ball at the top and bottom of a ramp. Ask learners how gravitational potential energy and kinetic energy change as the ball moves. Use a diagram or simulation to compare total mechanical energy at different heights. Discuss how friction transfers some mechanical energy to thermal energy, so mechanical energy is conserved only under the appropriate conditions.

**Practice**
1. State the law of conservation of mechanical energy.
   **Answer:** When only conservative forces do work, the total kinetic and potential energy of a system remains constant.
2. A 1 kg object is raised 5 m. Taking g = 10 m/s², calculate its gain in gravitational potential energy.
   **Answer:** ΔGPE = mgh = 1 × 10 × 5 = 50 J.
3. An object starts from rest and falls 2 m. Ignore air resistance and take g = 10 m/s². Find its speed just before reaching the ground.
   **Answer:** mgh = ½mv², so v = √(2gh) = √40 ≈ 6.32 m/s.

## Teaching and assessment notes

- Distinguish work done by an individual force from net work done by the resultant force.
- Use joules for work and energy; show all substitutions and units.
- Clarify that gravitational potential energy depends on the chosen reference level.
- Include elastic potential energy only with an appropriate spring model and within its safe elastic range.
- Explain that friction and air resistance transfer mechanical energy to other forms; do not claim mechanical energy is conserved in those cases.
- Avoid launching projectiles or using heavy suspended masses. Use diagrams, simulations or teacher-controlled low-energy demonstrations.

# M4 Physics — Work and Average Power

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017)  
**Subject / grade:** Physics, M4  
**Official coverage:** Additional Physics learning outcome 10  
**Coverage:** 1 lesson starter; 3 practice questions with answer keys  
**Use:** Teacher planning resource; use diagrams, low-energy demonstrations or supplied datasets.

## Lesson — Work and average power

**Objective:** M4-PHY-WORK-10 — Analyse and calculate work done by a constant force using the work equation and the area under a force–displacement graph, and explain and calculate average power.

**Starter / learning task:** Present two learners moving the same box through the same distance, but one takes less time. Ask whether they do different amounts of work and who has greater power. Review the angle between force and displacement, then connect work to the area under a force–displacement graph. Use a supplied graph or a safe, light classroom object rather than heavy loads.

**Practice**
1. A 20 N force acts in the direction of motion and moves an object 4 m. Calculate the work done.
   **Answer:** W = Fd = 20 × 4 = 80 J.
2. A force of 15 N moves an object 5 m at an angle of 60° to the displacement. Calculate the work done by the force.
   **Answer:** W = Fd cos θ = 15 × 5 × cos 60° = 37.5 J.
3. A machine transfers 600 J of energy in 10 seconds. Calculate its average power.
   **Answer:** P = W/t = 600/10 = 60 W.

## Teaching and assessment notes

- Work depends on the component of force parallel to displacement: W = Fd cos θ.
- A force perpendicular to displacement does zero work on the object.
- For a force–displacement graph, work is the signed area under the graph.
- Average power is work done divided by elapsed time; use watts (W).
- Distinguish power from work: the same work completed in less time means greater average power.
- Use clear sign conventions and include SI units in every calculation.

## Official alignment

Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017), Additional Physics, M4 learning outcome 10; IPST Physics curriculum-use manual, printed page 112.

# M5 Physics — Simple Harmonic Motion

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017)  
**Subject / grade:** Physics, M5  
**Official coverage:** Additional Physics learning outcome 1  
**Coverage:** 1 lesson starter; 3 practice questions with answer keys  
**Use:** Teacher planning resource. Use simulations or low-energy spring and pendulum demonstrations with secure equipment.

## Lesson — Simple harmonic motion

**Objective:** M5-PHY-SHM-01 — Investigate and explain simple harmonic motion of an object attached to a spring and a simple pendulum, and calculate related quantities.

**Starter / learning task:** Show a spring-mass system or a pendulum moving back and forth. Ask learners to identify the equilibrium position, amplitude and period. Use a position–time graph or simulation to show that the restoring acceleration is directed towards equilibrium and is proportional to displacement for ideal SHM.

**Key relationships**
- Displacement: x = A cos(ωt + φ) or an equivalent sine form, depending on the chosen starting position
- Angular frequency: ω = 2πf = 2π/T
- Acceleration: a = −ω²x
- Maximum speed: vmax = ωA
- Spring-mass period: T = 2π√(m/k)
- Simple-pendulum period for small oscillations: T = 2π√(L/g)

**Practice**
1. An object completes 20 oscillations in 10 seconds. Calculate its frequency and period.
   **Answer:** f = 20/10 = 2 Hz; T = 1/f = 0.5 s.
2. A spring-mass system has a mass of 0.25 kg and a spring constant of 100 N/m. Calculate its period. Use π = 3.14.
   **Answer:** T = 2π√(m/k) = 2 × 3.14 × √(0.25/100) = 0.314 s (approximately).
3. A particle in SHM has angular frequency 4 rad/s and displacement +0.05 m from equilibrium. Calculate its acceleration.
   **Answer:** a = −ω²x = −(4²)(0.05) = −0.80 m/s². The negative sign means acceleration is directed towards equilibrium.

## Teaching and assessment notes

- Identify equilibrium position, displacement, amplitude, period, frequency and phase.
- The acceleration relation a = −ω²x shows that acceleration is proportional to displacement and opposite in direction.
- The spring-mass formula assumes an ideal spring and negligible damping.
- The simple-pendulum period formula is an approximation for small angular displacements.
- Distinguish frequency (Hz) from angular frequency (rad/s).
- Use a simulation or teacher-controlled demonstration; secure all spring and pendulum equipment and keep oscillation amplitudes small.

# M5 Physics — Natural Frequency and Resonance

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017)  
**Subject / grade:** Physics, M5  
**Official coverage:** Additional Physics learning outcome 2  
**Coverage:** 1 lesson starter; 3 practice questions with answer keys

## Lesson — Natural frequency and resonance

**Objective:** M5-PHY-RESONANCE-02 — Explain the natural frequency of an object and resonance when an external driving frequency matches the natural frequency, increasing the oscillation amplitude.

**Starter / learning task:** Use a pendulum or simulation. First allow it to oscillate freely to observe its natural frequency. Then apply small periodic pushes at a frequency below, above and close to its natural frequency. Compare amplitudes. Explain that resonance occurs when the driving frequency matches the natural frequency, producing a large response. Discuss why engineers consider resonance in bridges, buildings and machines.

**Practice**
1. What is the natural frequency of an oscillator?
   **Answer:** The frequency at which it oscillates freely after being displaced and released, under the given conditions.
2. A swing has a natural frequency of 0.5 Hz. At what driving frequency is resonance expected in the idealised case?
   **Answer:** 0.5 Hz.
3. Why can resonance be dangerous in a bridge or machine?
   **Answer:** Repeated driving near the natural frequency can produce large oscillation amplitudes, causing excessive vibration or structural damage.

## Teaching and assessment notes

- Distinguish free oscillation from forced oscillation.
- The natural frequency depends on the physical system; for an ideal simple pendulum at small amplitude it depends on length and gravitational field strength.
- Resonance is a large-amplitude response near the natural frequency, not simply any oscillation.
- Real systems have damping, which limits the amplitude.
- Demonstrate using low-energy, teacher-controlled equipment or a simulation. Do not use heavy suspended masses or attempt to induce structural resonance in real buildings or bridges.

# SOURCE PACK 10: M5-physics-wave-properties-teacher-starter-pack.md

# M5 Physics — Wave Properties and Behaviour

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017)  
**Subject / grade:** Physics, M5  
**Official coverage:** Additional Physics learning outcome 3  
**Coverage:** 1 lesson starter; 3 practice questions with answer keys

## Lesson — Wave motion and properties

**Objective:** M5-PHY-WAVES-03 — Explain wave motion as energy transfer, distinguish wave types, describe wave properties, explain Huygens principle and superposition, and calculate wave speed, frequency and wavelength.

**Starter / learning task:** Send a pulse along a rope or use a ripple-tank simulation. Ask learners whether the medium travels with the pulse. Track one marked point on the rope to distinguish particle motion from wave propagation. Introduce transverse and longitudinal waves, then label amplitude, wavelength, crest, trough and period.

**Key relationships**
- Wave speed: v = fλ
- Frequency and period: f = 1/T
- For a travelling wave, energy is transferred while particles of a mechanical medium oscillate about equilibrium.
- Huygens principle: each point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets; the new wavefront is their common envelope.
- Superposition: the resultant displacement is the algebraic sum of the individual wave displacements.

**Practice**
1. A wave has frequency 5 Hz and wavelength 2 m. Calculate its speed.
   **Answer:** v = fλ = 5 × 2 = 10 m/s.
2. A wave completes 12 cycles in 3 seconds. Calculate its frequency and period.
   **Answer:** f = 12/3 = 4 Hz; T = 1/4 = 0.25 s.
3. Explain the difference between a transverse and a longitudinal mechanical wave.
   **Answer:** In a transverse wave, particles oscillate perpendicular to the direction of propagation; in a longitudinal wave, particles oscillate parallel to it.

## Teaching and assessment notes

- A mechanical wave requires a medium; an electromagnetic wave can propagate through a vacuum.
- Do not confuse the motion of the wave with the oscillation of particles in the medium.
- Wave speed depends on the medium; frequency is set by the source.
- Reinforce SI units: metres (m), seconds (s), hertz (Hz) and metres per second (m/s).
- Use simulations or a light rope/spring demonstration; keep equipment clear of faces and walkways.

IPST, *Physics Curriculum-use Manual for Additional Science Courses*, revised 2017, M5 learning outcome 3, printed page 130: https://www.scimath.org/ebook-physics/item/8437-2-2560-2551

# SOURCE PACK 11: M5-physics-sound-hearing-teacher-starter-pack.md

# M5 Physics — Sound, Hearing and Sound Phenomena
## Official alignment
Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017), Additional Physics, M5 outcomes 6–7. IPST Physics curriculum-use manual, printed pp. 136 and 138.

## Outcome 6 — Sound intensity, sound level and hearing
**Learning objective:** Explain sound intensity and sound level, their relationship to source power and distance, the role of frequency and the ear in hearing, sound quality, and the effects and prevention of noise pollution; calculate sound intensity and sound level.

**Lesson starter (10 minutes):** Play two short, safe audio samples at low volume. Ask students what makes one sound seem louder or higher-pitched. Introduce intensity, decibels, frequency, hearing range and sound quality. Never expose students to loud sound.

**Teach and practise:** Distinguish intensity (power per unit area) from sound level (logarithmic measure in dB); use supplied datasets to compare distance and source power; identify ear structures and the effect of prolonged exposure; investigate noise pollution and prevention.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. A source emits 2 W uniformly through an area of 4 m². Find intensity. **Answer:** I = P/A = 0.5 W m⁻².
2. If intensity is 10⁻⁶ W m⁻² and the reference intensity is 10⁻¹² W m⁻², find sound level. **Answer:** β = 10 log₁₀(I/I₀) = 60 dB.
3. Give two ways to reduce harmful noise exposure. **Answer:** Reduce source volume/duration; increase distance or use suitable hearing protection.

## Outcome 7 — Resonance, beats, standing waves and Doppler effect
**Learning objective:** Investigate resonance of air in a pipe open at one end; explain beats, standing sound waves, the Doppler effect and sonic shock waves; calculate related quantities and describe practical applications.

**Lesson starter (10 minutes):** Use a simulation or low-volume audio demonstration to compare two nearby frequencies. Students describe the repeating rise and fall in loudness, then connect it to beats.

**Teach and practise:** Model the fundamental and harmonics in an open-closed pipe; explain beats as the difference between two close frequencies; describe nodes and antinodes in standing waves; use diagrams to explain apparent frequency change when source/listener move; distinguish a sonic boom from ordinary sound.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. Two tones have frequencies 256 Hz and 260 Hz. Find beat frequency. **Answer:** 4 Hz.
2. An open-closed pipe has length 0.25 m and sound speed 340 m s⁻¹. Find its fundamental frequency using f = v/(4L). **Answer:** 340 Hz.
3. What causes the Doppler effect? **Answer:** Relative motion of source and observer changes the observed frequency.

## Safety and assessment
Use simulations, diagrams and low-volume recordings. Do not use high-volume sound, expose students to noise hazards, or conduct unsafe demonstrations. Assess with labelled wave diagrams, short calculations and explanations using evidence.

# SOURCE PACK 12: M5-physics-light-optics-teacher-starter-pack.md

# M5 Physics — Light: Interference, Diffraction and Natural Phenomena
## Official alignment
Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017), Additional Physics, M5 outcomes 8 and 12. IPST Physics curriculum-use manual, printed pp. 141 and 147.

## Outcome 8 — Interference and diffraction of light
**Learning objective:** Explain and investigate interference and diffraction of light using a double slit, diffraction grating and single slit; interpret fringe and diffraction patterns and calculate related quantities.

**Lesson starter (10 minutes):** Show a teacher-prepared image of a double-slit fringe pattern and a single-slit diffraction pattern. Ask students to identify repeated bright and dark regions and propose how waves explain them.

**Teach and practise:** Explain coherent sources, constructive/destructive interference, fringe spacing, diffraction through a narrow slit and grating maxima. Use diagrams and simulated data to connect wavelength, slit spacing and screen distance.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. What condition produces a bright fringe in a double-slit experiment? **Answer:** The path difference is an integer multiple of the wavelength, mλ.
2. Light of wavelength 600 nm passes through slits separated by 0.30 mm. The screen is 2.0 m away. Find the fringe spacing. **Answer:** Δy = λL/d = (600×10⁻⁹×2.0)/(0.30×10⁻³) = 0.004 m = 4.0 mm.
3. Why does a narrow single slit produce a broad diffraction pattern? **Answer:** Diffraction becomes more pronounced when slit width is comparable to the wavelength.

## Outcome 12 — Natural phenomena involving light
**Learning objective:** Explain rainbows, halos, mirages and the changing colours of the sky using reflection, refraction, dispersion and total internal reflection where appropriate.

**Lesson starter (10 minutes):** Present photographs of a rainbow, halo and mirage. Students sort observations into possible explanations before the teacher introduces refraction, dispersion and reflection.

**Teach and practise:** Trace light paths through water droplets for primary/secondary rainbows; explain ice-crystal refraction/reflection in halos; describe mirages using layers of air with different refractive indices; explain sky colour through wavelength-dependent scattering.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. Which process separates white sunlight into component colours in a rainbow? **Answer:** Dispersion caused by wavelength-dependent refraction in water droplets.
2. What causes a mirage near a hot road? **Answer:** Refraction through air layers with changing temperature and refractive index, sometimes with total internal reflection.
3. Why does the clear daytime sky usually appear blue? **Answer:** Shorter wavelengths are scattered more strongly by air molecules (Rayleigh scattering).

## Safety and assessment
Use simulations, diagrams and teacher-prepared images. Never direct students to look at the Sun or use unfiltered lasers. Assess ray diagrams, pattern interpretation and short calculations.

# SOURCE PACK 13: M5-physics-geometrical-optics-teacher-starter-pack.md

# M5 Physics — Geometrical Optics: Mirrors, Refraction and Lenses

## Official alignment
Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017), Additional Physics, M5 outcomes 9–11. IPST Physics curriculum-use manual, printed pp. 142–146.

## Outcome 9 — Reflection and image formation by mirrors
**Learning objective:** Investigate and explain reflection using the law of reflection; draw reflected rays and determine image position, size and type for plane and spherical mirrors; calculate related quantities and explain everyday applications.

**Lesson starter (10 minutes):** Show a plane mirror, a spoon and a simple ray diagram. Ask students to compare the images and predict how changing the mirror's curvature changes the image.

**Teach and practise:** Establish that the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, measured from the normal. Construct ray diagrams for plane, concave and convex mirrors. Practise the mirror equation and magnification with sign conventions stated before calculation.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. A ray strikes a plane mirror at 35° to the normal. What is its angle of reflection? **Answer:** 35°.
2. What are two properties of the image in a plane mirror? **Answer:** It is virtual and upright, the same size as the object, laterally inverted and the same distance behind the mirror as the object is in front.
3. Which spherical mirror gives a wider field of view and is commonly used as a vehicle side mirror? **Answer:** A convex mirror; it forms an upright, reduced virtual image and provides a wider field of view.

## Outcome 10 — Refraction and total internal reflection
**Learning objective:** Investigate and explain the relationship between refractive index, angle of incidence and angle of refraction; explain real and apparent depth, critical angle and total internal reflection; calculate related quantities.

**Lesson starter (10 minutes):** Place a pencil partly in a clear water container. Students sketch what they observe and propose why the pencil appears displaced.

**Teach and practise:** Use Snell's law, n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂, with angles measured from the normal. Compare real and apparent depth. Explain that total internal reflection occurs only when light travels from higher to lower refractive index and the incidence angle exceeds the critical angle.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. Light travels from air into glass. Does it bend towards or away from the normal? **Answer:** Towards the normal, because its speed decreases in the higher-index medium.
2. What is the critical angle condition for total internal reflection? **Answer:** Light must travel from higher to lower refractive index, and the incidence angle must be greater than the critical angle.
3. A material has refractive index 1.5 and is surrounded by air. Find its critical angle. **Answer:** sin θc = 1/1.5; θc ≈ 41.8°.

## Outcome 11 — Thin lenses and image formation
**Learning objective:** Draw ray diagrams and determine image position, size and type for thin lenses; use the thin-lens equation and magnification relationship to calculate related quantities and explain practical applications.

**Lesson starter (10 minutes):** Use a magnifying glass to project an image of a distant window onto a screen. Ask students why the image can be sharp only at a particular screen position.

**Teach and practise:** Compare converging and diverging lenses. Construct principal rays and use the thin-lens equation 1/f = 1/do + 1/di and magnification m = hi/ho = −di/do, making the sign convention explicit. Connect lenses to magnifiers, cameras and microscopes.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. Which lens is thicker at the centre and converges parallel incoming rays? **Answer:** A convex (converging) lens.
2. A converging lens has focal length 10 cm and an object is 30 cm away. Find the image distance. **Answer:** 1/10 = 1/30 + 1/di, so di = 15 cm.
3. In the usual real-is-positive convention, what does a negative image distance indicate? **Answer:** A virtual image on the same side of the lens as the object.

## Safety and assessment
Use unbreakable mirrors or teacher-managed optical equipment. Do not direct students to look at the Sun through lenses or mirrors, and do not use concentrated sunlight as a classroom demonstration. Use low-power, enclosed ray boxes or simulations. Assess ray diagrams, explanation of optical conditions and calculations with clearly stated sign conventions.

# SOURCE PACK 14: M5-physics-water-waves-sound-propagation-teacher-starter-pack.md

# M5 Physics — Water Waves and Sound Production

## Official alignment
Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017), Additional Physics, M5 outcomes 4 and 5. The IPST Physics curriculum-use manual's outcome register identifies outcome 4 as surface-water-wave behaviour and outcome 5 as sound production, propagation and wave properties. The official manual describes reflection, refraction, interference and diffraction of waves and the relationship between sound speed and air temperature. Source: https://www.scimath.org/e-books/8437/flippingbook/35/

## Outcome 4 — Behaviour of surface water waves
**Learning objective:** Observe and explain reflection, refraction, interference and diffraction of surface water waves; use wavefronts and ray directions to describe changes at boundaries and openings; calculate related quantities.

**Lesson starter (10 minutes):** Show a ripple-tank simulation or a shallow tray demonstration. Ask students to sketch wavefronts approaching a straight barrier, crossing into a shallow region and passing through a narrow gap.

**Teach and practise:** Explain reflection using equal angles of incidence and reflection. Explain refraction as a change in wave speed and wavelength when depth changes while frequency remains constant. Use two coherent sources to introduce constructive and destructive interference; compare diffraction through wide and narrow gaps. Keep the focus on surface water waves and avoid conflating these with the later light-interference outcome.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. A water wave strikes a straight barrier at 30° to the normal. What is its angle of reflection? **Answer:** 30°.
2. A wave enters a region where its speed decreases while frequency stays constant. What happens to its wavelength? **Answer:** It decreases because λ = v/f.
3. When is diffraction most noticeable at a gap? **Answer:** When the gap width is comparable to the wavelength; diffraction is also more pronounced for narrower openings relative to wavelength.

## Outcome 5 — Production and propagation of sound
**Learning objective:** Explain how vibrating sources produce sound and how sound propagates through a medium; relate particle displacement to pressure variations; explain sound speed in air and its dependence on temperature in degrees Celsius; describe sound as a mechanical longitudinal wave and calculate related quantities.

**Lesson starter (10 minutes):** Strike a tuning fork gently and hold it near a suspended lightweight paper strip without touching it. Ask what is vibrating and how the sound reaches a listener. Alternatively use a sound-wave simulation.

**Teach and practise:** A vibrating source creates compressions and rarefactions that travel through a material medium. Sound in air is a longitudinal mechanical wave: particle displacement is parallel to propagation, while pressure and density vary. Use v ≈ 331 + 0.6T m/s for air, where T is in °C, within ordinary classroom conditions. Reinforce v = fλ and distinguish particle oscillation from wave travel.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. Why can sound not travel through a vacuum? **Answer:** Sound is a mechanical wave and requires particles in a medium to transfer the disturbance.
2. Calculate the approximate speed of sound in air at 20°C using v = 331 + 0.6T. **Answer:** v = 331 + 0.6(20) = 343 m/s.
3. A sound wave in air has frequency 500 Hz and speed 343 m/s. Find its wavelength. **Answer:** λ = v/f = 343/500 = 0.686 m.

## Safety and assessment
Keep sound demonstrations at comfortable listening levels. Do not use explosive sound sources or ask students to place vibrating equipment against their ears. Water-wave demonstrations should use shallow, stable containers and dry floors promptly. Assess wavefront diagrams, explanations of particle motion and pressure variation, and calculations with correct units.

# M5 Physics — Nuclear Structure and Particle Physics

## Official alignment

Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017), Additional Physics, Group 4 outcomes **15 and 18**. The official IPST outcome-to-indicator comparison crosswalk explicitly labels these two outcomes as **M5**. They must not be assigned to M6 merely because nearby Group 4 outcomes are M6. Outcome 15 covers nuclear forces, stability and binding energy; outcome 18 covers particle-physics research and the Standard Model.

## Outcome 15 — Nuclear Forces, Stability and Binding Energy

**Objective:** Explain nuclear forces, nuclear stability and binding energy; calculate related quantities.

**Starter:** Show a simple chart of binding energy per nucleon for light, medium and heavy nuclei. Ask why some nuclear changes can release energy.

**Teach and practise:** Explain the role of the strong nuclear force in holding nucleons together, the competition with electrostatic repulsion among protons, mass defect and binding energy. Use \(E_b=\Delta mc^2\) and binding energy per nucleon \(E_b/A\). Emphasise that stability is not explained by proton repulsion alone.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. A nucleus has a mass defect of \(3.0\times10^{-29}\) kg. Find its binding energy using \(c=3.0\times10^8\) m/s. **Answer:** \(E_b=\Delta mc^2=2.7\times10^{-12}\) J.
2. A nucleus has binding energy 160 MeV and 20 nucleons. Find binding energy per nucleon. **Answer:** \(160/20=8.0\) MeV per nucleon.
3. What is the main role of the strong nuclear force in a nucleus? **Answer:** It provides an attractive interaction between nucleons at very short range, helping bind the nucleus despite electrostatic repulsion between protons.

## Outcome 18 — Particle Physics Research and the Standard Model

**Objective:** Explain particle-physics research, the Standard Model and applications of particle-physics research.

**Starter:** Show a simplified particle-family chart with quarks, leptons and force carriers. Ask what experiments can reveal about matter too small to see directly.

**Teach and practise:** Explain that protons and neutrons contain quarks; quarks interact through the strong interaction, mediated by gluons. Introduce leptons and force-carrier bosons at an age-appropriate level. Describe the Standard Model as a framework for elementary particles and their interactions, and connect accelerator research to medical and imaging technologies.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. What fundamental constituents make up protons and neutrons? **Answer:** Quarks (two up and one down quark in a proton; one up and two down quarks in a neutron).
2. Which particle mediates the strong interaction between quarks? **Answer:** The gluon.
3. Give one practical application of technologies developed through particle-physics research. **Answer:** Medical applications such as accelerator-based cancer radiotherapy or positron-emission tomography (PET) imaging.

## Safety and assessment

Use diagrams, simulations, videos and teacher-provided datasets. No radioactive sources, particle beams, high-voltage equipment or improvised experiments are used by students. Assess explanation of nuclear stability, mass-defect calculations, correct identification of quarks/leptons/force carriers and evidence-based discussion of research applications.

# SOURCE PACK 16: M5-physics-electrostatics-dc-electricity-colour-vision-teacher-starter-pack.md

# M5 Physics — Electrostatics, DC Electricity and Colour Vision

## Official alignment

Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017), Additional Physics:
- **Group 3, M5 outcomes 1–11:** electrostatics, Coulomb's law, electric fields and potential, capacitors, current, resistance, DC circuits, emf, power and renewable electricity. The M5 sequence is printed on pp. 31–35; the separate M6 Group 3 sequence restarts at outcome 1.
- **Group 2, M5 outcome 13:** colour vision, object colours, pigment/light mixing and colour blindness, printed p. 30.

These are separate content-group outcomes. The group number is not the grade level.

## Group 3 — Outcome 1: Electric Charging by Friction and Induction

**Objective:** Explain how friction transfers electrons and how electrostatic induction redistributes charge; apply conservation of electric charge.

**Starter:** Rub a balloon on a clean dry cloth and observe attraction to small paper pieces. Ask how a neutral object can become charged.

**Teach and practise:** Distinguish electron transfer by friction from charge redistribution by induction. Use diagrams to track positive and negative charge; emphasise conservation of total charge.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. Which particles usually move between objects during charging by friction? **Answer:** Electrons.
2. A neutral object gains electrons. What is its net charge? **Answer:** Negative.
3. What happens to charges in a neutral metal object when a negatively charged rod is brought near without touching it? **Answer:** Electrons are repelled toward the far side, leaving the near side relatively positive; total charge remains zero.

## Group 3 — Outcome 2: Coulomb’s Law

**Objective:** Explain the direction and magnitude of forces between point charges and calculate electric force using Coulomb’s law.

**Starter:** Draw two positive charges, then a positive and a negative charge. Ask which pairs attract and which repel.

**Teach and practise:** Use \(F=k|q_1q_2|/r^2\); discuss attraction/repulsion, inverse-square distance and SI units.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. Charges of 2 μC and 3 μC are 0.30 m apart in air. Find the force magnitude (\(k=9.0\times10^9\)). **Answer:** \(F=0.60\) N.
2. What happens to the force if the distance between two point charges doubles? **Answer:** It becomes one quarter as large.
3. Do two negative point charges attract or repel? **Answer:** Repel.

## Group 3 — Outcome 3: Electric Fields and Electric Force

**Objective:** Explain electric fields and force on a charge; calculate fields from point charges and determine resultant fields using vector addition.

**Starter:** Show electric-field-line diagrams around positive and negative charges. Ask what the arrows represent.

**Teach and practise:** Use \(E=F/q\) and \(E=k|Q|/r^2\); field direction is the force direction on a positive test charge. Combine fields as vectors.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. A 2 μC test charge experiences a 0.04 N force. Find the field magnitude. **Answer:** \(E=0.04/(2\times10^{-6})=2.0\times10^4\) N/C.
2. In which direction do electric-field lines point around a positive point charge? **Answer:** Radially outward.
3. Two equal electric-field vectors act in opposite directions at a point. What is the resultant field? **Answer:** Zero.

## Group 3 — Outcome 4: Electric Potential and Potential Difference

**Objective:** Explain electric potential energy, electric potential and potential difference; calculate values for point charges and uniform fields.

**Starter:** Compare electric potential to gravitational height as an energy-per-unit concept. Ask what work is needed to move a charge.

**Teach and practise:** Use \(V=kQ/r\), \(U=qV\) and \(\Delta V=Ed\) in a uniform field, with sign and direction conventions stated.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. Find the potential 0.20 m from a +2 μC point charge (\(k=9.0\times10^9\)). **Answer:** \(V=90,000\) V.
2. A 2 C charge moves through a potential difference of 5 V. Find the change in potential energy magnitude. **Answer:** 10 J.
3. What is potential difference measured in? **Answer:** Volts (J/C).

## Group 3 — Outcome 5: Capacitance, Stored Energy and Capacitor Networks

**Objective:** Explain capacitor construction, calculate capacitance and stored energy, and determine equivalent capacitance in series and parallel.

**Starter:** Show a labelled capacitor diagram and ask what the insulating layer between the conductors does.

**Teach and practise:** Use \(C=Q/\Delta V\), \(U=\frac12 Q\Delta V\); for parallel capacitors add capacitances, while for series use reciprocal addition.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. A capacitor stores 60 μC at 12 V. Find its capacitance. **Answer:** \(C=5\) μF.
2. Two capacitors of 2 μF and 3 μF are connected in parallel. Find the equivalent capacitance. **Answer:** 5 μF.
3. What happens to equivalent capacitance when identical capacitors are connected in series? **Answer:** It is less than the capacitance of one capacitor; for two identical capacitors it is half.

## Group 3 — Outcome 6: Applications of Electrostatics

**Objective:** Explain electrostatic applications such as dust removal, spray painting and photocopying, and explain electrostatic hazards such as lightning.

**Starter:** Show a photocopier or electrostatic precipitator diagram. Ask how electric charge can help move particles.

**Teach and practise:** Connect attraction/repulsion and induced charge to electrostatic precipitators, spray painting and photocopying; discuss lightning as a discharge and safe shelter practices.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. Why can an electrostatic precipitator remove dust from exhaust gas? **Answer:** Charged dust particles are attracted to oppositely charged collection plates.
2. Why can electrostatic spray painting produce an even coating? **Answer:** Charged paint droplets are attracted to the oppositely charged or grounded surface and repel one another, helping spread the coating.
3. What is a safe action during a thunderstorm outdoors? **Answer:** Move to a substantial enclosed building or hard-topped vehicle; avoid open fields, isolated trees and water.

## Group 3 — Outcome 7: Electron Drift and Electric Current

**Objective:** Explain electron drift in a conductor and calculate current using \(I=neAv_d\).

**Starter:** Show a conductor diagram with electron drift opposite to conventional current. Ask why current direction is defined differently from electron motion.

**Teach and practise:** Define charge-carrier density, cross-sectional area and drift speed. Use \(I=neAv_d\) and distinguish drift speed from random thermal motion.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. If electron drift speed increases while all other quantities stay constant, what happens to current? **Answer:** Current increases in direct proportion.
2. A wire has \(n=8.0\times10^{28}\) m⁻³, \(A=1.0\times10^{-6}\) m², \(v_d=1.0\times10^{-4}\) m/s and \(e=1.6\times10^{-19}\) C. Find current. **Answer:** \(I=1.28\) A.
3. What is the direction of conventional current relative to electron drift in a metal? **Answer:** Opposite.

## Group 3 — Outcome 8: Ohm’s Law, Resistivity and Resistor Networks

**Objective:** Investigate Ohm’s law; calculate resistance from resistivity, length and area; determine series and parallel equivalent resistance.

**Starter:** Show a simple low-voltage circuit with a resistor and meter readings. Ask what happens to current when voltage increases at constant temperature.

**Teach and practise:** Use \(V=IR\), \(R=\rho L/A\), series \(R_s=R_1+R_2+...\), and parallel reciprocal addition.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. A 12 V supply is connected across a 4 Ω resistor. Find current. **Answer:** \(I=3\) A.
2. A wire has resistivity \(2.0\times10^{-8}\) Ωm, length 2 m and area \(1.0\times10^{-6}\) m². Find resistance. **Answer:** \(R=0.04\) Ω.
3. Find the equivalent resistance of 6 Ω and 3 Ω resistors in parallel. **Answer:** 2 Ω.

## Group 3 — Outcome 9: EMF, Electrical Energy and Power

**Objective:** Explain DC-source emf and calculate terminal voltage, electrical energy and power, including internal resistance where appropriate.

**Starter:** Compare a battery's labelled voltage with a measured terminal voltage under load. Ask why they may differ.

**Teach and practise:** Use \(\varepsilon=V+Ir\), \(W=IVt\) and \(P=IV\); explain internal resistance and energy transfer.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. A 12 V source has internal resistance 1 Ω and supplies 2 A. Find terminal voltage. **Answer:** \(V=12-(2)(1)=10\) V.
2. A 6 V device draws 0.5 A for 10 minutes. Find electrical energy used. **Answer:** \(W=IVt=0.5\times6\times600=1800\) J.
3. Find the power of a device operating at 12 V and 2 A. **Answer:** 24 W.

## Group 3 — Outcome 10: Series and Parallel Battery Circuits

**Objective:** Calculate equivalent emf and internal resistance for series and parallel battery combinations and solve DC circuits with resistors.

**Starter:** Draw two identical batteries in series and then in parallel. Ask what changes and what stays the same.

**Teach and practise:** For identical cells, series connection adds emf and internal resistance; parallel connection keeps emf the same and reduces equivalent internal resistance. Apply \(I=\varepsilon/(R+r)\) to simple circuits.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. Two identical 1.5 V cells are connected in series. Find total emf. **Answer:** 3.0 V.
2. Two identical 1.5 V cells, each with internal resistance 0.4 Ω, are connected in parallel. Find equivalent internal resistance. **Answer:** 0.2 Ω; emf remains 1.5 V.
3. A 6 V battery with internal resistance 1 Ω is connected to a 5 Ω resistor. Find current. **Answer:** \(I=6/(5+1)=1\) A.

## Group 3 — Outcome 11: Renewable Electricity Technologies

**Objective:** Explain how solar, wind, hydroelectric and other renewable sources can be converted into electricity and compare relevant technologies.

**Starter:** Show pictures of solar panels, wind turbines and a hydroelectric station. Ask what energy transformation occurs in each.

**Teach and practise:** Trace energy conversions and compare resource availability, intermittency, storage, location and environmental impacts. Separate energy source from generator and grid systems.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. What energy conversion occurs in a photovoltaic cell? **Answer:** Radiant energy from sunlight is converted directly into electrical energy.
2. How does a wind turbine generate electricity? **Answer:** Wind turns blades and a shaft, driving a generator that converts mechanical energy into electrical energy.
3. Give one advantage and one limitation of solar electricity. **Answer:** Advantage: low operational emissions and scalable generation. Limitation: output varies with sunlight and may require storage or backup.

## Group 2 — Outcome 13: Colour Vision, Colour Mixing and Colour Blindness

**Objective:** Explain how light and pigments determine object colour, compare additive light mixing with subtractive pigment mixing, and explain colour-vision deficiency in terms of cone cells.

**Starter:** Shine red and green light onto a screen (or use a simulation), then compare with mixing red and green paints. Ask why the results differ.

**Teach and practise:** Explain that object colour depends on reflected/transmitted light and pigments selectively absorb wavelengths. Additive RGB light mixing differs from subtractive pigment mixing. Introduce cone cells and colour-vision deficiency respectfully and factually.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. What colour is produced when red and green light are mixed at suitable intensities? **Answer:** Yellow light.
2. Why does a green leaf usually appear green in white light? **Answer:** Its pigments absorb more strongly in other parts of the visible spectrum and reflect/transmit more green light.
3. Which retinal cells are primarily responsible for colour vision? **Answer:** Cone photoreceptors.

## Safety and assessment

Use only low-voltage, current-limited classroom circuits with approved batteries and components. Do not use mains electricity, improvised high-voltage sources or lightning demonstrations. Capacitors must be appropriately rated and safely discharged by the teacher before handling. Static demonstrations should use ordinary safe classroom materials. Use simulations and diagrams for hazardous electrostatic equipment. Colour-vision activities must not label or diagnose students; use optional simulations and respectful scientific language.
