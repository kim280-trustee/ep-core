# M6 Physics — Modern, Atomic and Nuclear Physics

## Outcome 10 — Planck Hypothesis, Bohr Atom and Hydrogen Spectrum

**Objective:** Explain Planck quantisation, the Bohr model and hydrogen spectral lines; calculate related quantities.

**Starter:** Display a hydrogen line spectrum and a continuous spectrum. Ask why hydrogen produces distinct lines rather than every colour.

**Teach and practise:** Introduce photon energy \(E=hf\), quantised energy levels in the Bohr model, and photon emission/absorption during transitions. Use \(\Delta E=hf=hc/\lambda\).

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. A photon has frequency \(6.0\times10^{14}\) Hz. Find its energy using \(h=6.63\times10^{-34}\) J·s. **Answer:** \(E=hf=3.98\times10^{-19}\) J.
2. Does an electron emit or absorb a photon when it moves from a higher energy level to a lower one? **Answer:** It emits a photon; the photon energy equals the energy difference.
3. Why does hydrogen produce a line spectrum? **Answer:** Its electron can occupy only particular allowed energy levels, so transitions produce photons with specific energies/wavelengths.

## Outcome 11 — Photoelectric Effect

**Objective:** Explain the photoelectric effect and calculate photon energy, maximum photoelectron kinetic energy and metal work function.

**Starter:** Show a photoelectric-effect simulation with adjustable light frequency and intensity. Ask which control changes electron energy and which changes the number emitted.

**Teach and practise:** Use \(hf=W+K_{\max}\), threshold frequency \(f_0=W/h\), and distinguish intensity from frequency.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. Light of frequency \(8.0\times10^{14}\) Hz strikes a metal with work function \(3.0\times10^{-19}\) J. Find maximum kinetic energy (use \(h=6.63\times10^{-34}\) J·s). **Answer:** Photon energy = \(5.304\times10^{-19}\) J; \(K_{\max}=2.304\times10^{-19}\) J.
2. What happens if the light frequency is below the threshold frequency, even when intensity increases? **Answer:** No photoelectrons are emitted in the basic photoelectric model.
3. At a frequency above threshold, what does increasing light intensity mainly change? **Answer:** The number/rate of emitted photoelectrons, not the maximum kinetic energy.

## Outcome 12 — Wave-Particle Duality and de Broglie Wavelength

**Objective:** Explain wave-particle duality and calculate de Broglie wavelength from particle momentum.

**Starter:** Show an electron-diffraction pattern and ask why a particle beam can form an interference pattern.

**Teach and practise:** Use \(\lambda=h/p\), with momentum \(p=mv\) for a non-relativistic particle. Connect electron diffraction to wave-like behaviour.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. Find the de Broglie wavelength of a particle with momentum \(6.63\times10^{-24}\) kg·m/s. **Answer:** \(\lambda=h/p=1.0\times10^{-10}\) m.
2. What happens to de Broglie wavelength when momentum doubles? **Answer:** It halves.
3. What does wave-particle duality mean? **Answer:** Light and matter can show both wave-like and particle-like behaviour, depending on the experiment.

## Outcome 13 — Radioactivity and Alpha, Beta and Gamma Radiation

**Objective:** Explain radioactivity and distinguish alpha, beta and gamma radiation.

**Starter:** Display a teacher-provided diagram showing three radiation paths through an electric or magnetic field. Ask why their paths differ.

**Teach and practise:** Compare charge, relative mass and penetrating/ionising behaviour. Use nuclear equations to track mass number and atomic number. Treat beta-minus, beta-plus and gamma carefully as distinct processes.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. Which radiation is a helium nucleus with charge +2? **Answer:** Alpha radiation.
2. Which of alpha, beta and gamma is uncharged electromagnetic radiation? **Answer:** Gamma radiation.
3. In alpha decay, how do mass number and atomic number change? **Answer:** Mass number decreases by 4 and atomic number decreases by 2.

## Outcome 14 — Activity, Radioactive Decay and Half-Life

**Objective:** Calculate activity, remaining radioactive nuclei and half-life; interpret radioactive-decay evidence.

**Starter:** Use a decay simulation or prepared count-rate dataset. Ask why the count rate falls but does not usually reach zero immediately.

**Teach and practise:** Use \(A=\lambda N\), \(N=N_0e^{-\lambda t}\), and \(T_{1/2}=\ln 2/\lambda\). Explain that activity is measured in becquerels (Bq).

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. A sample has 8000 undecayed nuclei. How many remain after three half-lives? **Answer:** \(8000/2^3=1000\) nuclei.
2. A sample has a half-life of 5 days. What fraction remains after 15 days? **Answer:** \(1/8\), or 12.5%.
3. What does an activity of 250 Bq mean? **Answer:** An average of 250 nuclear decays occur each second.

## Outcome 16 — Nuclear Reactions, Fission and Fusion

**Objective:** Explain nuclear reactions, fission and fusion, and calculate nuclear energy from mass-energy equivalence.

**Starter:** Compare a diagram of a heavy nucleus splitting with two light nuclei joining. Ask what is different and what both processes can release.

**Teach and practise:** Balance nuclear equations by conserving nucleon number and charge. Use \(E=\Delta mc^2\); distinguish fission from fusion and avoid implying every nuclear reaction releases energy.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. A nuclear reaction has a mass decrease of \(2.0\times10^{-29}\) kg. Find the released energy using \(c=3.0\times10^8\) m/s. **Answer:** \(E=\Delta mc^2=1.8\times10^{-12}\) J.
2. What is nuclear fission? **Answer:** A heavy nucleus splits into two or more lighter nuclei, often releasing energy and neutrons.
3. What is nuclear fusion? **Answer:** Two light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus; under suitable conditions the reaction can release energy.

## Outcome 17 — Nuclear Energy, Radiation Applications and Protection

**Objective:** Explain uses of nuclear energy and radiation, associated hazards and appropriate radiation-protection principles.

**Starter:** Present three case cards: medical imaging/treatment, electricity generation and industrial inspection. Ask what benefit and safety concern belong to each.

**Teach and practise:** Discuss controlled uses of radiation and nuclear energy, exposure pathways, dose and risk. Introduce time, distance and shielding as general protection principles; explain that suitable shielding depends on radiation type and context.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. Give one medical use of ionising radiation. **Answer:** Diagnostic imaging with suitable radiation, or radiotherapy for cancer treatment under clinical control.
2. State three general ways to reduce unnecessary external radiation exposure. **Answer:** Reduce time near the source, increase distance, and use appropriate shielding.
3. Why must radioactive sources never be handled by students as an ordinary classroom activity? **Answer:** Exposure and contamination risks require licensed controls, trained supervision and approved facilities; use simulations or prepared datasets in class.

## Safety and assessment

**No student handling, opening, testing or improvised use of radioactive sources.** Do not use radioactive minerals, smoke detectors, luminous materials or unknown objects as classroom sources. Teach through simulations, teacher-provided datasets, diagrams and approved media only. No improvised nuclear demonstrations. Assess conceptual understanding, balanced nuclear equations, calculations with units, interpretation of decay data and safe decision-making.
