## Lesson 1 — Explain characteristics and organisation of living things

**Objective:** `M4-BIO-01`  
**Official source alignment:** Additional Biology, strand 1, M4 learning outcome 1  
**Learning outcome:** Explain how characteristics such as nutrition, energy use, growth, response, homeostasis, reproduction and adaptation support life; relate cells, tissues, organs and systems.

**Teaching activity:** Use diagrams, models, safe microscopy, supplied datasets and teacher-supervised demonstrations. Ask learners to explain evidence, compare mechanisms and show calculations where appropriate.

**Independent practice**

# M4 Biology — Genetics and Evolution Teacher Starter Pack

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017), Additional Biology strand 2  
**Subject:** BIOLOGY  
**Grade:** M4  
**Coverage:** 15 official additional-study learning outcomes, pp. 139–140. These are learning outcomes, not lower-secondary indicators.

Each lesson aligns to one official M4 outcome and includes three practice questions with answer keys. Use hypothetical family data and classroom-safe models. Do not ask learners to disclose personal genetic or medical information. Treat genetic conditions and biotechnology ethics respectfully and without stigmatising language.

## Lesson 1 — Analyse Mendel’s experiments

**Objective:** `M4-BIO-GEN-01`  
**Outcome:** Research Mendel’s experiments, explain the design and results, and summarise how evidence supported patterns of inheritance.

**Teaching activity:** Reconstruct a simple pea-plant cross with coloured counters; identify the question, controlled cross, observations and evidence.

**Practice**
1. Why did Mendel study pea plants?  
   **Answer:** They had clear contrasting traits, short generation times and could be cross-pollinated in controlled ways.
2. What is a true-breeding line?  
   **Answer:** A line that produces the same trait generation after generation when self-fertilised.
3. What did the reappearance of a recessive trait in F2 suggest?  
   **Answer:** Hereditary factors remain present even when not expressed in F1.

## Lesson 2 — Apply Mendel’s laws to inheritance probabilities

**Objective:** `M4-BIO-GEN-02`  
**Outcome:** Explain segregation and independent assortment; calculate genotype and phenotype probabilities in F1 and F2 generations.

**Teaching activity:** Model allele separation with counters, then use Punnett squares and probability rules for monohybrid and dihybrid crosses.

**Practice**
1. State the law of segregation.  
   **Answer:** The two alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation, so each gamete receives one allele.
2. Cross Aa × Aa. What is the genotype ratio?  
   **Answer:** 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa.
3. For complete dominance in Aa × Aa, what is the phenotype ratio?  
   **Answer:** 3 dominant : 1 recessive.

## Lesson 3 — Explain extensions of Mendelian inheritance

**Objective:** `M4-BIO-GEN-03`  
**Outcome:** Analyse inheritance patterns extending simple Mendelian models, including incomplete dominance, codominance, multiple alleles, polygenic inheritance and linkage.

**Teaching activity:** Compare model examples: flower colour, ABO blood groups (as a textbook model), quantitative traits and linked genes.

**Practice**
1. What is incomplete dominance?  
   **Answer:** The heterozygote has an intermediate phenotype.
2. What is codominance?  
   **Answer:** Both alleles are expressed in the heterozygote, as in the AB blood group.
3. What does it mean when genes are linked?  
   **Answer:** They are on the same chromosome and tend to be inherited together.

## Lesson 4 — Compare discontinuous and continuous variation

**Objective:** `M4-BIO-GEN-04`  
**Outcome:** Compare traits with distinct categories and traits showing a continuous range, including genetic and environmental contributions.

**Teaching activity:** Sort fictional trait datasets into categories and plot a continuous measurement as a frequency distribution.

**Practice**
1. Give an example of discontinuous variation.  
   **Answer:** ABO blood group.
2. Give an example of continuous variation.  
   **Answer:** Height in a population.
3. Why can continuous traits be influenced by the environment?  
   **Answer:** Environmental factors such as nutrition and conditions during development can affect their expression.

## Lesson 5 — Explain inheritance of genes on chromosomes

**Objective:** `M4-BIO-GEN-05`  
**Outcome:** Explain chromosomal gene transmission and distinguish autosomal and sex-linked inheritance.

**Teaching activity:** Use chromosome diagrams and fictional pedigrees to identify autosomal and X-linked patterns without making claims about real families.

**Practice**
1. What is an autosomal gene?  
   **Answer:** A gene located on a non-sex chromosome.
2. What is a sex-linked gene?  
   **Answer:** A gene located on a sex chromosome, commonly the X chromosome in classroom examples.
3. Why can X-linked recessive traits appear more often in males in typical XX/XY inheritance examples?  
   **Answer:** A male with one X chromosome has no second X allele to mask a recessive allele on that chromosome.

## Lesson 6 — Explain DNA structure, function and replication

**Objective:** `M4-BIO-GEN-06`  
**Outcome:** Describe genetic material, DNA chemical composition and structure, and DNA replication.

**Teaching activity:** Build a DNA ladder model, label nucleotide components and sequence complementary bases.

**Practice**
1. What are the three components of a DNA nucleotide?  
   **Answer:** Phosphate, deoxyribose sugar and a nitrogenous base.
2. Which bases pair in DNA?  
   **Answer:** Adenine with thymine; cytosine with guanine.
3. Why is DNA replication called semiconservative?  
   **Answer:** Each new DNA molecule contains one original strand and one newly synthesised strand.

## Lesson 7 — Explain protein synthesis and RNA roles

**Objective:** `M4-BIO-GEN-07`  
**Outcome:** Identify transcription and translation and explain the roles of DNA, mRNA, tRNA and rRNA.

**Teaching activity:** Use a paper codon chart and coloured cards to model transcription and translation.

**Practice**
1. What is transcription?  
   **Answer:** The production of an RNA copy from a DNA template.
2. What does mRNA do?  
   **Answer:** Carries coding information from DNA to the ribosome.
3. What is the role of tRNA?  
   **Answer:** Delivers amino acids and matches its anticodon to an mRNA codon.

## Lesson 8 — Connect genes, alleles, proteins and traits

**Objective:** `M4-BIO-GEN-08`  
**Outcome:** Explain how genetic material and alleles influence proteins and phenotypes, connecting gene expression to Mendelian inheritance.

**Teaching activity:** Trace a simplified pathway from allele sequence to protein activity to a hypothetical observable trait.

**Practice**
1. What is an allele?  
   **Answer:** An alternative form of a gene at a particular locus.
2. How can a gene affect a trait?  
   **Answer:** Its expression can produce or regulate a protein that contributes to the trait.
3. Does genotype always determine phenotype alone?  
   **Answer:** No. Environment and interactions among genes can also influence phenotype.

## Lesson 9 — Explain gene and chromosome mutations

**Objective:** `M4-BIO-GEN-09`  
**Outcome:** Describe gene-level and chromosome-level mutations, causes and examples of associated disorders or syndromes.

**Teaching activity:** Compare neutral fictional DNA sequence changes and chromosome diagrams; discuss mutation as a source of variation without implying all mutations are harmful.

**Practice**
1. What is a gene mutation?  
   **Answer:** A change in the nucleotide sequence of a gene.
2. Name one chromosome mutation involving chromosome number.  
   **Answer:** Aneuploidy, such as an extra chromosome.
3. Are all mutations harmful?  
   **Answer:** No. Effects can be harmful, neutral or sometimes beneficial depending on context.

## Lesson 10 — Explain recombinant-DNA genetic modification

**Objective:** `M4-BIO-GEN-10`  
**Outcome:** Explain the principles used to create genetically modified organisms through recombinant DNA technology.

**Teaching activity:** Use a paper-based sequence showing a target gene, vector, joining step and host cell; avoid unsupervised genetic engineering.

**Practice**
1. What is recombinant DNA?  
   **Answer:** DNA formed by joining DNA segments from different sources.
2. What is a vector used for?  
   **Answer:** To carry genetic material into a host cell.
3. Why are restriction enzymes used in some recombinant DNA methods?  
   **Answer:** They cut DNA at specific recognition sequences.

## Lesson 11 — Evaluate applications and ethics of DNA technology

**Objective:** `M4-BIO-GEN-11`  
**Outcome:** Investigate applications in environment, forensics, medicine, agriculture and industry, and discuss bioethical considerations.

**Teaching activity:** Assign groups different fictional case studies and ask them to weigh benefits, risks, consent, privacy, access and environmental effects.

**Practice**
1. Give one medical application of DNA technology.  
   **Answer:** Producing a therapeutic protein such as human insulin.
2. How can DNA profiling support forensic work?  
   **Answer:** It can compare biological samples with reference profiles, subject to quality controls and legal safeguards.
3. Name one ethical issue in genetic technology.  
   **Answer:** Genetic privacy, informed consent, equitable access or potential environmental impacts.

## Lesson 12 — Evaluate evidence for biological evolution

**Objective:** `M4-BIO-GEN-12`  
**Outcome:** Investigate and explain evidence and scientific data used to support and explain evolution.

**Teaching activity:** Compare fossil sequences, homologous structures, molecular similarities and observed changes in populations.

**Practice**
1. What can fossils show about evolution?  
   **Answer:** They provide evidence of organisms from past geological periods and changes through time.
2. What are homologous structures?  
   **Answer:** Structures with a shared evolutionary origin, even if their functions differ.
3. How can DNA comparisons support evolutionary relationships?  
   **Answer:** Shared sequence similarities can indicate common ancestry.

## Lesson 13 — Compare Lamarckian and Darwinian evolution

**Objective:** `M4-BIO-GEN-13`  
**Outcome:** Compare Lamarck’s ideas with Darwin’s theory of evolution by natural selection.

**Teaching activity:** Use a neutral case study and distinguish an individual's lifetime changes from heritable variation across generations.

**Practice**
1. What did Lamarck propose about use and disuse?  
   **Answer:** He proposed that frequently used structures become stronger and unused structures diminish, with acquired changes passed on.
2. What is natural selection?  
   **Answer:** Individuals with heritable traits that improve reproductive success tend to leave more offspring.
3. What is a key difference between the explanations?  
   **Answer:** Darwinian natural selection acts on heritable variation already present in populations; it does not require organisms to acquire traits because they need them.

## Lesson 14 — Use the Hardy-Weinberg principle

**Objective:** `M4-BIO-GEN-14`  
**Outcome:** State equilibrium conditions, explain factors changing allele frequencies, and calculate allele and genotype frequencies.

**Teaching activity:** Use hypothetical population data and practise p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1. State assumptions before interpreting results.

**Practice**
1. What do p and q represent?  
   **Answer:** The frequencies of two alleles at a locus, with p + q = 1 in a two-allele model.
2. If p = 0.7 and q = 0.3, what is the expected heterozygote frequency under Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium?  
   **Answer:** 2pq = 2(0.7)(0.3) = 0.42.
3. Name two forces that can change allele frequencies.  
   **Answer:** Natural selection and genetic drift (also mutation, migration and non-random mating can affect equilibrium assumptions).

## Lesson 15 — Explain the process of speciation

**Objective:** `M4-BIO-GEN-15`  
**Outcome:** Investigate and explain processes through which new species arise, including reproductive isolation and population divergence.

**Teaching activity:** Model how geographic separation, mutation, selection and drift can cause populations to diverge, then discuss reproductive isolation.

**Practice**
1. What is speciation?  
   **Answer:** The evolutionary process by which new species arise.
2. What is reproductive isolation?  
   **Answer:** Barriers that prevent populations from successfully interbreeding and exchanging genes.
3. How can geographic isolation contribute to speciation?  
   **Answer:** It reduces gene flow, allowing separated populations to diverge over generations.

## Lesson 1 — Classify plant tissues

**Objective:** `M5-BIO-PLANT-01`  
**Official alignment:** Additional Biology strand 3, M5 learning outcome 1  
**Learning focus:** Explain meristematic and permanent tissues, including dermal, ground and vascular tissues.

**Practice**
1. What is a meristem?  
   **Answer:** A region of actively dividing plant cells.
2. Name the two main vascular tissues.  
   **Answer:** Xylem and phloem.
3. What is one role of epidermal tissue?  
   **Answer:** Protection and regulation of exchange.

## Lesson 2 — Compare internal root structures

**Objective:** `M5-BIO-PLANT-02`  
**Official alignment:** Additional Biology strand 3, M5 learning outcome 2  
**Learning focus:** Observe monocot and dicot root cross-sections and identify epidermis, cortex, endodermis and vascular tissues.

**Practice**
1. What is the function of root hairs?  
   **Answer:** They increase surface area for absorption.
2. Where is the endodermis found?  
   **Answer:** At the inner boundary of the root cortex.
3. How do typical monocot and dicot roots differ internally?  
   **Answer:** Their vascular arrangements differ; dicot roots commonly have central star-shaped xylem, while monocot roots commonly have a central pith with vascular tissues around it.

## Lesson 3 — Compare internal stem structures

**Objective:** `M5-BIO-PLANT-03`  
**Official alignment:** Additional Biology strand 3, M5 learning outcome 3  
**Learning focus:** Compare monocot and dicot stem cross-sections and relate vascular-bundle arrangement to stem organisation.

**Practice**
1. What do vascular bundles contain?  
   **Answer:** Xylem and phloem.
2. How are vascular bundles commonly arranged in a dicot stem?  
   **Answer:** In a ring.
3. How are they commonly arranged in a monocot stem?  
   **Answer:** Scattered through the ground tissue.

## Lesson 4 — Explain the internal structure of leaves

**Objective:** `M5-BIO-PLANT-04`  
**Official alignment:** Additional Biology strand 3, M5 learning outcome 4  
**Learning focus:** Identify epidermis, palisade and spongy mesophyll, stomata and veins in a leaf cross-section.

**Practice**
1. Which tissue is usually rich in chloroplasts?  
   **Answer:** Palisade mesophyll.
2. What is the role of stomata?  
   **Answer:** They regulate gas exchange and water vapour loss.
3. What do leaf veins contain?  
   **Answer:** Xylem and phloem.

## Lesson 5 — Explain plant gas exchange and transpiration

**Objective:** `M5-BIO-PLANT-05`  
**Official alignment:** Additional Biology strand 3, M5 learning outcome 5  
**Learning focus:** Investigate stomatal gas exchange and transpiration and interpret the effects of environmental conditions.

**Practice**
1. What is transpiration?  
   **Answer:** Loss of water vapour from aerial parts of a plant, mainly through stomata.
2. Which gas enters leaves for photosynthesis?  
   **Answer:** Carbon dioxide.
3. Name one factor that can increase transpiration.  
   **Answer:** Higher air movement, temperature or light intensity, or lower humidity.

## Lesson 6 — Explain water and mineral transport

**Objective:** `M5-BIO-PLANT-06`  
**Official alignment:** Additional Biology strand 3, M5 learning outcome 6  
**Learning focus:** Explain water uptake and movement through xylem, including cohesion, adhesion and transpiration pull.

**Practice**
1. Which tissue transports water upward?  
   **Answer:** Xylem.
2. What is transpiration pull?  
   **Answer:** A tension created by water evaporation from leaves that helps draw water upward.
3. How do root hairs help water uptake?  
   **Answer:** They provide a large surface area for absorption.

## Lesson 7 — Explain mineral nutrition in plants

**Objective:** `M5-BIO-PLANT-07`  
**Official alignment:** Additional Biology strand 3, M5 learning outcome 7  
**Learning focus:** Relate essential mineral elements to plant growth and recognise common deficiency symptoms.

**Practice**
1. Which mineral is needed to make chlorophyll?  
   **Answer:** Magnesium.
2. Why do plants need nitrogen?  
   **Answer:** To make amino acids, proteins and nucleic acids.
3. Name one possible symptom of mineral deficiency.  
   **Answer:** Chlorosis, stunted growth or poor root development, depending on the element.

## Lesson 8 — Explain translocation of food in plants

# M5 Biology Teacher Starter Pack — Animal Physiology

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017), Additional Biology, strand 4  
**Grade:** M5  
**Coverage:** 21 official learning outcomes; 21 lesson starters; 63 practice questions with answer keys

> **Grade alignment:** The official curriculum lists no M4 outcomes for Biology strand 4. Outcomes 1–21 are therefore aligned to M5, not M4.

## Teaching and safety guidance

Use labelled diagrams, prepared slides, animations, virtual microscopy and teacher-provided datasets. Do not ask students to collect or handle human blood, perform blood typing, dissect animals, expose themselves to pathogens, or conduct experiments on living animals. Any lung-volume demonstration must be optional, hygienic, supervised and educational rather than diagnostic; a simulation or supplied dataset is preferable. Discuss HIV, allergies and autoimmune conditions respectfully, without stigma or asking students to disclose personal or family health information. Medical questions should be referred to qualified health professionals.

## Lesson sequence and practice

### 1. Comparative digestive systems
**Objective:** Compare animals with no digestive tract, an incomplete digestive tract and a complete digestive tract.  
**Starter:** Display three labelled diagrams and ask students to predict how food enters, is digested and waste leaves each system.

1. What is the main difference between an incomplete and a complete digestive tract?  
   **Answer:** An incomplete tract has one opening serving as both mouth and exit; a complete tract has separate mouth and anus.
2. Which type of animal has no specialised digestive tract?  
   **Answer:** A sponge; digestion occurs inside cells.
3. Give one advantage of a complete digestive tract.  
   **Answer:** Food moves in one direction, allowing specialised regions to perform different digestive functions.

### 2. Feeding in Hydra and Planaria
**Objective:** Explain feeding in Hydra and Planaria using prepared observations.  
**Starter:** Compare diagrams of Hydra tentacles and a Planaria feeding structure; students annotate how food is captured and taken in.

1. What do Hydra tentacles help it do?  
   **Answer:** Capture prey and move it towards the mouth.
2. Which specialised cells on Hydra tentacles help capture prey?  
   **Answer:** Cnidocytes, which contain stinging structures.
3. How does Planaria take food into its digestive cavity?  
   **Answer:** It extends a muscular pharynx through its mouth.

### 3. Human digestion and absorption
**Objective:** Identify digestive organs and explain digestion and absorption.  
**Starter:** Arrange organ cards in the path food travels; add accessory organs and their functions.

1. Where does most nutrient absorption occur?  
   **Answer:** In the small intestine, through its villi and microvilli.
2. What is the role of bile in digestion?  
   **Answer:** It emulsifies fats into smaller droplets; it is not an enzyme.
3. Which enzyme in saliva begins starch digestion?  
   **Answer:** Salivary amylase.

### 4. Gas exchange across animal groups
**Objective:** Compare gas exchange structures in the named animal groups.  
**Starter:** Sort picture cards into body-surface diffusion, gills, tracheal systems and lungs.

1. How do fish usually exchange gases with water?  
   **Answer:** Through gills, where dissolved oxygen diffuses into blood.
2. What structure carries air directly to tissues in insects?  
   **Answer:** The tracheal system, including tracheae and tracheoles.
3. Why can a thin, moist body surface support gas exchange in some small animals?  
   **Answer:** Gases can diffuse across it over a short distance.

### 5. Mammalian lung structure
**Objective:** Relate lung structures to efficient gas exchange.  
**Starter:** Label a lung diagram from trachea to alveoli and identify features that increase exchange efficiency.

1. What are alveoli?  
   **Answer:** Tiny air sacs where gas exchange occurs.
2. Why are alveoli surrounded by many capillaries?  
   **Answer:** To provide a rich blood supply and maintain diffusion gradients.
3. How does a large alveolar surface area help gas exchange?  
   **Answer:** It provides more area for oxygen and carbon dioxide to diffuse.

### 6. Human ventilation and gas exchange
**Objective:** Explain ventilation and diffusion between alveoli, blood and tissues.  
**Starter:** Trace oxygen and carbon dioxide with arrows during inhalation, exhalation and tissue exchange.

1. In which direction does oxygen diffuse at the alveoli?  
   **Answer:** From alveolar air into the blood.
2. In which direction does carbon dioxide diffuse at the alveoli?  
   **Answer:** From blood into alveolar air.
3. What happens to the diaphragm during inhalation?  
   **Answer:** It contracts and flattens, increasing thoracic cavity volume.

### 7. Lung function and exhaled air volume
**Objective:** Interpret safe measurements or supplied datasets of exhaled air volume.  
**Starter:** Present a sample graph from a spirometry dataset and ask students to identify the measured volume and compare trials.

1. What does tidal volume describe?  
   **Answer:** The volume of air inhaled or exhaled in a normal, quiet breath.
2. What instrument can measure lung volumes?  
   **Answer:** A spirometer.
3. Why should a classroom lung-volume activity not be used to diagnose disease?  
   **Answer:** Diagnosis requires validated procedures, clinical interpretation and qualified health professionals.

### 8. Open and closed circulation
**Objective:** Compare open and closed circulatory systems.  
**Starter:** Trace fluid movement on two simplified circulation diagrams.

1. In an open circulatory system, where does circulatory fluid flow?  
   **Answer:** It leaves vessels and bathes organs in body spaces.
2. In a closed circulatory system, where does blood flow?  
   **Answer:** Within vessels.
3. State one advantage of a closed system.  
   **Answer:** It can deliver blood under controlled pressure and direct flow to particular tissues.

### 9. Blood vessel diameter and flow
**Objective:** Interpret prepared observations of fish-tail blood flow and relate vessel size to flow speed.  
**Starter:** Use a recorded microscopic video or supplied images; compare flow in vessels of different diameters.

1. Which vessels carry blood away from the heart?  
   **Answer:** Arteries.
2. Which vessels are the main sites of exchange with tissues?  
   **Answer:** Capillaries.
3. What general relationship can be observed between vessel diameter and blood-flow speed in the same branching network?  
   **Answer:** Flow speed generally decreases as blood enters the many narrow capillaries and increases as vessels merge; total cross-sectional area and pressure also matter.

### 10. Human heart and blood vessels
**Objective:** Relate heart and vessel structures to their functions.  
**Starter:** Label a heart diagram and classify arteries, veins and capillaries by structure and function.

1. What is the main function of the heart?  
   **Answer:** To pump blood through the circulatory system.
2. Why do arteries have thick, elastic walls?  
   **Answer:** They withstand and help maintain the higher pressure of blood leaving the heart.
3. What do valves in veins help prevent?  
   **Answer:** Backflow of blood.

### 11. Blood flow through the mammalian heart
**Objective:** Trace blood through the heart, lungs and body.  
**Starter:** Arrange route cards from the vena cava through the heart and lungs to the aorta.

1. Which chamber pumps blood to the lungs?  
   **Answer:** The right ventricle.
2. Which chamber pumps blood to the body?  
   **Answer:** The left ventricle.
3. What is the route after blood returns from the lungs?  
   **Answer:** Pulmonary veins → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta.

### 12. Components of blood
**Objective:** Compare red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets and plasma.  
**Starter:** Match each component to an illustration and its function.

1. Which blood component transports most oxygen?  
   **Answer:** Red blood cells, using haemoglobin.
2. What is the main role of platelets?  
   **Answer:** They help blood clot and reduce blood loss.
3. What is plasma?  
   **Answer:** The liquid component of blood that transports cells and dissolved substances.

### 13. ABO and Rh blood groups
**Objective:** Explain blood groups and interpret compatibility charts.  
**Starter:** Use a non-clinical antigen/antibody card model and a supplied red-cell transfusion compatibility chart.

1. Which antigen is present on type A red blood cells?  
   **Answer:** A antigen.
2. Which ABO antibodies are normally found in the plasma of a person with type B blood?  
   **Answer:** Anti-A antibodies.
3. Why must blood compatibility be checked before transfusion?  
   **Answer:** Incompatible antigens and antibodies can cause dangerous immune reactions; clinical matching includes more than the ABO group alone.

### 14. Lymphatic system
**Objective:** Explain lymph composition and the roles of lymph vessels and nodes.  
**Starter:** Trace tissue fluid from capillaries into lymph vessels and back to the bloodstream.

1. Where does lymph originate?  
   **Answer:** From interstitial fluid collected into lymphatic capillaries.
2. What is one function of lymph nodes?  
   **Answer:** They filter lymph and provide sites where immune cells encounter antigens.
3. How does the lymphatic system help maintain fluid balance?  
   **Answer:** It returns excess tissue fluid and proteins to the bloodstream.

### 15. Innate and adaptive immunity
**Objective:** Compare non-specific and specific immune responses.  
**Starter:** Sort defence examples into barriers, innate responses and adaptive responses.

1. Give one example of a physical innate defence.  
   **Answer:** Skin or mucous membranes.
2. What makes an adaptive immune response specific?  
   **Answer:** Lymphocytes recognise particular antigens and respond to them.
3. Which cells can produce antibodies?  
   **Answer:** B lymphocytes that differentiate into plasma cells.

### 16. Active and passive immunity
**Objective:** Distinguish natural and medically acquired active and passive immunity.  
**Starter:** Use four scenario cards: infection, vaccination, maternal antibodies and antibody treatment.

1. What produces active immunity?  
   **Answer:** The person's own immune system responds to an antigen.
2. Why is passive immunity usually temporary?  
   **Answer:** Received antibodies gradually break down; immune memory is not usually formed.
3. Is vaccination an example of active or passive immunity?  
   **Answer:** Artificially acquired active immunity.

### 17. Selected immune system disorders
**Objective:** Explain HIV-related immune deficiency, allergies and autoimmune conditions respectfully.  
**Starter:** Analyse teacher-selected diagrams of immune responses; focus on mechanisms rather than personal disclosure.

1. Which immune cells are a major target of HIV?  
   **Answer:** CD4 T helper cells.
2. What causes an allergic reaction?  
   **Answer:** An immune response to an ordinarily harmless substance (allergen).
3. What happens in an autoimmune condition?  
   **Answer:** The immune system mistakenly targets the body's own cells or tissues.

### 18. Comparative excretion
**Objective:** Compare excretory structures in the listed animal groups.  
**Starter:** Match animal groups to diagrams of diffusion, flame cells, nephridia, Malpighian tubules and kidneys.

1. What structures help Planaria remove excess water and wastes?  
   **Answer:** Protonephridia containing flame cells.
2. What are the excretory structures of many insects called?  
   **Answer:** Malpighian tubules.
3. What is a major nitrogenous waste removed by mammalian kidneys?  
   **Answer:** Urea.

### 19. Kidney and urinary tract
**Objective:** Identify urinary structures and explain their functions.  
**Starter:** Label kidneys, ureters, urinary bladder and urethra on a body diagram.

1. What is the main function of the kidneys?  
   **Answer:** To filter blood, regulate water and ion balance, and form urine.
2. Which tubes carry urine from the kidneys to the bladder?  
   **Answer:** The ureters.
3. What is the function of the urinary bladder?  
   **Answer:** It stores urine before it is expelled through the urethra.

### 20. Nephron and urine formation
**Objective:** Explain filtration, reabsorption and secretion in the nephron.  
**Starter:** Arrange process cards on a nephron diagram and annotate where each process occurs.

1. Where does blood filtration begin in the nephron?  
   **Answer:** At the renal corpuscle, where fluid is filtered from glomerular capillaries into Bowman's capsule.
2. What is selective reabsorption?  
   **Answer:** The movement of useful substances and needed water from the filtrate back into the blood.
3. What is tubular secretion?  
   **Answer:** The movement of selected substances from blood into the nephron tubule.

### 21. Kidney disorders and prevention
**Objective:** Describe selected kidney disorders and evidence-based risk reduction.  
**Starter:** Interpret a teacher-provided case study about kidney function without asking students to identify or disclose anyone's diagnosis.

1. What is a kidney stone?  
   **Answer:** A hard deposit formed from minerals and salts in the urinary system.
2. How can uncontrolled high blood pressure affect the kidneys?  
   **Answer:** It can damage kidney blood vessels and filtering structures over time.
3. Name one evidence-based way to reduce preventable kidney risks.  
   **Answer:** Maintain healthy blood pressure and blood glucose, avoid smoking, and use medicines only as directed by a health professional.

- Require labelled diagrams and correctly directed arrows for digestion, gas exchange, circulation and urine formation.
- Use fictional scenarios and prepared data for blood-group, immune and kidney topics.
- Do not treat practice answers as medical advice or diagnostic guidance.

**Starter:** Sort short animal-behaviour clips into innate, learned or behaviour influenced by both.

1. What is an innate behaviour?  
   **Answer:** A behaviour that is present without the individual having to learn it through experience.
2. What is habituation?  
   **Answer:** A simple form of learning in which response to a repeated, harmless stimulus decreases.
3. Give one example of learned behaviour.  
   **Answer:** A dog learning to respond to a trained signal or a bird learning a song through experience.

### 18. Behaviour and nervous-system evolution
**Objective:** Relate behaviour to the evolution of nervous-system organisation.  
**Starter:** Compare evidence from animal case studies, avoiding the assumption that evolution is a simple ladder of progress.

1. What is one broad trend seen in many animal lineages?  
   **Answer:** Nervous systems can show increasing specialisation and centralisation, although evolution is branching and diverse.
2. How can sensory specialisation influence behaviour?  
   **Answer:** It allows animals to detect particular environmental cues and respond in ways relevant to survival or reproduction.
3. Why should animal nervous systems not be ranked as a simple ladder from inferior to superior?  
   **Answer:** Different nervous systems are adaptations to different environments and ways of life.

### 19. Animal communication
**Objective:** Explain visual, acoustic, chemical and tactile communication between animals.  
**Starter:** Show examples of courtship displays, alarm calls, scent marking and tactile signals; learners identify signal, receiver and likely response.

1. What is a pheromone?  
   **Answer:** A chemical signal released by an organism that affects the behaviour or physiology of another member of the same species.
2. Give one example of acoustic communication.  
   **Answer:** A bird song or a frog call used in territorial or reproductive signalling.
3. Why does the receiver's sensory system matter in animal communication?  
   **Answer:** The receiver must be able to detect and interpret the signal for it to influence behaviour.

- Assess comparisons across animal groups using evidence rather than simplistic evolutionary rankings.
- Use safe, optional sensory demonstrations and accessible alternatives.
- Keep reproductive-health teaching factual, respectful and age appropriate.

## Lesson 1 — Levels and importance of biodiversity
**Objective:** M6-BIO-BIODIV-01 — Explain genetic, species and ecosystem diversity and how they are connected.
**Starter / learning task:** Explain genetic, species and ecosystem diversity and how they are connected. Have learners justify their explanations with examples, diagrams or evidence from supplied resources.

**Practice**
1. What is genetic diversity?
   **Answer:** Variation in genetic information among individuals or populations of the same species.
2. How is species diversity different from ecosystem diversity?
   **Answer:** Species diversity concerns the variety and relative abundance of species; ecosystem diversity concerns the variety of habitats and ecological systems.
3. Why does biodiversity matter to people and ecosystems?
   **Answer:** It supports ecosystem functions and services, resilience, food resources, medicines and cultural values.

## Lesson 2 — Origin of early cells and unicellular life
**Objective:** M6-BIO-BIODIV-02 — Evaluate scientific hypotheses and evidence about early cells and the evolution of unicellular organisms.
**Starter / learning task:** Evaluate scientific hypotheses and evidence about early cells and the evolution of unicellular organisms. Have learners justify their explanations with examples, diagrams or evidence from supplied resources.

**Practice**
1. What is a protocell in origin-of-life research?
   **Answer:** A proposed early cell-like system with a boundary and some life-like chemical processes; it is not itself a confirmed living ancestor.
2. What evidence supports the ancient existence of microbial life?
   **Answer:** Evidence includes ancient microbial structures, chemical signatures and geological records, interpreted with uncertainty.
3. Why should explanations of life's origin distinguish evidence from hypotheses?
   **Answer:** The early Earth record is incomplete, so scientific models are evaluated and revised as new evidence appears.

## Lesson 3 — Major groups of organisms
**Objective:** M6-BIO-BIODIV-03 — Compare bacteria, protists, plants, fungi and animals using cellular and nutritional characteristics.
**Starter / learning task:** Compare bacteria, protists, plants, fungi and animals using cellular and nutritional characteristics. Have learners justify their explanations with examples, diagrams or evidence from supplied resources.

**Practice**
1. Are bacteria prokaryotic or eukaryotic?
   **Answer:** Prokaryotic; they lack a membrane-bound nucleus.
2. What feature distinguishes fungi from plants in nutrition?
   **Answer:** Fungi absorb nutrients from organic material; plants generally photosynthesise, although there are exceptions.
3. Why is appearance alone unreliable for classification?
   **Answer:** Similar forms can evolve independently; cellular, reproductive and molecular evidence provides stronger relationships.

## Lesson 4 — Taxonomic hierarchy and scientific names
**Objective:** M6-BIO-BIODIV-04 — Classify organisms through taxonomic ranks and write species-level scientific names correctly.
**Starter / learning task:** Classify organisms through taxonomic ranks and write species-level scientific names correctly. Have learners justify their explanations with examples, diagrams or evidence from supplied resources.

**Practice**
1. Name the common taxonomic ranks from broad to specific.
   **Answer:** Domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species.
2. How is a binomial scientific name written?
   **Answer:** The genus begins with a capital letter, the specific epithet is lowercase, and the whole name is italicised when typed.
3. Why are scientific names useful?
   **Answer:** They provide a standard international name that reduces ambiguity among common names.

## Lesson 5 — Dichotomous keys
**Objective:** M6-BIO-BIODIV-05 — Construct and apply a dichotomous key using observable paired characteristics.
**Starter / learning task:** Construct and apply a dichotomous key using observable paired characteristics. Have learners justify their explanations with examples, diagrams or evidence from supplied resources.

**Practice**
1. What is a dichotomous key?
   **Answer:** An identification tool that uses a sequence of paired choices to identify an organism or specimen.
2. What makes a useful pair of statements in a key?
   **Answer:** The two alternatives are clear, mutually exclusive and based on observable characteristics.
3. Why should a key avoid vague terms such as 'large' without a reference?
   **Answer:** Different users may interpret them differently, reducing reliable identification.
