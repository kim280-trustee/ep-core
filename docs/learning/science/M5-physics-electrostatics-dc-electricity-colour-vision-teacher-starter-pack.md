# M5 Physics — Electrostatics, DC Electricity and Colour Vision

## Official alignment

Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017), Additional Physics:
- **Group 3, M5 outcomes 1–11:** electrostatics, Coulomb's law, electric fields and potential, capacitors, current, resistance, DC circuits, emf, power and renewable electricity. The M5 sequence is printed on pp. 31–35; the separate M6 Group 3 sequence restarts at outcome 1.
- **Group 2, M5 outcome 13:** colour vision, object colours, pigment/light mixing and colour blindness, printed p. 30.

These are separate content-group outcomes. The group number is not the grade level.

## Group 3 — Outcome 1: Electric Charging by Friction and Induction

**Objective:** Explain how friction transfers electrons and how electrostatic induction redistributes charge; apply conservation of electric charge.

**Starter:** Rub a balloon on a clean dry cloth and observe attraction to small paper pieces. Ask how a neutral object can become charged.

**Teach and practise:** Distinguish electron transfer by friction from charge redistribution by induction. Use diagrams to track positive and negative charge; emphasise conservation of total charge.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. Which particles usually move between objects during charging by friction? **Answer:** Electrons.
2. A neutral object gains electrons. What is its net charge? **Answer:** Negative.
3. What happens to charges in a neutral metal object when a negatively charged rod is brought near without touching it? **Answer:** Electrons are repelled toward the far side, leaving the near side relatively positive; total charge remains zero.

## Group 3 — Outcome 2: Coulomb’s Law

**Objective:** Explain the direction and magnitude of forces between point charges and calculate electric force using Coulomb’s law.

**Starter:** Draw two positive charges, then a positive and a negative charge. Ask which pairs attract and which repel.

**Teach and practise:** Use \(F=k|q_1q_2|/r^2\); discuss attraction/repulsion, inverse-square distance and SI units.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. Charges of 2 μC and 3 μC are 0.30 m apart in air. Find the force magnitude (\(k=9.0\times10^9\)). **Answer:** \(F=0.60\) N.
2. What happens to the force if the distance between two point charges doubles? **Answer:** It becomes one quarter as large.
3. Do two negative point charges attract or repel? **Answer:** Repel.

## Group 3 — Outcome 3: Electric Fields and Electric Force

**Objective:** Explain electric fields and force on a charge; calculate fields from point charges and determine resultant fields using vector addition.

**Starter:** Show electric-field-line diagrams around positive and negative charges. Ask what the arrows represent.

**Teach and practise:** Use \(E=F/q\) and \(E=k|Q|/r^2\); field direction is the force direction on a positive test charge. Combine fields as vectors.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. A 2 μC test charge experiences a 0.04 N force. Find the field magnitude. **Answer:** \(E=0.04/(2\times10^{-6})=2.0\times10^4\) N/C.
2. In which direction do electric-field lines point around a positive point charge? **Answer:** Radially outward.
3. Two equal electric-field vectors act in opposite directions at a point. What is the resultant field? **Answer:** Zero.

## Group 3 — Outcome 4: Electric Potential and Potential Difference

**Objective:** Explain electric potential energy, electric potential and potential difference; calculate values for point charges and uniform fields.

**Starter:** Compare electric potential to gravitational height as an energy-per-unit concept. Ask what work is needed to move a charge.

**Teach and practise:** Use \(V=kQ/r\), \(U=qV\) and \(\Delta V=Ed\) in a uniform field, with sign and direction conventions stated.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. Find the potential 0.20 m from a +2 μC point charge (\(k=9.0\times10^9\)). **Answer:** \(V=90,000\) V.
2. A 2 C charge moves through a potential difference of 5 V. Find the change in potential energy magnitude. **Answer:** 10 J.
3. What is potential difference measured in? **Answer:** Volts (J/C).

## Group 3 — Outcome 5: Capacitance, Stored Energy and Capacitor Networks

**Objective:** Explain capacitor construction, calculate capacitance and stored energy, and determine equivalent capacitance in series and parallel.

**Starter:** Show a labelled capacitor diagram and ask what the insulating layer between the conductors does.

**Teach and practise:** Use \(C=Q/\Delta V\), \(U=\frac12 Q\Delta V\); for parallel capacitors add capacitances, while for series use reciprocal addition.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. A capacitor stores 60 μC at 12 V. Find its capacitance. **Answer:** \(C=5\) μF.
2. Two capacitors of 2 μF and 3 μF are connected in parallel. Find the equivalent capacitance. **Answer:** 5 μF.
3. What happens to equivalent capacitance when identical capacitors are connected in series? **Answer:** It is less than the capacitance of one capacitor; for two identical capacitors it is half.

## Group 3 — Outcome 6: Applications of Electrostatics

**Objective:** Explain electrostatic applications such as dust removal, spray painting and photocopying, and explain electrostatic hazards such as lightning.

**Starter:** Show a photocopier or electrostatic precipitator diagram. Ask how electric charge can help move particles.

**Teach and practise:** Connect attraction/repulsion and induced charge to electrostatic precipitators, spray painting and photocopying; discuss lightning as a discharge and safe shelter practices.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. Why can an electrostatic precipitator remove dust from exhaust gas? **Answer:** Charged dust particles are attracted to oppositely charged collection plates.
2. Why can electrostatic spray painting produce an even coating? **Answer:** Charged paint droplets are attracted to the oppositely charged or grounded surface and repel one another, helping spread the coating.
3. What is a safe action during a thunderstorm outdoors? **Answer:** Move to a substantial enclosed building or hard-topped vehicle; avoid open fields, isolated trees and water.

## Group 3 — Outcome 7: Electron Drift and Electric Current

**Objective:** Explain electron drift in a conductor and calculate current using \(I=neAv_d\).

**Starter:** Show a conductor diagram with electron drift opposite to conventional current. Ask why current direction is defined differently from electron motion.

**Teach and practise:** Define charge-carrier density, cross-sectional area and drift speed. Use \(I=neAv_d\) and distinguish drift speed from random thermal motion.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. If electron drift speed increases while all other quantities stay constant, what happens to current? **Answer:** Current increases in direct proportion.
2. A wire has \(n=8.0\times10^{28}\) m⁻³, \(A=1.0\times10^{-6}\) m², \(v_d=1.0\times10^{-4}\) m/s and \(e=1.6\times10^{-19}\) C. Find current. **Answer:** \(I=1.28\) A.
3. What is the direction of conventional current relative to electron drift in a metal? **Answer:** Opposite.

## Group 3 — Outcome 8: Ohm’s Law, Resistivity and Resistor Networks

**Objective:** Investigate Ohm’s law; calculate resistance from resistivity, length and area; determine series and parallel equivalent resistance.

**Starter:** Show a simple low-voltage circuit with a resistor and meter readings. Ask what happens to current when voltage increases at constant temperature.

**Teach and practise:** Use \(V=IR\), \(R=\rho L/A\), series \(R_s=R_1+R_2+...\), and parallel reciprocal addition.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. A 12 V supply is connected across a 4 Ω resistor. Find current. **Answer:** \(I=3\) A.
2. A wire has resistivity \(2.0\times10^{-8}\) Ωm, length 2 m and area \(1.0\times10^{-6}\) m². Find resistance. **Answer:** \(R=0.04\) Ω.
3. Find the equivalent resistance of 6 Ω and 3 Ω resistors in parallel. **Answer:** 2 Ω.

## Group 3 — Outcome 9: EMF, Electrical Energy and Power

**Objective:** Explain DC-source emf and calculate terminal voltage, electrical energy and power, including internal resistance where appropriate.

**Starter:** Compare a battery's labelled voltage with a measured terminal voltage under load. Ask why they may differ.

**Teach and practise:** Use \(\varepsilon=V+Ir\), \(W=IVt\) and \(P=IV\); explain internal resistance and energy transfer.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. A 12 V source has internal resistance 1 Ω and supplies 2 A. Find terminal voltage. **Answer:** \(V=12-(2)(1)=10\) V.
2. A 6 V device draws 0.5 A for 10 minutes. Find electrical energy used. **Answer:** \(W=IVt=0.5\times6\times600=1800\) J.
3. Find the power of a device operating at 12 V and 2 A. **Answer:** 24 W.

## Group 3 — Outcome 10: Series and Parallel Battery Circuits

**Objective:** Calculate equivalent emf and internal resistance for series and parallel battery combinations and solve DC circuits with resistors.

**Starter:** Draw two identical batteries in series and then in parallel. Ask what changes and what stays the same.

**Teach and practise:** For identical cells, series connection adds emf and internal resistance; parallel connection keeps emf the same and reduces equivalent internal resistance. Apply \(I=\varepsilon/(R+r)\) to simple circuits.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. Two identical 1.5 V cells are connected in series. Find total emf. **Answer:** 3.0 V.
2. Two identical 1.5 V cells, each with internal resistance 0.4 Ω, are connected in parallel. Find equivalent internal resistance. **Answer:** 0.2 Ω; emf remains 1.5 V.
3. A 6 V battery with internal resistance 1 Ω is connected to a 5 Ω resistor. Find current. **Answer:** \(I=6/(5+1)=1\) A.

## Group 3 — Outcome 11: Renewable Electricity Technologies

**Objective:** Explain how solar, wind, hydroelectric and other renewable sources can be converted into electricity and compare relevant technologies.

**Starter:** Show pictures of solar panels, wind turbines and a hydroelectric station. Ask what energy transformation occurs in each.

**Teach and practise:** Trace energy conversions and compare resource availability, intermittency, storage, location and environmental impacts. Separate energy source from generator and grid systems.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. What energy conversion occurs in a photovoltaic cell? **Answer:** Radiant energy from sunlight is converted directly into electrical energy.
2. How does a wind turbine generate electricity? **Answer:** Wind turns blades and a shaft, driving a generator that converts mechanical energy into electrical energy.
3. Give one advantage and one limitation of solar electricity. **Answer:** Advantage: low operational emissions and scalable generation. Limitation: output varies with sunlight and may require storage or backup.

## Group 2 — Outcome 13: Colour Vision, Colour Mixing and Colour Blindness

**Objective:** Explain how light and pigments determine object colour, compare additive light mixing with subtractive pigment mixing, and explain colour-vision deficiency in terms of cone cells.

**Starter:** Shine red and green light onto a screen (or use a simulation), then compare with mixing red and green paints. Ask why the results differ.

**Teach and practise:** Explain that object colour depends on reflected/transmitted light and pigments selectively absorb wavelengths. Additive RGB light mixing differs from subtractive pigment mixing. Introduce cone cells and colour-vision deficiency respectfully and factually.

**Answer-keyed questions**
1. What colour is produced when red and green light are mixed at suitable intensities? **Answer:** Yellow light.
2. Why does a green leaf usually appear green in white light? **Answer:** Its pigments absorb more strongly in other parts of the visible spectrum and reflect/transmit more green light.
3. Which retinal cells are primarily responsible for colour vision? **Answer:** Cone photoreceptors.

## Safety and assessment

Use only low-voltage, current-limited classroom circuits with approved batteries and components. Do not use mains electricity, improvised high-voltage sources or lightning demonstrations. Capacitors must be appropriately rated and safely discharged by the teacher before handling. Static demonstrations should use ordinary safe classroom materials. Use simulations and diagrams for hazardous electrostatic equipment. Colour-vision activities must not label or diagnose students; use optional simulations and respectful scientific language.
