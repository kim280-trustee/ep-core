# M6 Chemistry — Organic Compounds and Polymers Teacher Starter Pack

**Curriculum:** Thai Basic Education Core Curriculum B.E. 2551 (2008), revised B.E. 2560 (2017)  
**Subject / grade:** Chemistry, M6  
**Official strand:** Additional Chemistry strand 1  
**Coverage:** 15 official M6 learning outcomes; 15 lesson starters; 45 practice questions with answer keys  
**Use:** Teacher planning resource. Use diagrams, simulations and teacher-controlled microscale demonstrations. Never ask students to taste chemicals or directly smell reaction mixtures; follow school risk assessments for flammable, corrosive and volatile substances.

## Lesson 1 — Organic compounds in daily life
**Objective:** M6-CHEM-ORG-01 — Find examples of organic compounds with single, double or triple carbon-carbon bonds and explain where they are used.
**Starter / learning task:** Find examples of organic compounds with single, double or triple carbon-carbon bonds and explain where they are used. Ask learners to justify classifications and predictions using molecular structures, balanced equations or supplied evidence.

**Practice**
1. What makes a compound organic?
   **Answer:** Organic compounds are carbon-based compounds, commonly containing carbon-hydrogen bonds; examples include fuels, solvents, medicines and food molecules.
2. Why is ethene important in industry?
   **Answer:** It is a feedstock for making chemicals and the polymer poly(ethene).
3. Name one safety consideration when using organic solvents.
   **Answer:** Many are volatile and flammable, so ventilation and ignition control are important.

## Lesson 2 — Organic structural representations
**Objective:** M6-CHEM-ORG-02 — Translate a small set of organic molecules between Lewis, condensed and skeletal formulae.
**Starter / learning task:** Translate a small set of organic molecules between Lewis, condensed and skeletal formulae. Ask learners to justify classifications and predictions using molecular structures, balanced equations or supplied evidence.

**Practice**
1. What does a skeletal formula omit?
   **Answer:** It usually omits carbon symbols at line ends and vertices and omits hydrogens attached to carbon.
2. What does a condensed formula show?
   **Answer:** It groups atoms to show connectivity without drawing every bond.
3. Why use more than one representation?
   **Answer:** Each format makes different structural information easier to see.

## Lesson 3 — Functional groups
**Objective:** M6-CHEM-ORG-03 — Sort molecular structures into classes by their functional groups.
**Starter / learning task:** Sort molecular structures into classes by their functional groups. Ask learners to justify classifications and predictions using molecular structures, balanced equations or supplied evidence.

**Practice**
1. What functional group identifies an alcohol?
   **Answer:** A hydroxyl group, –OH, attached to a carbon atom.
2. What group identifies a carboxylic acid?
   **Answer:** The carboxyl group, –COOH.
3. Why do functional groups matter?
   **Answer:** They strongly influence characteristic reactions and physical properties.

## Lesson 4 — IUPAC naming
**Objective:** M6-CHEM-ORG-04 — Practise selecting the parent chain, numbering it and naming representative compounds with one principal functional group.
**Starter / learning task:** Practise selecting the parent chain, numbering it and naming representative compounds with one principal functional group. Ask learners to justify classifications and predictions using molecular structures, balanced equations or supplied evidence.

**Practice**
1. What is the parent chain?
   **Answer:** The longest suitable carbon chain containing the principal functional group or multiple bond.
2. Why is numbering important?
   **Answer:** It identifies the positions of substituents, multiple bonds and functional groups.
3. What does the suffix in an organic name often indicate?
   **Answer:** The principal functional-group class or characteristic bonding.

## Lesson 5 — Structural isomerism
**Objective:** M6-CHEM-ORG-05 — Draw alternative carbon skeletons or functional-group arrangements for the same molecular formula.
**Starter / learning task:** Draw alternative carbon skeletons or functional-group arrangements for the same molecular formula. Ask learners to justify classifications and predictions using molecular structures, balanced equations or supplied evidence.

**Practice**
1. What are structural isomers?
   **Answer:** Compounds with the same molecular formula but different atom connectivity.
2. Give an example of two structural isomers with formula C4H10.
   **Answer:** Butane and 2-methylpropane.
3. Do structural isomers always have identical properties?
   **Answer:** No; different structures can produce different physical and chemical properties.

## Lesson 6 — Physical properties of organic compounds
**Objective:** M6-CHEM-ORG-06 — Compare boiling point and water-solubility data for compounds with different functional groups and chain lengths.
**Starter / learning task:** Compare boiling point and water-solubility data for compounds with different functional groups and chain lengths. Ask learners to justify classifications and predictions using molecular structures, balanced equations or supplied evidence.

**Practice**
1. Why do alcohols often boil at higher temperatures than similar-sized alkanes?
   **Answer:** Alcohol molecules can form hydrogen bonds, which require more energy to overcome.
2. How does increasing carbon-chain length usually affect water solubility in a homologous series?
   **Answer:** It generally decreases because the non-polar hydrocarbon portion becomes larger.
3. Name two factors that affect boiling point in organic compounds.
   **Answer:** Molecular size and intermolecular forces, including hydrogen bonding and dipole interactions.

## Lesson 7 — Hydrocarbon reactions
**Objective:** M6-CHEM-ORG-07 — Use reaction maps to classify combustion, bromine reactions and oxidation tests for hydrocarbons.
**Starter / learning task:** Use reaction maps to classify combustion, bromine reactions and oxidation tests for hydrocarbons. Ask learners to justify classifications and predictions using molecular structures, balanced equations or supplied evidence.

**Practice**
1. What are the products of complete combustion of a hydrocarbon?
   **Answer:** Carbon dioxide and water.
2. What observation can indicate reaction of bromine with an alkene?
   **Answer:** The orange-brown bromine colour is decolourised under suitable test conditions.
3. What does a positive potassium permanganate test commonly indicate for an alkene?
   **Answer:** Oxidation of the carbon-carbon double bond, often accompanied by loss of the purple colour under suitable conditions.

## Lesson 8 — Reactions of organic functional groups
**Objective:** M6-CHEM-ORG-08 — Build reaction schemes for esterification, amide formation, hydrolysis and saponification.
**Starter / learning task:** Build reaction schemes for esterification, amide formation, hydrolysis and saponification. Ask learners to justify classifications and predictions using molecular structures, balanced equations or supplied evidence.

**Practice**
1. What are the typical reactants in esterification?
   **Answer:** A carboxylic acid and an alcohol.
2. What is hydrolysis?
   **Answer:** Breaking a chemical bond by reaction with water.
3. What is saponification?
   **Answer:** Alkaline hydrolysis of fats or esters to form a carboxylate salt (soap) and an alcohol such as glycerol for triglycerides.

## Lesson 9 — Testing organic reactions
**Objective:** M6-CHEM-ORG-09 — Interpret teacher-supervised demonstrations or supplied observations for esterification, hydrolysis and saponification.
**Starter / learning task:** Interpret teacher-supervised demonstrations or supplied observations for esterification, hydrolysis and saponification. Ask learners to justify classifications and predictions using molecular structures, balanced equations or supplied evidence.

**Practice**
1. Why is a control useful in a reaction test?
   **Answer:** It helps distinguish the effect of the tested condition from other causes.
2. What evidence might support ester formation?
   **Answer:** A characteristic fruity odour may be noted only by safe teacher-controlled methods; students should not directly smell reaction mixtures.
3. Why should reaction mixtures not be tasted or directly handled?
   **Answer:** They may contain corrosive, irritant or flammable substances.

## Lesson 10 — Applications of organic compounds
**Objective:** M6-CHEM-ORG-10 — Research an organic compound used in medicine, agriculture, food, materials or energy and assess its benefits and risks.
**Starter / learning task:** Research an organic compound used in medicine, agriculture, food, materials or energy and assess its benefits and risks. Ask learners to justify classifications and predictions using molecular structures, balanced equations or supplied evidence.

**Practice**
1. Name one industrial use of ethanol.
   **Answer:** It can be used as a solvent, fuel or chemical feedstock.
2. Why assess exposure route as well as toxicity?
   **Answer:** Risk depends on the substance, dose, route and duration of exposure.
3. What makes an application evaluation balanced?
   **Answer:** It compares benefits, hazards, environmental effects and evidence.

## Lesson 11 — Polymerisation types
**Objective:** M6-CHEM-POLY-11 — Compare monomer and polymer structures to distinguish addition and condensation polymerisation.
**Starter / learning task:** Compare monomer and polymer structures to distinguish addition and condensation polymerisation. Ask learners to justify classifications and predictions using molecular structures, balanced equations or supplied evidence.

**Practice**
1. What happens in addition polymerisation?
   **Answer:** Unsaturated monomers join to form a polymer without eliminating a small molecule as the defining step.
2. What is commonly released in condensation polymerisation?
   **Answer:** A small molecule such as water or methanol may be eliminated, depending on the monomers.
3. What structural clue can indicate addition polymerisation?
   **Answer:** A carbon-carbon double bond in the monomer becomes part of the saturated polymer backbone.

## Lesson 12 — Polymer structure and properties
**Objective:** M6-CHEM-POLY-12 — Compare linear, branched and cross-linked polymer models and relate structure to material behaviour.
**Starter / learning task:** Compare linear, branched and cross-linked polymer models and relate structure to material behaviour. Ask learners to justify classifications and predictions using molecular structures, balanced equations or supplied evidence.

**Practice**
1. How can cross-linking affect a polymer?
   **Answer:** It can increase stiffness, strength or elasticity and reduce chain mobility, depending on degree and chemistry.
2. Why can branching change polymer density?
   **Answer:** Branching can reduce how closely polymer chains pack together.
3. How does polymer structure influence use?
   **Answer:** It affects properties such as flexibility, strength, thermal behaviour and chemical resistance.

## Lesson 13 — Plastics and rubber
**Objective:** M6-CHEM-POLY-13 — Classify common plastic and rubber products from labelled samples, recycling codes or supplied property data.
**Starter / learning task:** Classify common plastic and rubber products from labelled samples, recycling codes or supplied property data. Ask learners to justify classifications and predictions using molecular structures, balanced equations or supplied evidence.

**Practice**
1. What does thermoplastic mean?
   **Answer:** A polymer that softens when heated and can generally be reshaped repeatedly without major chemical change.
2. What is an elastomer?
   **Answer:** A polymer that can undergo substantial reversible deformation.
3. Why are material tests useful?
   **Answer:** They provide evidence about properties that help identify suitable applications.

## Lesson 14 — Polymer modification
**Objective:** M6-CHEM-POLY-14 — Investigate examples of polymer blending, cross-linking, additives or changes in polymerisation conditions using data.
**Starter / learning task:** Investigate examples of polymer blending, cross-linking, additives or changes in polymerisation conditions using data. Ask learners to justify classifications and predictions using molecular structures, balanced equations or supplied evidence.

**Practice**
1. How can a plasticiser change a polymer?
   **Answer:** It can increase chain mobility and flexibility.
2. What can vulcanisation do to rubber?
   **Answer:** It creates cross-links that improve elasticity, strength and resistance to deformation.
3. Why must additives be evaluated?
   **Answer:** They may change performance, safety, recyclability and environmental impact.

## Lesson 15 — Environmental impacts of polymers
**Objective:** M6-CHEM-POLY-15 — Evaluate a local polymer-waste case and propose prevention, reuse, recycling or safer design strategies.
**Starter / learning task:** Evaluate a local polymer-waste case and propose prevention, reuse, recycling or safer design strategies. Ask learners to justify classifications and predictions using molecular structures, balanced equations or supplied evidence.

**Practice**
1. Why can plastic waste persist in the environment?
   **Answer:** Many common polymers degrade slowly under environmental conditions.
2. What is one limitation of recycling?
   **Answer:** Collection, sorting, contamination and material degradation can reduce the amount that can be recycled effectively.
3. Name one way to reduce polymer waste at source.
   **Answer:** Choose reusable products and avoid unnecessary single-use packaging.
