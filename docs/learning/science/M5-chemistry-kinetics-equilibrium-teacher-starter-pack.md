## Lesson 1 — Reaction progress and measured data
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-KINETICS-01 — Plan or interpret a suitable investigation and graph the measured increase or decrease of a substance as a reaction proceeds.
**Starter / learning task:** How can reaction progress be represented with measured data? Use a worked example, teacher-provided dataset or particle model. Ask learners to state assumptions, show calculations and explain evidence.

**Practice**
1. What does reaction rate measure?
   **Answer:** The change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time.
2. What belongs on the x-axis of a reaction-progress graph?
   **Answer:** Elapsed time.
3. Why repeat measurements?
   **Answer:** To improve reliability and identify anomalous results.

## Lesson 2 — Reaction rates and concentration graphs
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-KINETICS-02 — Calculate average reaction rate from measured data and use stoichiometric relationships to graph concentration changes for substances not directly measured.
**Starter / learning task:** How can concentration data be used to calculate reaction rate? Use a worked example, teacher-provided dataset or particle model. Ask learners to state assumptions, show calculations and explain evidence.

**Practice**
1. How is average rate calculated from concentration data?
   **Answer:** Change in concentration divided by the time interval.
2. What does a steeper concentration-time slope indicate?
   **Answer:** A greater rate of concentration change.
3. How can an unmeasured product concentration be inferred?
   **Answer:** Use the balanced equation and stoichiometric ratios.

## Lesson 3 — Collision theory and activation energy
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-KINETICS-03 — Use particle-collision diagrams and energy profiles to explain how collision orientation and activation energy influence reaction rate.
**Starter / learning task:** Why do some particle collisions produce a reaction while others do not? Use a worked example, teacher-provided dataset or particle model. Ask learners to state assumptions, show calculations and explain evidence.

**Practice**
1. What is activation energy?
   **Answer:** The minimum energy barrier that reacting particles must overcome.
2. Why does collision orientation matter?
   **Answer:** Particles must collide in a suitable orientation for bonds to rearrange.
3. What is an effective collision?
   **Answer:** A collision with sufficient energy and suitable orientation that leads to reaction.

## Lesson 4 — Investigating factors that affect rate
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-KINETICS-04 — Explain evidence from controlled investigations of concentration, reactant surface area, temperature and catalysts on reaction rate.
**Starter / learning task:** How can we test which factors change reaction rate? Use a worked example, teacher-provided dataset or particle model. Ask learners to state assumptions, show calculations and explain evidence.

**Practice**
1. Name four factors that can affect reaction rate.
   **Answer:** Concentration, surface area, temperature and catalysts.
2. How should a fair test be designed?
   **Answer:** Change one independent variable while controlling other relevant variables.
3. What does a catalyst do?
   **Answer:** Provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy and is regenerated.

## Lesson 5 — Comparing rates under different conditions
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-KINETICS-05 — Compare reaction rates when concentration, surface area, temperature or catalyst conditions change, identifying controlled variables.
**Starter / learning task:** How can two rate experiments be compared fairly? Use a worked example, teacher-provided dataset or particle model. Ask learners to state assumptions, show calculations and explain evidence.

**Practice**
1. What generally happens to rate when temperature increases?
   **Answer:** Rate generally increases because a greater fraction of particles have sufficient energy.
2. Why can powdered solids react faster than large lumps?
   **Answer:** They expose greater surface area for collisions.
3. Why must other variables be controlled when comparing rates?
   **Answer:** To attribute the observed difference to the variable being tested.

## Lesson 6 — Reaction rates in everyday life and industry
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-KINETICS-06 — Explain examples of factors affecting reaction rate in everyday life and industry using particle-level reasoning.
**Starter / learning task:** Where do reaction-rate principles appear in daily life and industry? Use a worked example, teacher-provided dataset or particle model. Ask learners to state assumptions, show calculations and explain evidence.

**Practice**
1. Why is food often refrigerated?
   **Answer:** Lower temperature generally slows reactions involved in spoilage.
2. How does a catalyst help industrial production?
   **Answer:** It can increase rate without being consumed overall.
3. Why does increasing concentration often increase rate?
   **Answer:** There are more particles per unit volume and typically more frequent collisions.

## Lesson 7 — Reversible reactions and dynamic equilibrium
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-EQUIL-07 — Use evidence or models to distinguish reversible reactions from reactions that proceed effectively in one direction and explain dynamic equilibrium.
**Starter / learning task:** How can a reversible reaction be distinguished from an irreversible one? Use a worked example, teacher-provided dataset or particle model. Ask learners to state assumptions, show calculations and explain evidence.

**Practice**
1. What is a reversible reaction?
   **Answer:** A reaction that can proceed in both forward and reverse directions.
2. What is dynamic equilibrium?
   **Answer:** A closed-system condition where forward and reverse reaction rates are equal.
3. Do concentrations have to be equal at equilibrium?
   **Answer:** No; they remain constant, but need not be equal.

## Lesson 8 — Concentration and rate changes toward equilibrium
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-EQUIL-08 — Explain how reactant and product concentrations and forward and reverse rates change from reaction start until equilibrium is established.
**Starter / learning task:** How do rates and concentrations change as equilibrium is approached? Use a worked example, teacher-provided dataset or particle model. Ask learners to state assumptions, show calculations and explain evidence.

**Practice**
1. What happens to forward rate after reactants are first mixed?
   **Answer:** It is usually initially high and changes as reactant concentrations change.
2. What happens to reverse rate as products accumulate?
   **Answer:** It generally increases.
3. What defines equilibrium at the particle level?
   **Answer:** Forward and reverse rates are equal.

## Lesson 9 — Equilibrium constants
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-EQUIL-09 — Write an equilibrium-constant expression for a reaction and calculate its value from equilibrium concentrations.
**Starter / learning task:** How does the equilibrium constant describe a system at equilibrium? Use a worked example, teacher-provided dataset or particle model. Ask learners to state assumptions, show calculations and explain evidence.

**Practice**
1. What does Kc represent?
   **Answer:** The equilibrium constant expressed using equilibrium concentrations.
2. Are pure solids included in a Kc expression?
   **Answer:** No; their activity is treated as constant.
3. What does a large Kc generally indicate?
   **Answer:** Products are favoured at equilibrium for the reaction as written.

## Lesson 10 — Equilibrium concentrations
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-EQUIL-10 — Use initial conditions, stoichiometry and an equilibrium constant to determine unknown equilibrium concentrations.
**Starter / learning task:** How can unknown equilibrium concentrations be calculated? Use a worked example, teacher-provided dataset or particle model. Ask learners to state assumptions, show calculations and explain evidence.

**Practice**
1. When must concentrations be used in a Kc expression?
   **Answer:** Use equilibrium concentrations, not initial concentrations.
2. What is an ICE table used for?
   **Answer:** Organising initial, change and equilibrium concentrations.
3. What must be checked after solving for equilibrium concentration?
   **Answer:** Physical validity, units and consistency with the reaction stoichiometry.

## Lesson 11 — Equilibrium in multistep reactions
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-EQUIL-11 — Combine equilibrium relationships for linked reactions and calculate an overall equilibrium constant or unknown equilibrium concentration.
**Starter / learning task:** How are equilibrium constants combined for linked reactions? Use a worked example, teacher-provided dataset or particle model. Ask learners to state assumptions, show calculations and explain evidence.

**Practice**
1. How is the overall K for reactions added together found?
   **Answer:** Multiply the equilibrium constants for the component reactions.
2. What happens to K if a reaction is reversed?
   **Answer:** The new constant is the reciprocal.
3. What happens to K if all coefficients are multiplied by n?
   **Answer:** The new constant is K raised to the power n.

## Lesson 12 — Le Chatelier principle
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-EQUIL-12 — Identify disturbances to an equilibrium system and predict the direction of shift; distinguish effects on equilibrium position from effects on the equilibrium constant.
**Starter / learning task:** How does an equilibrium system respond when conditions change? Use a worked example, teacher-provided dataset or particle model. Ask learners to state assumptions, show calculations and explain evidence.

**Practice**
1. What does Le Chatelier principle predict?
   **Answer:** How an equilibrium responds to a disturbance by shifting to oppose that change.
2. Does a catalyst change the equilibrium constant?
   **Answer:** No; it speeds up both directions and does not change the equilibrium position.
3. What does changing temperature do to K?
   **Answer:** It changes K; the direction depends on whether the forward reaction is endothermic or exothermic.

## Lesson 13 — Equilibrium in biological and industrial systems
**Objective:** M5-CHEM-EQUIL-13 — Use examples from living systems, natural phenomena and industrial processes to explain the importance of chemical equilibrium.
**Starter / learning task:** Where does chemical equilibrium matter in living systems and industry? Use a worked example, teacher-provided dataset or particle model. Ask learners to state assumptions, show calculations and explain evidence.

**Practice**
1. Give a biological example of chemical equilibrium.
   **Answer:** Carbon dioxide and carbonic acid/bicarbonate equilibria in blood.
2. Give an industrial example.
   **Answer:** The Haber process for ammonia production.
3. Why is equilibrium useful in process design?
   **Answer:** It helps predict conditions that favour desired products and optimise yield.
